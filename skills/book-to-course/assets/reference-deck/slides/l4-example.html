Example · The loop in action

The resistant colleague

The situation

A colleague keeps resisting your project. You decide it must be about status.

What happened

You offer them public credit for the project. They're still resistant.

The lesson

Don't label them irrational. The surprise means your model of the game was incomplete.

Internomics · Module 0 — Orientation

Here's a simple example. Picture a colleague who keeps resisting a project you're leading. Every meeting they push back. They raise concerns. They slow things down. So you build a model. You decide their resistance is about status. Maybe they feel sidelined. Maybe they wanted to lead this themselves. That's a reasonable guess. It might even be right. So you act on it. You offer them public credit for the project. You make sure their name is attached to it. And then you observe. They're still resistant. Nothing changed. Now this is the moment that matters. You have two options. Option one is the one most of us take. You decide they're just difficult. Maybe irrational. You write them off. Option two is to treat the surprise as information. Your model predicted that credit would solve it. It didn't. So something about your model was wrong. That's what the lesson card means. The surprise didn't mean you failed. It means you just learned something about the game. Notice how much of this happened before a single clever sentence was involved. The problem wasn't your wording. You offered credit clearly. The problem was the read underneath. Here's the misunderstanding to watch for. People think a failed move means they need a stronger version of the same move. More credit. More praise. Louder. But if the model is wrong, a stronger move just fails harder. So pause here for a second. What would you guess the real issue might be? Hold that thought. Let's walk through how the loop plays out.
