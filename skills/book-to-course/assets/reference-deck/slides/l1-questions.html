Internomics · Module 0 — Orientation

Here's what that shift looks like in practice. Each row is a question most of us ask by habit. Next to it is the question game theory pushes us to ask instead. Instead of only asking "What should I say?" we ask "What game am I actually in?" Instead of "How do I convince them?" we ask "What do they actually value?" Instead of "Was that a good move?" we ask "Then what? What response is that move likely to create?" Instead of "Why does this problem keep happening?" we ask "What is making this pattern stable?" And sometimes instead of "How do I play this game better?" we discover the better question is "Can I change the game?" Notice what these new questions have in common. Every one of them points away from you and toward the situation. They ask about the other person's values. They ask about responses. They ask about patterns. They ask about the structure itself. That's the whole idea of this course in one table. Game theory was never created as a theory of better communication. It's the formal study of strategic interaction. But the mental models it gives us are extraordinarily useful. They force us to ask different questions. And different questions lead to different choices. You'll meet each of these questions again as we go through the modules. Here's a question for you. Which column do you live in most of the time? Be honest. Most of us live in the left one.
