One more thing about how to hold these ideas. Game theory was never created as a theory of better communication. It's the formal study of strategic interaction. And human beings are far messier than the perfectly rational players in many mathematical models. Real people get tired. They get emotional. They care about fairness when the math says they shouldn't. So I want to be upfront with you. We'll use game theory as a lens. A lens doesn't tell you what will happen. It tells you where to look. It changes the questions you ask. And it helps you notice things you'd otherwise miss. What it doesn't do is guarantee that anyone will behave a certain way. If I ever made it sound like a formula that predicts people, I'd be misleading you. So whenever real behavior departs from the clean model in this course, I'll say so. Here's why that matters. The mental models are extraordinarily useful exactly because they force us to ask better questions. They're not useful as fortune telling. The common mistake is to learn a model and then insist the other person must fit it. When they don't, people decide the other person is irrational. A better response is to say my model was incomplete. We'll come back to that idea in lesson four. For now, here's a question to consider. Where in your life do you treat your read of someone as a certainty? What would change if you treated it as one useful view among several? Now let's put all of this to work on a very ordinary situation.
