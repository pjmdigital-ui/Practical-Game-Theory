Internomics · Module 0 — Orientation

Now I don't want you walking into a conversation thinking, "Okay. Step one, identify the game. Step two, identify the payoffs." That's useful while you're learning. But the eleven models are not a procedure. They're a way of seeing. Think about an experienced driver. They aren't consciously saying check the mirror. Calculate the distance. Adjust the steering. They've developed perception. They just notice things. The same thing should happen here. At first you'll consciously ask the questions. Eventually you'll start noticing the structure almost automatically. Someone makes an aggressive demand and you think, what payoff are they protecting? A team keeps failing in the same way and you think, this is a pattern and not an incident. Someone tells you something that strongly benefits them and you think, what makes that credible? That's the goal. Not recitation. Recognition. And that's what I mean by strategic judgment. It has four parts, and they're on the screen. Knowing which model matters. Knowing when it matters. Knowing how much weight to give it. And knowing when new evidence should change your mind. You probably won't need all eleven lenses in any one situation. Usually two or three matter most. Here's the common mistake. Some people learn these models and then overanalyze everything. Most interactions don't need a strategic audit. Use as much analysis as the stakes and the uncertainty justify. You don't have to run a checklist every time someone tells you where the restroom is. So here's a question. In which part of your life do you most need this kind of judgment right now? Hold that answer. Here's the line that sums up this whole lesson.
