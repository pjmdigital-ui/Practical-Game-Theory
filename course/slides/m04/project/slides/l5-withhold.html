Secrecy is not deception

Legitimate

Withholding information you are not obligated to disclose

Not legitimate

Deliberately providing false information to induce a decision

A contract bid: You don't have to announce how you'll respond if they undercut your price.

A negotiation: You don't have to reveal your reservation price.

Internomics · Module 4 — Manage Predictability

This distinction is worth making explicit. Secrecy is not deception. You don't owe every competitor complete information about your plans. Keeping your strategy private is not necessarily deception. Here's an example. Say two companies are competing for a contract. One company doesn't have to announce "Here is exactly how we're going to respond if you undercut our price." They can keep that strategy private. Nobody would call that lying. Likewise in a negotiation you don't necessarily have to reveal your reservation price. That's the lowest number you'd accept. Remember the honest line from Lesson Three. "I'm not prepared to disclose my bottom line." So here's the line I want you to see clearly. On one side is withholding information you are not obligated to disclose. On the other side is deliberately providing false information to induce someone into a decision. The first is legitimate. The second is not. Inventing a competing offer you don't have is on the wrong side. Making up a deadline that doesn't exist is on the wrong side. Saying "I'd rather not share that" is on the right side. I'm drawing this line carefully for a reason. I don't want you to come away thinking that game theory teaches you to manipulate people. It doesn't. It teaches you to understand the strategic value of information and uncertainty. What you do with that knowledge is an ethical question. And that question is yours to answer. So think about this. Have you ever felt pressured to answer a question you didn't owe an answer to? Next let's look at when surprise actually helps.
