Internomics · Module 4 — Manage Predictability

Let's start this lesson with the case for surprise. Because there is one. This idea applies well outside formal competition. Think about a manager who always responds to every problem with the same solution. Over time people learn exactly what will happen when they bring her a problem. They can shape how they present it to get the answer they want. She becomes easy to manipulate. Think about a negotiator who always uses the same concession sequence. We saw that in Lesson Three. The other side learns it and pushes right up to the last step. That pattern can be exploited. Think about a salesperson who always handles objections the same way. Buyers who have heard the move before see it coming. He becomes predictable in a way that weakens him. In each of these cases changing the pattern can be valuable. Now I want to be really precise about why. It's not because randomness is inherently powerful. Randomness by itself does nothing for you. It's because predictability gives the other player information they can use. That's the whole reason. Change the pattern only when the pattern is handing someone information that works against you. Here's the misunderstanding to watch for. People hear this and start varying everything just to seem unpredictable. That's not the lesson. Vary the specific pattern that is being used against you. Leave the rest alone. So here's a question for you. Is there a move you make so often that people can see it coming? Next let's draw the ethical line around this kind of surprise.
