Internomics · Module 4 — Manage Predictability

Here are the last three mistakes. The fifth is becoming unpredictable in the wrong way. This is the overcorrection. Someone realizes they've been a pushover. So they swing hard the other way. They tolerate something one day and explode the next. That doesn't make you strategic. It makes you difficult to trust. Your team can't coordinate around you. Your family can't anticipate you. Where you need a consequence to be real, the answer is to be deliberately consistent. The sixth is answering every question about your limits. Many of us were raised to think that answering every question is the polite thing to do. But giving away your bottom line tells the other side exactly how far they need to push. You can decline honestly. Protecting strategically valuable information isn't deception. It's just good sense. The seventh is romanticizing surprise. Surprise is useful only when the uncertainty it creates is valuable. A spouse gains nothing from wondering whether you'll keep your word. A customer gains nothing from wondering whether your product will work. An employee gains nothing from wondering whether their vacation will be approved. In all of those cases predictability is the gift. Now notice something about all seven mistakes. Each one comes from following a rule without looking at the game. "Always be flexible." "Always be open." "Always keep them guessing." The fix is the same every time. Before you choose, ask what your predictability enables the other player to do. That one question protects you from every mistake on these two slides. Now let's take this into your week.
