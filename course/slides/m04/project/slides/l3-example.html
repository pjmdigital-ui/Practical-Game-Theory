Example · The concession pattern

$100 → $90 → $80

The situation

A negotiator always makes three concessions. First $100. Then $90. Then $80.

What happened

The other side learns the pattern. They push. You move. They push again. You move again.

The lesson

They never needed your true minimum. Only that you'll move again.

Your pattern told them: there's more room.

Internomics · Module 4 — Manage Predictability

Let's make the tax concrete with a negotiation. Imagine a negotiator who always makes three concessions. First a hundred dollars. Then ninety. Then eighty. It's a habit. Maybe it even feels like good manners. The other side watches this happen once or twice. Then they learn the pattern. Here's the important part. They don't need to know your true minimum. They don't need to read your mind or see your numbers. They simply need to know one thing. You'll move again. So instead of accepting your first offer, they push. You move. They push again. You move again. By the third round your pattern has effectively revealed information about your limits. You never said a word about your floor. Your own behavior told them anyway. It said there's more room. That's the predictability tax in action. The cost isn't in any single concession. Each one might be perfectly reasonable on its own. The cost is in what the sequence teaches. It teaches the other side that pushing pays. Here's a question for you. Do you have a concession pattern? Think about how you handle a pushback on price or on a deadline or on your time. Do you always give a little? Do you always give it at the same moment? If someone has watched you three times, they may know that pattern better than you do. Let's put this lesson into one line.
