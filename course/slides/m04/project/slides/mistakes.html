Internomics · Module 4 — Manage Predictability

Let's look at the mistakes I see most often. There are seven of them. Here are the first four. The first is treating "keep them guessing" as the lesson. Game theory doesn't say that. Where trust matters, excessive unpredictability is destructive. Think of the spouse who never knows whether you'll keep a promise. So before you reach for surprise, ask what your predictability enables the other player to do. The second is confusing variety with unpredictability. People think doing different things makes them hard to read. But a rigid sequence of different moves is still a pattern. Rock then paper then scissors every time is just as exploitable as rock every time. If the other player can learn it, they can use it. The third is stating a boundary but never enforcing it. This one is everywhere. You say "this is the last time." Then it isn't. A boundary that's predictable but unenforced teaches the opposite of what you said. Remember that your behavior creates more information than your words. Dana's "I won't keep doing this" is the textbook case. The fourth is letting secrecy slide into deception. You don't have to answer every question about your limits. Declining honestly is fine. Saying "I'm not prepared to disclose my bottom line" is fine. But deliberately providing false information to push someone into a decision is not fine. That's a line we don't cross. Here's a question for you. Which of these four have you made in the last month? Most of us can find at least one. Let's look at the other three.
