Internomics · Module 4 — Manage Predictability

We have two research spotlights this time. They point in opposite directions. That's the point. The first comes from soccer. Chiappori, Levitt and Groseclose published a study in 2002. They analyzed 459 penalty kicks from the top French and Italian professional leagues. Why penalty kicks? Because they're a clean strategic setting. The kicker and the goalkeeper have opposing goals. Their tendencies are known. The stakes are high. The researchers found that the testable predictions of mixed-strategy theory fit the data surprisingly well. A later analysis used a different statistical method. It reached broadly consistent results. So what does that suggest for us? When another person's gain depends on correctly anticipating you, your predictability changes the payoff of your action. A kick to the left is worth less if the goalkeeper expects it. And a concession is worth less if the other side knows it's coming. That's the predictability tax with real data behind it. Now here's what it does not prove. Professional soccer is close to a pure zero-sum contest. One side's gain is the other side's loss. It doesn't show that every conversation works like a penalty kick. It doesn't show that unpredictable people win. And it doesn't tell you to randomize how you communicate. It also doesn't show that people perfectly randomize. Related research on baseball pitches by Bhattacharya and Howard found that professionals only approximate the mixed-strategy benchmark. They get closer as the stakes rise. So the honest summary is this. In truly competitive moments a readable pattern costs you. Now let's look at a setting where a readable pattern helps you.
