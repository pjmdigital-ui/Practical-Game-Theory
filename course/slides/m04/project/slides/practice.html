Dana and the Friday revisions

The pattern: For two years Dana has stayed late whenever account manager Rob brings an "urgent" Friday revision.

The drift: The Friday requests have multiplied. Several sat in his inbox since Tuesday.

Her words: She told Rob she "wouldn't keep doing this." Then she stayed late anyway.

Another pattern: Every Thursday morning she reviews the juniors' work. They plan their weeks around it.

Her plan: Become "harder to read" across the board.

Internomics · Module 4 — Manage Predictability

Let's practice. Here's the scenario. Dana is a senior designer at a marketing agency. For two years the account manager Rob has brought her "urgent" client revisions on Friday afternoon. And every time she stays late and finishes them. Lately the Friday requests have multiplied. Several of them had been sitting in his inbox since Tuesday. That detail matters. Hold on to it. Dana has complained in meetings that people need to plan better. She once told Rob directly that she wouldn't keep doing this. Then she stayed late anyway. There's a second pattern in Dana's week too. She reviews the junior designers' work every Thursday morning. The juniors have built their weeks around it. They know when feedback is coming. They plan their drafts to land before it. Now Dana is frustrated. She feels taken for granted. And she's thinking about a fix. She wants to become harder to read across the board so nobody takes her for granted again. Before we get to the questions, take a moment with the story. Notice how many of the ideas from this module are sitting in it. There's an unintended rule. There's a predictability tax. There's a stated boundary that nobody believes. There's a stoplight kind of pattern. And there's a plan that might be the wrong kind of unpredictable. Here's the trap to watch for as you think it through. Dana's frustration is completely fair. That doesn't make her plan a good one. Keep those two things separate. Let's look at the questions.
