Definition · Mixed strategy

A player chooses among actions by probabilities, rather than the same action every time.

Internomics · Module 4 — Manage Predictability

Game theory has long been interested in situations where players can't predict exactly what another player will do. In formal game theory, this leads to what's called a mixed strategy: a player chooses among actions according to probabilities, rather than choosing the same action every time. The classic example is rock-paper-scissors. If you always throw rock, your opponent eventually learns to throw paper. If you always throw paper, they learn to throw scissors. If they know your pattern, your strategy becomes exploitable. And here's the important part: they don't need to know your exact next move. They only need to know enough about your tendency to adjust their own behavior. The solution isn't to find the one action that's always best. It's to keep the opponent from knowing which one you'll choose.
