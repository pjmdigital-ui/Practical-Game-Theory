Internomics · Module 4 — Manage Predictability

Game theory has long been interested in situations where players can't predict exactly what another player will do. In formal game theory this leads to something called a mixed strategy. A player chooses among actions according to probabilities. They don't choose the same action every time. The classic example is rock-paper-scissors. If you always throw rock, your opponent eventually learns to throw paper. If you always throw paper, they learn to throw scissors. If they know your pattern, your strategy becomes exploitable. Now here's the part people tend to miss. Your opponent doesn't need to know your exact next move. They only need to know enough about your tendency to adjust their own behavior. A lean in one direction is enough for them. So what's the solution? It isn't to find the one action that's always best. In rock-paper-scissors there is no such action. Every throw loses to something. The solution is to keep the opponent from knowing which action you're going to choose. That's why this idea matters for us. The danger isn't only in being totally predictable. It's in having a tilt that someone else can see. Think about it this way. Where in your life do you have a tilt? Maybe you usually give in. Maybe you usually go quiet. The other person doesn't need certainty to use that. Next let's clear up the most common misreading of this idea.
