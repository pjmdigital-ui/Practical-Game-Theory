Example · Two managers

Same trait, opposite effect

The situation

Manager A says: "I really don't like firing people."

Manager B is known for one thing. Break this rule and there will be consequences.

What happened

A's team learns he never fires anyone. Some take risks they wouldn't otherwise take.

B's team knows the rule is real. Cooperation improves.

The lesson

Both are perfectly predictable. Only one is exploitable.

The difference is what each pattern lets others do.

Internomics · Module 4 — Manage Predictability

Here's a story about two managers. The first one says "I really don't like firing people." That's a personal preference. It's a pretty human one too. Most of us don't enjoy that part of the job. But watch what happens over time. Employees notice. They learn "He never actually fires anyone." Now the statement has become part of the strategic environment. It's no longer just a feeling he has. It's a fact his team can plan around. Some employees may begin taking risks they wouldn't otherwise take. They cut a corner. They miss a deadline. They push a limit. Why not? The worst case is a conversation. His predictability has changed their incentives. Now picture the opposite manager. Her employees know one thing for sure. "If you violate this rule, there will be consequences." That predictability can actually improve cooperation. People don't waste time testing her. They know where the line is. They work inside it. Notice what's going on here. Both managers are perfectly predictable. The trait is exactly the same. The strategic effect is opposite. The difference is what each pattern allows the other players to do. And here's the subtle part. You may think you're simply behaving consistently. Other people may experience that consistency as a rule. Neither manager announced a policy. Their teams read one anyway. So here's a question for you. If you manage or lead anyone, which manager would they say you are? Let's close the lesson with the one line to remember.
