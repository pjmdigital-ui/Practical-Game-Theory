Internomics · Module 4 — Manage Predictability

Remember the Prisoner's Dilemma from the last module? Two players may each have an incentive to defect. They do it even though mutual cooperation would be better for both of them. That's the trap of the one-shot game. Now imagine they play the game repeatedly. Suddenly today's decision affects tomorrow's. If I cooperate today, perhaps you're more likely to cooperate tomorrow. If I defect, perhaps you punish me next time. And now predictability gets complicated in a really interesting way. Suppose you know that I will always retaliate against defection. My response is completely predictable. By everything we said in the first half of this module, that sounds like a weakness. But it isn't one here. That predictability may actually support cooperation. You know exactly what defecting will cost you. So you don't do it. And neither do I. This is an important paradox. The same predictability that makes you exploitable in one game can make you trustworthy in another. In rock-paper-scissors a known pattern gets you beaten. In a repeated relationship a known response can keep everyone honest. That's why we can't turn any strategic idea into a universal rule. Not "always be unpredictable." Not "always be consistent." We have to understand the structure of the game first. Here's the misunderstanding to watch for. People love a simple rule they can apply everywhere. This module is a warning against that habit. The right move depends on the game. So ask yourself this. Which of your relationships are repeated games? In which ones would a known response actually protect you?
