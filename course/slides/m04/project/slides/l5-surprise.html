Internomics · Module 4 — Manage Predictability

Now the balance. There's a tendency in strategic thinking to romanticize surprise. Movies love it. The genius who nobody can read. That's a mistake. Surprise is useful only when the uncertainty it creates is valuable. Look at the three cards on the slide. If your employee doesn't know whether you'll approve their vacation request, that's not necessarily strategic. It's just stressful for them. If your spouse doesn't know whether you'll keep your commitment, that's not strategic. That's a relationship problem. If your customer doesn't know whether your product will actually work, that's not strategic either. That's a business problem. In all of those situations predictability creates value. So here's the question I want you to ask before you decide anything. Who benefits from knowing what I'm going to do? Answer it honestly. If the answer is "both of us," predictability probably helps. Keep the pattern. Make it even clearer. If the answer is "they can use that information to consistently take advantage of me," then perhaps some uncertainty is valuable. Notice how much work that one question does. It sorts the stoplight from the poker table in a few seconds. It keeps you from protecting information that should be shared. And it keeps you from sharing information that should be protected. Here's the misunderstanding to watch for. Some people answer "they benefit" and stop there. But someone benefiting isn't the same as someone exploiting. Your team benefits from knowing your schedule. That's good. The question is whether they use it against you.
