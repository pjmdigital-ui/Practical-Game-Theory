Internomics · Module 5 — See the Equilibrium

Here's how I'd read it. Start with the loop. Priya flags risks. Marcus goes quiet. Priya escalates with longer emails and the cc. Marcus feels attacked and shares less. Priya sees even less and escalates further. It's "I push and you resist" at work. Now why does each response make sense? Given Marcus's silence, escalating is Priya's way to make sure risks get handled. It's also how she makes sure she isn't blamed. Given Priya's escalation, guarding information is Marcus's way to protect his reputation. Neither of them is irrational. Both want a partnership. The pattern persists anyway. Stable doesn't mean good. Why did "just change" backfire? Marcus changed while everyone else kept their old expectations. So his silence read as disengagement. He paid the price for moving alone. That's the heart of equilibrium. If I change while you don't, I'm worse off. How should you label it honestly? There are no formal strategies or payoffs here. So it isn't a proven Nash equilibrium. It resembles an equilibrium trap. And the equilibrium check supports that. Changing alone really did leave Marcus worse off. Finally, what would have to change? Look at the last row. A 15-minute joint risk review replaces the one-way email. That changes the sequence and the information. Marcus's handling becomes visible. Priya no longer needs to escalate. An agreement that neither copies the director on the other's area without talking first is a commitment. Briefing the director together uses the third party. Now each person expects the other to cooperate. Neither carries the whole risk of going first. Notice what doesn't work. "Marcus should communicate more" changes nothing about the structure.
