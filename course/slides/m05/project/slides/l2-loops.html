Why equilibrium matters in communication

Three loops worth recognizing

Pursue

You pursue → they withdraw → you pursue harder → they withdraw further

Criticize

They criticize → you defend → they read your defense as proof → you defend harder

Concede

You ask for a concession → they make one → you ask again → they resist → you push harder

Each move makes sense locally. The overall pattern is terrible.

Internomics · Module 5 — See the Equilibrium

Let's bring this closer to everyday conversation. Here are three loops from the book. I'd bet you've lived inside at least one of them. The first is pursue and withdraw. You pursue. They withdraw. So you pursue harder. And they withdraw further. Each step makes the next one feel necessary. The second is criticize and defend. They criticize. You defend. They take your defense as proof that they were right. So you defend harder. Notice how the defense becomes the fuel. And it feels like the reasonable move. The third is the concession loop. You ask for a concession. They make one. You ask for another. They start to resist. You read the resistance as a lack of cooperation. So you push harder. Here's the insight. Look at any single step in any of these loops. It makes sense. If someone pulls away, of course you reach for them. If someone attacks you, of course you defend yourself. The individual moves make sense when you view them locally. But the overall pattern is terrible. That's the gap between a move and a system. The common mistake is trying to win one step of the loop. You build a better defense. You find a sharper argument. You make a stronger case for more. But winning a step usually just feeds the next round. So here's a question for you. Which of these three loops do you fall into most often? And which role do you usually play in it? Now let's look at a full example and see how naming the pattern changes everything.
