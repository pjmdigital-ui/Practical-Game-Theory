A more sophisticated view of conflict

You can be rational and still produce a bad outcome

The usual story

"Someone must be acting irrationally." Sometimes they are.

What game theory adds

Bad collective outcomes can emerge from individually rational responses.

The better question

Not only "Who's behaving badly?" Also "What interaction is producing this pattern?"

Internomics · Module 5 — See the Equilibrium

This is one of the most important ideas in the entire book. You can be rational and still produce a bad outcome. Look at the first card. When something goes wrong between people, we usually explain it one way. Someone must be acting irrationally. Someone must be the problem. And sometimes that's true. Some people really do behave badly. But game theory shows us something else. Look at the middle card. Bad collective outcomes can emerge from individually rational responses. That's exactly what makes the Prisoner's Dilemma so important. Each player's behavior is understandable. It fits the incentives. It fits what the other player is doing. And yet the combined result is worse than another outcome they could have reached. Nobody had to be foolish. Nobody had to be cruel. This gives us a more sophisticated way to think about conflict. Look at the third card. Instead of asking only who's behaving badly, we can ask what interaction is producing this pattern. That's a much more interesting question. It's also a much more useful one. When you only hunt for the villain, the best you can do is win an argument about blame. When you look at the interaction, you can start to see what keeps it going. Now here's the misunderstanding to watch for. This idea doesn't erase responsibility. People still own their choices. It just adds a second question about the structure they're choosing inside. So think about a conflict you've been in lately. Was anyone truly irrational? Or was each of you making sense from where you stood? Let's test this with three quick questions.
