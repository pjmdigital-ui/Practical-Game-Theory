Definition · Nash equilibrium

Given what everyone else does, no one gains by changing alone.

The formal version

A combination of strategies. Each player's strategy is a best response to the strategies of the others.

The test

Hold everyone else still. Would this one person gain by changing alone? If no one would, the pattern holds.

Why it matters: it explains why people keep doing things they don't like.

Internomics · Module 5 — See the Equilibrium

Let's start with the definition. In game theory a Nash equilibrium is a combination of strategies. In it each player's strategy is a best response to the strategies of the others. That's the formal version on the left. Here's the simpler version in the headline. Given what everyone else is doing, no individual player has a reason to change their strategy on their own. The card on the right gives you a test you can run in your head. Hold everyone else still. Then look at one person and ask whether they would gain by changing alone. If nobody would gain by moving alone, the pattern holds. That's an equilibrium. Notice the word "alone" in the headline. That's the whole key. Equilibrium is not about whether a group could do better together. It's about whether any one person can do better by switching while everyone else stays the same. So why does this matter for you? Because it explains something that puzzles a lot of us. People keep doing things they don't like. They complain about it. They say they want something different. And yet they keep doing it. That isn't always weakness or stupidity. Very often it's because changing alone doesn't look attractive from where they stand. Here's a question for you. Think of a habit in your team or your family that everyone grumbles about. Would anyone gain by being the only one to stop? If the answer is no, you're probably looking at an equilibrium.
