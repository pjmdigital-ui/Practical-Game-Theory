Example · The dilemma in three questions

Where do they end up?

1. Which outcome would both prefer?

Both cooperate. Nobody argues with that.

2. If the other's choice is fixed, what should each do?

Defect. If they cooperate, defect. If they defect, defect.

3. So where do they end up?

Both defect. Stable. And both would rather be somewhere else.

The lesson: everyone doing what makes sense locally can produce an outcome everyone dislikes globally.

Internomics · Module 5 — See the Equilibrium

Let's walk the Prisoner's Dilemma in three questions. I want you to answer each one in your head before I do. First question. Which outcome would both players prefer? Both cooperate. Nobody argues with that. Each of them would rather be in that box than in the box where both defect. Second question. What should each player do if the other player's choice is fixed? Treat the other person as already decided. If they cooperate, I do better by defecting. If they defect, I do better by defecting too. So the answer is defect. And the other player runs the very same logic. Third question. So where do they end up? Both defect. Now look at what we've got. Neither player would change on their own. That makes it an equilibrium. And both players would rather be somewhere else. That makes it a bad one. Here's the realization I want you to land on. We usually explain bad outcomes by saying someone must be acting irrationally. Here nobody is. Each player is doing what makes sense from where they stand. And together they produce an outcome everyone dislikes. That's the lesson at the bottom of the slide. Everyone doing what makes sense locally can produce an outcome everyone dislikes globally. Hold on to those three questions. You can run them on almost any stuck situation. What would we both prefer? What does each of us do given the other's move? Where does that leave us? Try it on a standoff you're in right now. Here's the one sentence to remember from this lesson.
