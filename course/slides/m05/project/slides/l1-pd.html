Internomics · Module 5 — See the Equilibrium

The classic example of a stable but bad outcome is the Prisoner's Dilemma. We've met it before. Let's revisit it with fresh eyes. Two people each make a choice. They can cooperate or they can defect. Look at the four cards. If both cooperate, they get a relatively good outcome. If one defects while the other cooperates, the defector does especially well. If both defect, they both do worse than they would have under mutual cooperation. Now put yourself in one player's shoes. You think it through. If the other person cooperates, I'd be better off defecting. If the other person defects, I'd better defect too. So no matter what they do, defection looks like my best response. And here's the catch. The other player reasons exactly the same way. So both defect. And now you have an equilibrium. Neither player gains by switching alone. But it's a lousy equilibrium. Both of them would prefer the top left card. Both would rather cooperate. Here's the part I really want you to sit with. The problem isn't that they don't understand cooperation would be better. They do understand it. The problem is the structure of the game. Each player's individual best response pushes them toward defection. That's one of the deepest lessons game theory has to offer. A lot of people hear this and think the players just need more information or better values. Sometimes that helps. But the trap is in the structure. So ask yourself where you've seen two smart people both choose the safe move and both end up worse off. Next, let's look at what this says about rationality.
