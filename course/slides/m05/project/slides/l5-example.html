Internomics · Module 5 — See the Equilibrium

Thomas Schelling famously illustrated coordination with a simple thought experiment. I love this one. Look at the first card. Imagine you've agreed to meet someone in New York City tomorrow. You've agreed on the day. But you haven't agreed on the location. And you haven't agreed on the time. You can't call them. So where do you go? Take a second and actually answer that for yourself. Now look at the middle card. A famous result of Schelling's experiments was that people frequently converged on noon at Grand Central Station. Think about how remarkable that is. There are countless places to meet in New York. There are twenty-four hours in a day. And yet a lot of people landed on the same place at the same time. Now the third card. Nothing about those choices was mathematically unique. Noon isn't better than eleven. Grand Central isn't the only station. They simply stood out. The place and the time worked as a focal point. A focal point is something that stands out and helps people line up their expectations. Here's why this is so fascinating. It shows that people don't always need to communicate explicitly to coordinate. They can use context. They can use conventions. They can use salience and shared expectations. That's the line at the bottom of the slide. And it connects to everything we just said about multiple equilibria. When several patterns are possible, a focal point can help people find the same one.
