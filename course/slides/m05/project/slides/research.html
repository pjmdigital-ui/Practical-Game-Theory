Research spotlight · Charness, Rigotti & Rustichini

Change the payoffs, and behavior changes

What it shows

In one-shot Prisoner's Dilemmas with 348 participants, cooperation ranged from 23% to 60% depending on the payoff for mutual cooperation. Beliefs about others shifted too.

What it doesn't prove

That people always cooperate, that your relationship is a Prisoner's Dilemma, or that Nash equilibrium is either a reliable predictor or useless.

Internomics · Module 5 — See the Equilibrium

Let's look at some research. Charness, Rigotti and Rustichini ran clean, one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma experiments with 348 participants, and they varied how much players earned from mutual cooperation. Cooperation ranged from 23 percent to 60 percent depending on that payoff, and participants' beliefs about whether others would cooperate shifted as the structure changed. Two lessons. First, structure matters: change the payoffs and you change behavior, which is exactly why you shift a pattern by changing what makes it stable. Second, the simple money-only prediction, "everyone defects," didn't hold. Beliefs, norms, social preferences and errors matter too. What it doesn't prove: that people always cooperate, that any particular relationship is a Prisoner's Dilemma, or that Nash equilibrium is either a reliable predictor or useless. It's a useful structure to think with, and an incomplete description of real people.
