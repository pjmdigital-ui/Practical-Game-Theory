Research spotlight · Charness, Rigotti & Rustichini

Change the payoffs and behavior changes

What it shows

One-shot Prisoner's Dilemmas with 348 participants. Cooperation ranged from 23% to 60% depending on the payoff for mutual cooperation. Beliefs about others shifted too.

What it doesn't prove

Not that people always cooperate. Not that your relationship is a Prisoner's Dilemma. Not that Nash is either a reliable predictor or useless.

What it suggests: structure matters. But the money-only prediction ("everyone defects") didn't hold. Beliefs and norms matter too.

Internomics · Module 5 — See the Equilibrium

Let's look at some research. This study comes from Charness, Rigotti and Rustichini. They ran clean one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma experiments with 348 participants. One-shot means each pair played once. There was no future relationship to protect. And they varied one thing. They changed how much players earned from mutual cooperation. Look at the left card. Cooperation ranged from 23 percent to 60 percent depending on that payoff. And participants' beliefs about whether others would cooperate shifted as the structure changed. So what does that suggest for real conversations? Two lessons. Look at the line at the bottom. First, structure matters. Change the payoffs and you change behavior. That's exactly why you shift a pattern by changing what makes it stable. It's the levers idea from Lesson 4 in a lab. Second, the simple money-only prediction didn't hold. That prediction says everyone defects. But many people cooperated anyway. Beliefs matter. Norms matter. Social preferences and errors matter too. Now look at the right card. Here's what this study does not prove. It doesn't show that people always cooperate. It doesn't show that any particular relationship is a Prisoner's Dilemma. And it doesn't show that Nash equilibrium is either a reliable predictor or useless. The honest reading is in between. It's a useful structure to think with. And it's an incomplete description of real people. Here's the question I'd ask you. If one change in payoff can move cooperation that much, what's one payoff in your stuck situation that you could change? Now a second line of research on repeated games.
