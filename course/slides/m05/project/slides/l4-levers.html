Internomics · Module 5 — See the Equilibrium

So how do you break an equilibrium? This is where the concept becomes practically useful. Here's the principle. If a pattern is self-reinforcing, simply changing your intention may not be enough. Wanting it to be different won't move it. Trying harder won't move it. You have to change something that affects the strategic structure. Look at the eight boxes. These are your levers. Maybe your response changes. Maybe the sequence changes. That means who moves first and when. Maybe the incentives change. Maybe new information appears. Maybe someone makes a commitment. Maybe a third party gets involved. Maybe expectations change. That's the big one. Or maybe the available choices change. Let me give you a quick way to think about each one. Ask what is holding the pattern in place right now. If it's fear of going first, look at sequence or commitment. If it's a misread, look at information. If it's what each person predicts the other will do, look at expectations. Remember the two-hour meeting from the last lesson? Nobody wanted to be the one to leave early. Good intentions didn't touch that. A lever might. The common misunderstanding here is thinking that a lever is a trick. It isn't. These are honest changes to the game you're both playing. And the best ones usually make a better pattern attractive to both people. Not just to you. So look at the list and ask yourself which lever you've never tried. Most of us only ever pull the first one. We change our own response and hope. Now notice something about this list. Many of these levers will become later modules.
