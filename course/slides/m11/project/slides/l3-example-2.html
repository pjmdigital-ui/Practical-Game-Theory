Example · The client and the scope

"Will Paul do it this time?"

The situation

A client keeps asking for work outside the agreed scope. Every request turns into a debate.

What happened

A clearly defined scope. A change-request process. A fee for additional work.

The lesson

Less ambiguity isn't less generosity. It protects the relationship from resentment.

Internomics · Module 11 — Change the Game

Here's one from my own work. A client repeatedly asks for work outside the agreed scope. I keep debating each request. Every interaction turns into the same question in the client's head. Will Paul do it this time? Think about what that game rewards. If I say yes sometimes and no other times, the client learns that asking always pays. Every request is a fresh lottery ticket. And every request is a small negotiation that wears on both of us. So I change the game. I introduce a clearly defined scope. I add a change-request process. And I attach a fee for additional work. That's a different game. Notice which levers moved. The rules are clearer. The payoffs changed because extra work now has a price. And the options changed because there's now a proper way to ask for more. Here's the part people miss. I haven't become less generous. I've reduced the ambiguity about what happens when a request comes in. And that can actually protect the relationship. Without a structure, resentment builds quietly. I start to feel taken advantage of. The client starts to feel I'm being stingy. Neither of us ever says it out loud. The structure stops that before it starts. Here's the misunderstanding to watch for. Some people feel that rules and fees are cold. They worry the structure will hurt the relationship. Often it's the lack of structure that hurts it. So ask yourself this. Who in your work keeps asking the same question every week? Could one clear rule answer it for good? Now for the trickiest case of all. The game where you're both the designer and the player.
