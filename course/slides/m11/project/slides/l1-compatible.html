Internomics · Module 11 — Change the Game

What the cake rule achieves has a name. It's called incentive compatibility. Hurwicz's work made it one of the foundational ideas of mechanism design. Here's what it means at a high level. An incentive-compatible mechanism is designed so that following the desired strategy lines up with each participant's own incentives. People don't have to fight their self-interest to do the right thing. Look at the two boxes. The ideal isn't to force everyone to fight their incentives. That's the left box. It's the approach of the lecture and the warning and the motivational poster. It can work for a while. But it asks people to swim against the current every day. The ideal is the right box. Where possible, make the desirable action compatible with their incentives. That's exactly what happened in the cake example. The child cutting the cake doesn't have to become indifferent to getting the bigger piece. Nobody asked for a saint. The mechanism uses that preference. Because the other child chooses first, the cutter's self-interest creates an incentive for fairness. That is elegant game design. Now a common misunderstanding. People sometimes hear this and think it means bribing people to behave. It doesn't. The cake rule doesn't pay anyone anything. It only changes the order of moves. Incentive compatibility is about structure. Money is just one lever among many. So here's a question to try. Think of a rule you enforce by constant reminders. Could you rearrange the game so the reminders become unnecessary? That brings us to the takeaway for this lesson.
