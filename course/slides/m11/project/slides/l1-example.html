Example · The cake

One cuts, the other chooses

The situation

Two children. One cake. Each wants the larger piece. Even a perfect split by the parent draws complaints.

What happened

The parent changes the rule. One child cuts. The other chooses first. The cutter makes the pieces as equal as possible.

The lesson

No new personalities. No lecture on fairness. The rules made self-interest produce the fair outcome.

Internomics · Module 11 — Change the Game

Here's a simple example often used to illustrate mechanism design. Two children need to divide a cake. Each one wants the larger piece. A parent could try to divide it perfectly. And both children may still argue that the other piece is bigger. Now the parent is a referee in an endless dispute. Every split is a new chance to complain. Or the parent can change the mechanism. One child cuts. The other child chooses first. Watch what happens to the child holding the knife. Suddenly that child has every reason to make the pieces as equal as possible. Why? Because if one piece is clearly larger, the other child gets to take it. The cutter's best move is now a fair cut. Notice what the parent didn't do. The parent didn't change the children's personalities. The parent didn't give a lecture on fairness. The parent didn't try to make them less selfish. Both children still want the bigger piece. The parent changed the rules so the children's own incentives produced the fair outcome. That's the essence of this whole module. Here's a question for you. Where in your life are you playing the parent who keeps cutting the cake and refereeing the complaints? Maybe it's splitting chores. Maybe it's dividing work on a team. Maybe it's handing out credit. Is there a version of "one cuts and the other chooses" that would let people's own interests do the work for you? There's a name for what the cake rule achieves. Let's look at it.
