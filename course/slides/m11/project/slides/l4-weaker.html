The answer isn't always "Buyer, become smarter."

Internomics · Module 11 — Change the Game

Mechanism design matters especially when players are unequal. They may have unequal information. Unequal expertise. Unequal bargaining power. Here's the point. When one side holds most of the cards, the fix doesn't have to come from the weaker side alone. Sometimes the rules themselves should protect the weaker player. Remember the used-car problem from earlier in the course. A seller knows far more about the car than the buyer does. The buyer can't see the hidden problems. So the buyer is in a weak spot no matter how smart they are. Now look at the three cards. A rule requiring disclosure changes the game. So do inspection rights. So do warranties. Each one moves information or risk back toward the side that lacked it. None of them requires the buyer to become an expert mechanic. The solution to information asymmetry doesn't always have to be telling the buyer to become smarter. Sometimes institutions can make the game safer. That's one of the reasons we create rules in the first place. This connects to your own life more than you might think. You may be the stronger player in some games. The manager with the information. The expert with the client. The parent with the child. Here's the question for you. In which of your games are you the one holding most of the cards? What rule would you want in place if you were on the other side? Here's the misunderstanding to watch for. Protecting the weaker player isn't charity. It isn't about punishing the stronger player either. It's about making the game safer to play. Now let's look at a real system that was redesigned for exactly this reason.
