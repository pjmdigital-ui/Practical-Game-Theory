Internomics · Module 11 — Change the Game

Changing an incentive can change behavior a lot. We'll look at a striking study of tax inspectors in Pakistan in the research spotlight. The structure changed there and behavior changed with it. But here's the warning. Designing incentives is dangerous precisely because incentives work. Let me show you how it goes wrong with my own team as the example. Imagine I tell my team I want higher-quality leads. That's the first card. Then I pay them based entirely on the number of leads generated. That's the second card. What will tend to increase? Probably the number of leads. Quality may fall. Then I accuse the team of missing the point. But they didn't miss the point. The scoreboard told them what the point was. I said one thing and paid for another. They listened to the pay. This is why metrics are dangerous. Once a measurement is tied to money, people begin optimizing against it. The same happens when it's tied to status. Or promotion. Or punishment. Or reputation. And once that happens the measurement may stop being a good picture of the thing you originally cared about. Here's the misunderstanding to avoid. This doesn't mean your team is cynical or dishonest. They're doing what any smart player does. They read the rules and they play to win. The problem is the gap between what you want and what you score. So ask yourself this. What scoreboard have you set up for other people? Does it point at the thing you really want?
