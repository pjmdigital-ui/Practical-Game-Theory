Internomics · Module 11 — Change the Game

Here's one strong answer. Start with question one. The game rewards closing lots of tickets quickly. It also rewards not helping others. Mentoring or untangling a hard ticket lowers your count. And your coworkers are your rivals on the leaderboard. So the game punishes complex work. It punishes mentoring. It punishes thorough fixes. The workshops are speeches. The leaderboard is the game. Question two. The metric is tickets closed. The goal is solved problems and a stronger team. Because reopened tickets count as new, a shallow fix earns credit twice. Ticket-splitting is people playing the metric. It's also a multitask problem. Mentoring and durable fixes are hard to measure. So effort drains away from them. Question three is the levers. Payoffs could move to a team-level reward. Or to one weighted toward problems that stay resolved. A rule could count a reopened ticket against the original resolution. Observability could improve by publishing reopen rates. Who decides could change too. Let senior engineers escalate within set limits so tickets don't wait on Dana's calendar. Dana could even remove the public leaderboard and restore discretion. Question four is the stress test. Reward low reopen rates and engineers might discourage customers from reopening. Reward a team target and some may free ride. Reward satisfaction scores and people might survey only happy customers. That's stress-testing. It isn't an accusation. Question five. Firing people fixes the player before inspecting the game. Accountability can still matter. But a new hire faces the same payoffs. Dana should name the mechanism and make the smallest testable change. For example, stop counting reopens as new tickets for one quarter. She should explain it openly to the team.
