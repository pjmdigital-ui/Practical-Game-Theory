Internomics · Module 11 — Change the Game

The second family of problems comes from the work of Bengt Holmström and Paul Milgrom. They called it multitask incentives. Here's the point. Most jobs don't contain one output. They contain many. And some of those outputs are much easier to measure than others. Take a teacher. A teacher might need to teach knowledge. Develop reasoning. Manage behavior. Encourage curiosity. Provide feedback. Mentor students. Every one of those tasks matters. But only some of them show up cleanly on a test. Now imagine you strongly reward only the easiest one to measure. I've highlighted it in dark blue. Effort can shift away from the harder-to-measure tasks. Not because the teacher stopped caring. Because the game now pays for one box and ignores the other five. Holmström and Milgrom's analysis formalized exactly this. Strong incentives on one measurable activity can pull effort away from other valuable activities that are poorly measured. If the reward is tied mainly to test results, you may get more effort toward the test. That may or may not equal better education. The designer has to understand the difference. We'll see a real case of this in Kenya in the research spotlight. Here's the misunderstanding to watch for. People assume a strong incentive just adds effort on top. Often it moves effort instead. It takes time from somewhere else. Think about your own job for a second. Which of your tasks gets counted? Which ones never show up on any scoreboard? And which ones have you quietly started to neglect? That leads to the most important question in this lesson.
