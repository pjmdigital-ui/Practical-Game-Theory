Design for the behavior people are likely to have

For saints

"Everyone will behave perfectly. We don't need safeguards."

For monsters

"Everyone is trying to cheat us. Build a prison."

For people

Self-interest and morality. Loyalty and ambition. Error and creativity. All at once.

A robust design doesn't assume everyone is selfish. It also doesn't require everyone to be a saint.

Internomics · Module 11 — Change the Game

Here's a balance to hold. Don't design for the behavior you wish people had. Design for the behavior they're likely to have. Let me give you an example. Suppose you write an expense policy that relies entirely on the idea that everyone will behave reasonably. Maybe they will. Most people probably will. But if abusing the policy brings large benefits with no meaningful cost, eventually somebody may exploit it. Now everyone else watches. If nothing happens, the behavior can spread. The policy didn't fail because people were bad. It failed because it only worked if everyone was good. Look at the three cards. One extreme designs for saints. Everyone will behave perfectly so we don't need safeguards. The other extreme designs for monsters. Everyone is trying to cheat us so build a prison. Neither is wise. The saint design gets exploited. The dark card is the one to aim for. Real human beings contain self-interest and morality. They contain reciprocity and fairness. They contain loyalty and ambition. They contain error and creativity. All at the same time. You want a system that works reasonably well with ordinary human beings. Not only with saints. And not only against villains. Here's the misunderstanding to avoid. Designing for ordinary people doesn't mean expecting the worst. It means building in enough structure that one bad actor can't spread, while the good majority doesn't feel like suspects. So ask yourself this. Is a policy you've written designed for saints or for monsters? Next let's talk about a design tool that's easy to abuse.
