Research spotlight · Glewwe, Ilias & Kremer

Teacher incentives in Kenya

What it shows

Rewards tied to test scores raised scores on the linked exams. Most of it came through test prep. Little sign of better teaching. No lasting learning gains.

What it doesn't prove

Not that incentives "don't work." Experiments in India and Tanzania found different results. The design of the incentive changes the game.

Internomics · Module 11 — Change the Game

The second spotlight is the warning. It starts with theory. Bengt Holmström and Paul Milgrom's multitask analysis formalized a problem. Strong incentives on one measurable activity can pull effort away from other valuable work that's poorly measured. Paul Glewwe, Nauman Ilias and Michael Kremer studied a real case. It was a program in Kenyan primary schools that rewarded teachers based on students' test performance. Scores on the incentivized exams improved. At first glance that looks like success. But look closer. The researchers found little evidence that teacher attendance improved. Little evidence that homework assignment improved. Little evidence that classroom teaching improved. Teachers ran more test-preparation sessions. The score gains were concentrated on the exams linked to the incentive and not on unrelated tests. And the improvements didn't persist as broader long-run learning. The incentive worked. Just not entirely in the way the designers hoped. Here's what it suggests for you. Whenever you attach a payoff to a number, ask what behavior actually moves it. Calls made. Bugs fixed. Chores done. Then ask what harder-to-measure work might be quietly abandoned. Now here's what it doesn't prove. It doesn't prove that incentives don't work. Teacher-incentive experiments in rural India and Tanzania found different results. In Tanzania a design with multiple thresholds produced greater learning gains at lower cost than a design based on rank. So the design of the incentive changes the game. And there isn't always one correct rule. Goals like fairness and simplicity can trade off against each other. So can strategy-proofness. Now let's turn all of this into a tool you can use.
