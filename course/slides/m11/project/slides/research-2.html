Research spotlight · Glewwe, Ilias & Kremer

Teacher incentives in Kenya

What it shows

Rewards tied to test scores raised scores on the linked exams, mainly through test prep. Little sign of better attendance, homework or teaching, and no lasting learning gains.

What it doesn't prove

Not that incentives "don't work." Experiments in India and Tanzania found different results. The design of the incentive changes the game.

Internomics · Module 11 — Change the Game

The second spotlight is the warning. Holmström and Milgrom's multitask analysis formalized the problem that strong incentives on one measurable activity can pull effort away from other valuable but poorly measured work. Paul Glewwe, Nauman Ilias, and Michael Kremer studied a program in Kenyan primary schools that rewarded teachers based on students' test performance. Scores on the incentivized exams improved. At first glance: success. But they found little evidence that attendance, homework, or classroom teaching improved. Teachers ran more test-prep sessions, gains were concentrated on the linked exams rather than unrelated tests, and the improvements didn't persist as broader long-run learning. The incentive worked, just not the way the designers hoped. What it doesn't prove: that incentives don't work. Teacher-incentive experiments in rural India and Tanzania found different results; in Tanzania a multiple-threshold design produced greater learning gains at lower cost than a rank-based one. The design of the incentive changes the game, and goals like fairness, simplicity, and strategy-proofness can trade off.
