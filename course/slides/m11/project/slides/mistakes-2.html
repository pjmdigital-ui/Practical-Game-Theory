Internomics · Module 11 — Change the Game

Here are four more mistakes. Mistake five is assuming "set rule, get outcome." It feels like the job is done once the new rule is announced. It isn't. Players respond. They learn how the rule works. They adapt. So treat your design as a hypothesis and not as a finished product. Expect the system to talk back. Mistake six is designing for saints or for monsters. The saint design assumes everyone will behave perfectly and skips safeguards. The monster design assumes everyone is cheating and builds a prison. Design for ordinary human beings instead. They have self-interest and morality at the same time. Mistake seven is always adding rules and rewards. Our instinct is to fix every problem with one more rule. But remember the team that already took pride in its work. A bonus for every tiny output made coworkers stop helping each other. Sometimes the accumulated rules are the problem. Sometimes the best move is to remove the distorting incentive. Mistake eight is treating design as control or manipulation. Friction is powerful. Defaults are powerful. Hidden terms are powerful. That's exactly why they need care. Hold on to truth and autonomy. Hold on to dignity and informed choice. And there's one more in your workbook. Don't ignore exit. But don't treat it as automatically right either. Not every game deserves another move. Leaving still has costs. Here's the misunderstanding underneath all of these. People think game design is about being clever. Mostly it's about being humble and patient. You design. You watch. You adjust. Now let's put this to work in your own life this week.
