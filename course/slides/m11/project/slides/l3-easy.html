Internomics · Module 11 — Change the Game

This is one of the ideas I find most powerful in the entire framework. The highest form of strategic thinking isn't always becoming better at outsmarting everyone. Sometimes it's designing the interaction so people don't need to outsmart one another in the first place. Let me show you with something ordinary. A workplace expense system. Look at the left box. Bad design looks like forty ambiguous rules. Unclear exceptions. Managers interpreting the rules inconsistently. And employees constantly figuring out what they can get away with. That system turns every expense report into a small strategic contest. People spend energy on the game instead of the work. Now look at the right box. Better design makes the acceptable behavior obvious. Easy. Consistent. Verifiable. Nobody has to guess. Nobody has to probe the edges. The more you can make the desired behavior the easy behavior, the less energy everyone spends navigating the system. Think about how this connects to everything in this lesson. The couple with the chore system. The client with the scope. You and your phone. In every case the win came from making the right move the obvious move. We'll see the same idea at a much bigger scale in Lesson Four with school choice in Boston. Here's the misunderstanding to avoid. Simple doesn't mean loose. An easy system can still be firm. It just doesn't make people fight the structure to do the right thing. So ask yourself this. What system in your life forces people to be strategic just to do the normal thing? That brings us to the takeaway for this lesson.
