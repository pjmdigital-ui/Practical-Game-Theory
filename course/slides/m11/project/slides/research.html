Internomics · Module 11 — Change the Game

Two research spotlights this time. They pull in opposite directions on purpose. The first shows how powerful incentives can be. It's a field experiment by Adnan Khan and Asim Khwaja and Benjamin Olken. It took place in Punjab in Pakistan. It looked at property-tax administration. In one study 525 property-tax inspectors took part in a new system. Performance rankings determined the order in which inspectors could choose their future job postings. Think about what that design did. It didn't tell bureaucrats to work harder. Nobody gave a motivational speech. Instead the system connected performance with something many of them actually valued. Where they would be assigned to work. The result was substantial. The mechanism increased annual tax-revenue growth by roughly thirty to forty-one percent. The people hadn't been replaced. The work itself hadn't disappeared. The structure changed. And behavior changed with it. Here's what it suggests for your own conversations. Before you give another speech, ask what people genuinely value. Then ask whether your system connects the behavior you want to that thing. Now here's what it doesn't prove. It doesn't prove that any incentive redesign will improve outcomes. It doesn't prove this particular design would transfer unchanged to your team or your family. And it doesn't prove that people will respond in the form the designer intended. That last point is exactly what our second study is about. So hold this one with confidence and with care. Incentives can move behavior a lot. The next study shows they can move it in the wrong direction too.
