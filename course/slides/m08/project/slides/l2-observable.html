Commitment must usually be observable

A hidden commitment can't change their decision.

Hidden

I secretly make it impossible to accept less than $100,000. You don't know. Your behavior doesn't change.

Observable

You know about it or can credibly infer it. Now it can change your present decision.

Field example: factory workers were more likely to open a commitment savings account when the employer would observe the decision. (Breza, Kanz & Klapper)

Internomics · Module 8 — Commitment Changes the Game

Here's a condition people forget. A commitment usually has to be observable to affect the other player. Suppose I secretly make it impossible for myself to accept less than a hundred thousand. You don't know about it. Will your behavior change? Probably not. You'll keep pushing just like before. My private commitment may affect my future behavior. But it can't affect your present decision unless you know about it. Or unless you can credibly infer that it exists. That's why public commitments matter so much. Contracts matter. Delegated authority matters. Visible irreversible investments matter. They all alter other people's expectations. There's a nice field example of this in the book. Emily Breza and Martin Kanz and Leora Klapper ran a field experiment with factory workers. The workers were offered a commitment savings account. Opening one could signal that a worker expected to stay with the company. Workers were more likely to open the accounts when the employer would observe the decision. And in a separate experiment managers were more willing to invest in workers who held the accounts. Notice that this sits right between last module and this one. The account was partly a signal. "I expect to stay." But it also involved a real financial commitment. Here's the misunderstanding to avoid. People sometimes make a quiet decision and expect the other side to act differently. They won't. If it matters that they know, make sure they can see it. Now there's one more player who often needs a commitment. It's you.
