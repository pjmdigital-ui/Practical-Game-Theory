Schelling's paradox

A player can sometimes strengthen its position by openly making its own alternatives worse.

You don't just sound more serious. The future game has changed, so what they expect tomorrow changes what they do today.

Internomics · Module 8 — Commitment Changes the Game

Thomas Schelling made this paradox central to the study of strategic commitment. The Nobel committee summarized one of his key insights this way: a bargaining player can sometimes strengthen its position by openly making its own alternatives worse. The classic image is an army burning the bridge behind it. That seems insane. It's eliminated one of its own options. But retreat is no longer part of the future game, and the other side knows it. The determination has become more credible precisely because the army reduced its own freedom. Now, please hear me: the lesson is not "burn every bridge." That would be terrible advice. The lesson is that a commitment influences behavior today by changing what the other player expects will happen tomorrow. It isn't about sounding tough. It's that the future game is different.
