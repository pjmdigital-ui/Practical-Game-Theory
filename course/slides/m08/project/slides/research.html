Internomics · Module 8 — Commitment Changes the Game

We have two research spotlights this week. The first one is theory. Here's the point. Schelling showed the mechanism. He didn't promise that it works every time. When Thomas Schelling received the Nobel Prize the committee summarized one of his key insights this way. A bargaining player can sometimes strengthen its position by openly making its own alternatives worse. That's the paradox we opened this module with. The committee's materials also noted applications to delegating decision power. That's exactly the "my authority stops at this number" logic from Lesson Four. So what does it suggest for you? Ask how your future options look to the other side. They're not reacting to how you feel. They're reacting to what they expect you to be able to do later. Change those options and you can change their best response today. Now what does it not prove? It doesn't prove that commitment always creates power. It doesn't prove that you should never leave yourself an escape route. And it doesn't prove that the most extreme commitment wins. We've already seen why. Two extreme commitments can collide and produce no deal at all. And a commitment that ignores uncertainty can trap you. Here's the common misunderstanding. People hear "Nobel Prize" and "commitment creates power" and they stop listening. Then they start burning bridges everywhere. That's not what the theory says. It says commitment can work. It tells you how. It doesn't tell you to do it always. So keep that distinction in mind as we look at what happened when researchers tested this in the lab.
