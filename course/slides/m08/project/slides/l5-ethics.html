Internomics · Module 8 — Commitment Changes the Game

Now the ethics. Commitment can be used badly. And I want to be clear about where the line is. Imagine someone deliberately creates a crisis. Then they say "I've made it impossible for either of us to turn back, so now you have to do what I want." That can become coercive. They haven't just limited their own options. They've engineered a situation to strip away yours. This is where the ethical foundation of our whole framework matters. Understanding commitment does not give us permission to trap people. Our standard stays the same. Truth. Autonomy. Dignity. Informed choice. Now look at the four cards on screen. A contract can be a commitment. A boundary can be a commitment. A transparent deadline can be a commitment. A public promise can be a commitment. All of those can help people coordinate and trust one another. They make the future more predictable for everybody. The other person still gets to choose with full information. What's different about the crisis example? It intentionally destroys another person's meaningful ability to choose. That's something else entirely. So here's how I'd hold it. Strategy and ethics are separate questions. We need both. Strategy asks whether a commitment will work. Ethics asks whether it respects the other person. Here's the misunderstanding to avoid. Some people think "it worked" settles the question. It doesn't. A tactic can be effective and still wrong. So when you design a commitment ask yourself this. Does the other person still have a real and informed choice? Next let's remember that your commitment is itself a move in the game.
