Internomics · Module 8 — Commitment Changes the Game

Now the downside. Here's the point. A commitment can be perfectly credible and still lead somewhere terrible. Credible is not the same as good. Suppose two countries are in a confrontation. Country A publicly commits "If you cross this line, we will retaliate." Country B publicly commits "We will cross the line." Both commitments may be highly credible. Each side has staked its reputation on its words. Each side would pay a price for backing down. And that's exactly the problem. Being credible doesn't mean the outcome is good. It may mean both sides have removed the very paths that would have let them de-escalate. Neither one can step back without breaking its word. This is the dangerous side of commitment. A credible commitment can lock you into a terrible outcome. And here's what I want you to take from it. Game theory does not teach "commit more." That's a misreading I hear all the time. People learn about Schelling and they start making everything final. Game theory teaches something more useful. Understand what commitment does to the game. Sometimes it creates leverage. Sometimes it creates clarity. And sometimes it removes every exit for everyone. The same mechanism produces all three. You don't need two countries to see this. It happens between spouses. It happens between a manager and a team. It happens between two businesses that both announced their terms in public. So ask yourself this. Have you ever been stuck in a standoff where neither of you could back down without losing face? Let's shrink this down to a simple negotiation.
