Internomics · Module 8 — Commitment Changes the Game

Commitment works in both directions. A threat says "If you do X, I will do Y." A promise says "If you do X, I will reward you with Y." They feel like opposites. One is a stick and one is a carrot. But they share exactly the same strategic problem. Will the other player believe you'll follow through later? Here's the example from the book. A company tells an employee "If you hit this target, we'll promote you." The employee hits it. And now management realizes the promotion would be inconvenient. If the promise wasn't binding and management has a history of changing its mind, what happens? Future employees may stop responding to those promises. The promise loses its strategic value. Nobody works harder for a reward they don't believe in. So how do you fix that? You use a commitment mechanism. Maybe the bonus is written into a contract. Maybe the promotion criteria are published and externally reviewed. Maybe the money has already been placed in an incentive pool. The exact mechanism differs. The principle is the same. The future action becomes harder to reverse. And that changes behavior today. The employee now has a reason to believe you. Here's the misunderstanding to watch for. Leaders often think a promise is motivating because of how big the reward is. But a huge reward nobody believes does nothing. A modest reward people trust can do a lot. So think about your own team or family. Which of your promises do people actually believe? Next let's look at what happens when threats go unenforced.
