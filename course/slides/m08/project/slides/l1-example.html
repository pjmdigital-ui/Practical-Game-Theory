Example · The $100,000 negotiation

Same number, different game

The situation

I say $100,000 is my absolute minimum. You offer $85,000. I say no.

What happened

You wait. I come back at $95,000. My "absolute minimum" wasn't absolute.

The lesson

If a binding agreement truly stops me below $100,000, pushing stops making sense.

Fewer options. A stronger position. No need to ask "Will he cave?"

Internomics · Module 8 — Commitment Changes the Game

Let's play the whole thing out. The point is that the same number can sit inside two very different games. Here's the situation. I tell you a hundred thousand is my absolute minimum. You offer eighty-five. I say no. Then what happens? You wait. And eventually I come back at ninety-five. What did you learn? My absolute minimum wasn't absolute. My words weren't binding me. And from now on you'll treat every "final" number from me as an opening bid. Now imagine a different situation. For some reason I genuinely cannot accept less than a hundred thousand. Maybe I've entered an agreement that makes a lower price impossible. Maybe another party has an enforceable claim. Maybe I've handed the decision to someone with no authority to go lower. The mechanism doesn't matter much. What matters is that my future choice set is different. You no longer need to ask "Will he cave?" If that constraint is real and you can see it, pushing below a hundred thousand may no longer make sense for you. I have fewer options. And yet strategically my position may have become stronger. Here's the common misunderstanding. People think the second version works because I sound more confident. It doesn't. It works because the constraint is real and visible. If it were fake or hidden we'd be right back in the first game. So that's our first takeaway.
