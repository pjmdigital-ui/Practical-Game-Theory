Research spotlight · Swope, Cadigan & Schmitt

Commitment in the bargaining lab

What it shows

Credible commitment could raise the committer's payoff. This was especially true where others had reasons to hold out. It could also raise the chance of bargaining failure and reduce efficiency.

What it doesn't prove

That commitment helps in every negotiation. That a "final offer" works because it sounds firm. These are specific and structured lab games.

The evidence doesn't say "commit harder and you'll win." Commitment trades off credibility and power against flexibility and agreement.

Internomics · Module 8 — Commitment Changes the Game

The second spotlight is experimental. Here's the point. In the lab commitment did two things at once. It gave the committer more power. And it made deals more likely to fail. Kurtis Swope and John Cadigan and Pamela Schmitt ran laboratory bargaining games where players could credibly commit to a position. They found that commitment could increase the payoff to the person making it. That was especially true where the other parties had incentives to hold out for better terms. But there was a cost. Commitment could also increase the probability of bargaining failure. And it could reduce overall efficiency. Those aren't contradictory results. They're two sides of one mechanism. You gained power by eliminating flexibility. But flexibility was also one of the things that might have let a deal happen. The same research notes something else. Credible ultimatums can reduce uncertainty and remove the incentive for strategic delay in some settings. That's the Friday-at-noon idea from Lesson Four. The book cites a 2026 experiment too. It looked at commitment before sequential ultimatum bargaining. People did use commitment strategically. But they used it less aggressively than the theory predicts. Reciprocity and equal opportunity seemed to matter. So what does all this not prove? It doesn't prove commitment helps in every negotiation. It doesn't prove a "final offer" works because it sounds firm. These are specific and structured lab games. Game theory gives us the mechanism. Human beings add fairness and norms and mistakes and learning. So the evidence doesn't say "commit harder and you'll win." It says commitment trades off credibility and power against flexibility and the chance of agreement. Now let's turn all of this into a tool.
