Internomics · Module 8 — Commitment Changes the Game

Let's clear up two things people constantly confuse with commitment. First, it's not the sunk-cost fallacy. This distinction is essential. Suppose I invest a hundred thousand dollars in a project. That investment may work strategically. It may signal seriousness. It may create incentives to continue. It may change what competitors expect me to do. But imagine six months later we discover the project is terrible. The money is gone. Should we continue just because "we've already invested too much to quit"? No. That's the sunk-cost fallacy. Here's the difference. A strategic commitment uses today's action to deliberately change tomorrow's incentives or expectations. The sunk-cost fallacy lets yesterday's irreversible cost distort today's decision when that cost should no longer matter. Second, commitment is not toughness. A person who never changes their position isn't necessarily strategically committed. They may simply be stubborn. A real strategic commitment has a purpose. It changes incentives. Or expectations. Or options. Or credibility. And it does that in a way that affects the game. If refusing to adapt gives you no strategic benefit and creates enormous cost, that's not sophistication. That's rigidity. Commitment is valuable only when the constraint improves the game enough to justify losing flexibility. So a smart strategist makes commitments. A smart strategist also knows when a past commitment has become a liability. Here's a question to try. Think of something you're still doing mainly because of what you already put into it. Is that commitment? Or sunk cost? Next we need to talk about ethics.
