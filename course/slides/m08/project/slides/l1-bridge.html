Internomics · Module 8 — Commitment Changes the Game

The classic image for this idea is an army burning the bridge behind it. The point is simple. By destroying its own way out the army changes what the other side believes it will do. Picture two opposing forces. One army still has a bridge behind it. If the battle becomes costly it can retreat. And the opposing side knows that. They can plan around it. They can push just hard enough to make retreat look attractive. Now imagine the first army destroys its own bridge. That seems insane. It has eliminated one of its own options. But look at what else it did. It changed what the opponent believes. Retreat is no longer part of the future game. The army has made its determination more credible precisely because it reduced its own freedom. That's the logic people use to illustrate Schelling's insight. Deliberately restricting your own alternatives can alter the other player's incentives. Now please hear me on this. The lesson is not "burn every bridge." That would be terrible advice. In most of life you want your bridges. You want options. You want a way back. The lesson is narrower than that. Flexibility has value. Flexibility can also undermine credibility. That's the tradeoff. So here's the question. Is there a bridge in your life right now that you keep pointing to? And does it make your promises weaker than they need to be? Let's see this play out in our negotiation.
