The problem with "final"

"This is my absolute minimum" is only a statement.

If you can still accept less later, the other side can test you.

Maybe $100,000 really is my floor.

Maybe I'd take $90,000 if you held firm.

Maybe even $80,000.

You don't know. So you test me.

Internomics · Module 8 — Commitment Changes the Game

Here's the point. A "final" number is only a statement unless something stops me from changing it. Imagine we're negotiating. I look you in the eye and say "A hundred thousand is my absolute minimum. I will not accept a dollar less." What do you do? That depends on whether you believe me. Maybe a hundred thousand really is my floor. Or maybe I'd take ninety if you held firm long enough. Maybe even eighty. You don't know. My statement is only a statement. So you might test me. You make a lower offer and you wait. And here's the part most people miss. If I move you don't just get a better price. You learn something about me. You learn that my words don't bind me. That lesson doesn't stay in this one deal. It follows every "final" number I give you after that. So the question isn't whether I sound firm. I can sound as firm as I like. The question is whether anything actually stops me from changing my mind. Think about your own life for a second. How often have you said "this is my final offer" and then moved? And what did the other person learn when you did? Now let's ask the odd question. Why would having less freedom ever help me?
