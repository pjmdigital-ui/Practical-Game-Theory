Why it matters: delegation can create commitment. Handing authority to someone with fewer choices changes what's possible. It has to be true.

Internomics · Module 8 — Commitment Changes the Game

There's a major strategic difference between "I don't want to" and "I can't." Here's why. When you say "I'd rather not" you invite pressure. The other side knows your preference can change. Push hard enough or wait long enough and maybe it will. A preference is a mood. Moods move. But suppose you can truthfully say "My authority stops at this number." Something different has happened. It's not your mood that changed. It's your available actions. There's simply no point in pushing on a door that's locked. This is one reason delegation can create commitment. A decision-maker can sometimes strengthen a bargaining position by transferring authority to someone whose choices are more restricted. Schelling's commitment logic has been applied to exactly this kind of delegation and institutional constraint. Think about why companies send a negotiator with a fixed range instead of sending the owner. The owner could say yes to anything. The negotiator can't. And the other side knows it. Now here's the key word on this slide. Truthfully. This only works when the constraint is real. If you invent a constraint you're lying. You're also building a credibility problem you'll pay for later. We'll get to the fake version in a moment. So here's a question for you. When you say no to people do you usually say "I'd rather not"? Or do you explain what actually limits you? Next let's talk about how to tell a real constraint from a fake one.
