Internomics · Module 10 — Diagnose the Coordination Problem

Remember cheap talk from Module Seven? We learned that costless messages aren't necessarily credible evidence. Anyone can say anything when it costs nothing. But communication can do a different job entirely. It can help people coordinate. And for that job it doesn't need to be costly or binding. Cheap talk may be weak as proof. It can be powerful as coordination. Those are different jobs. That explains something you've probably noticed. "Just talk about it" feels miraculous in one situation and useless in another. Here's the contrast from the chapter. Two people both want to meet at the airport. One thinks Terminal A. The other thinks Terminal B. A thirty-second conversation solves the entire problem. Nobody needs to be persuaded. Nobody needs proof. They just need to line up. Now imagine two people arguing over who receives a hundred-thousand-dollar payment. They can talk for hours. Talking may clarify the disagreement. It doesn't make the conflict disappear. The money still goes to one of them. So before you say "We need better communication," stop and ask what communication is supposed to accomplish. Is the problem missing information? Is it credibility? Is it persuasion? Is it bargaining? Or is it coordination? Each one calls for a different kind of conversation. The common mistake is prescribing "more communication" as if it were one medicine for every illness. It isn't. Treating every communication problem as persuasion is like treating every medical problem with the same medication. Sometimes nobody needs convincing. They just need to know what we're doing and who's doing it. Let's see that play out across five departments.
