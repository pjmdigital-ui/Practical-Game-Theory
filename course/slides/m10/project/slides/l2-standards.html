Internomics · Module 10 — Diagnose the Coordination Problem

Think about the standards you use every day without noticing. Language. Electrical outlets. File formats. Traffic rules. Time zones. Measurement systems. Often the value comes partly from everyone using the same standard. The chapter has a nice thought experiment here. Imagine inventing a brilliant new alphabet. It's fifteen percent more efficient than English. Impressive. But if nobody else can read it, that efficiency isn't especially useful. The alphabet is better on paper and worse in practice. Its value depends on other people adopting it. That's the point of this slide. Coordination itself has value. It's not just a nice side benefit of a good choice. Sometimes it's most of the benefit. Now here's the caution, and it's an important one. Bad conventions can be stable too. Once a rule exists people can get trapped in it. An organization says, "We've always done it this way." Maybe that convention once solved a real coordination problem. But the environment changed. Now the convention creates inefficiency. And it still holds. Why? Because the fact that everyone coordinates around a behavior doesn't mean it's the best behavior. It may simply mean that everyone expects everyone else to keep doing it. So don't confuse stable with good. A stable convention tells you people are coordinated. It doesn't tell you they're coordinated on the right thing. We'll come back to that in the next module, where sometimes the answer is redesigning the system. For now let's see how this plays out with a real choice between two tools.
