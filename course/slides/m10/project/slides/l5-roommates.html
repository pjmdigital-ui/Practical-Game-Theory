Internomics · Module 10 — Diagnose the Coordination Problem

Now here's a trap, and it's an important one. Everything we've said so far could make you think that shared goals mean coordination. They don't always. Suppose four roommates all prefer a clean apartment. At first glance that sounds like a coordination problem. Everyone wants the same thing. We just need a chore chart, right? But imagine each roommate is privately thinking something like this. The apartment should be clean. But I'd rather somebody else did the cleaning. Now the structure is different. If everyone else cleans, your personal best outcome may be to enjoy the clean apartment without doing any of the work. That's a free-rider problem. Economists also call it a public-goods problem. The group shares a preference for the final outcome. But the individual incentive still pushes each person toward doing less and hoping others do more. That is not a pure coordination problem. It's a social dilemma. Public-goods experiments are built around exactly this tension. The group does better when people contribute. But each individual can often do better personally by holding back and enjoying what everyone else puts in. Research consistently finds that this tension produces free riding and fragile cooperation. Here's why the difference matters so much. In a coordination problem, people are waiting for clarity. In a social dilemma, clarity isn't what's missing. Everybody knows the dishes need doing. So the misunderstanding to watch for is this. "We all want the same thing" can hide a free-rider problem just as easily as it can hide a coordination problem.
