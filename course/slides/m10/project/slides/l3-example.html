Internomics · Module 10 — Diagnose the Coordination Problem

Here's the example for this lesson. Imagine five departments that all need to switch to a new reporting procedure. Every department prefers the new procedure if all five use it. That's important. Nobody is against it. But switching first imposes extra work. If you switch and the others don't, you're now running two systems and translating between them. So each department waits for proof that the others are moving. Five groups. All willing. All waiting. Now suppose management publicly announces: every department switches Monday. And everyone can see the migration plan. Who's doing what. When. The strategic calculation changes. Here's what I want you to notice. It doesn't change because management persuaded anyone that the new system is good. They already believed that. It changes because the uncertainty about what everyone else will do has been reduced. Each department can now see that the others are moving on the same day. Switching is no longer a lonely bet. That's coordination. And it's a very different intervention from a pep talk. A pep talk would have explained the benefits again. It would have told people to show some leadership. It might even have made people feel guilty. And it would have left the real problem untouched, because nobody would know whether the other four were going to move. The chapter's version of the kickoff is even more explicit. Here's the plan. Here are the teams responsible. Here is what everyone else is doing. So think about a change you've tried to lead. Did you give people a reason? Or did you give them a shared date and visible proof that others were moving? Let's put this on one line.
