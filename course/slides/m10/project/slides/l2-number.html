Internomics · Module 10 — Diagnose the Coordination Problem

Try this one. Pick a positive whole number. Another person will independently pick one too. If you both choose the same number, you win. What would you pick? Many people choose 1. Why? There are infinitely many positive whole numbers. And 1 isn't mathematically superior for the purpose of matching. Any number would work just as well if you both chose it. But 1 stands out. It's the first. It's simple. And crucially you can imagine the other person thinking the same thing. That shared sense of obviousness is what solves the problem. Here's the fascinating part. The players don't need to prefer the focal point. Nobody loves the number 1. They only need to believe that the other person is likely to recognize it too. Later experiments by Judith Mehta and Chris Starmer and Robert Sugden looked directly at this. They found evidence that people really do use salience in pure coordination games. They don't just choose randomly among equal options. Now here's the practical principle the chapter draws from it. When several solutions would work, the easiest one for everyone to recognize may be strategically better than the theoretically perfect one. That's a strange idea for people who love getting things right. It means sometimes the obvious answer wins because it's obvious. So where in your work is there an obvious default everyone would recognize? You might be underrating it. Next let's look at how groups turn focal points into habits.
