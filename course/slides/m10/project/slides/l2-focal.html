Internomics · Module 10 — Diagnose the Coordination Problem

Thomas Schelling made this idea famous with a wonderfully simple question. Imagine you and another person are told to meet somewhere in New York City tomorrow. You cannot communicate. Where do you go? Think about it for a second before I go on. There are thousands of possible locations. And yet people often don't choose randomly. They look for something that seems obvious. A famous landmark. A central station. A conventional time like noon. Notice what's going on in their heads. They aren't asking "Where would I most like to go?" They're asking something stranger. What place will the other person think I will think is obvious? It's a guess about a guess. And remarkably it often works. Schelling called these solutions focal points. His experiments suggested that people can coordinate at rates far above random chance. They do it by using shared culture and context and salience. The Nobel committee specifically highlighted this part of his work. Here's why this matters for you. In a coordination problem the hard part isn't caring about the outcome. Both people care. The hard part is predicting the other person. A focal point gives everyone the same prediction. It's something you can both point to without talking. So where would you go?
