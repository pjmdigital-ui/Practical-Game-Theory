Internomics · Module 10 — Diagnose the Coordination Problem

The second spotlight is about communication as coordination. Here's what was done. Russell Cooper and Douglas DeJong and Robert Forsythe and Thomas Ross ran lab coordination games. Players could send nonbinding messages before they acted. In the field, Aflagah and Bernard and Viceisza worked with 79 groundnut cooperatives in Senegal. They ran coordination games with 839 farmers. Then they ran a natural field experiment with roughly 2,800 cooperative members. They asked members how much they intended to sell through the cooperative. Then they revealed the group's combined intentions. Here's what was found. In the lab, nonbinding messages often improved coordination substantially. In some conditions one-way communication helped players land on the better outcome. In another type of game two-way communication consistently produced the best one. In Senegal, treated farmers contributed more than twice as much toward collective selling as the control group on average. And the effect was larger in bigger groups, where coordinating is harder. No new incentive was added. What changed was information about what everyone else intended to do. What does it suggest? When people already want an outcome but doubt that others will act, making intentions visible can move behavior. Now here's what it does not prove. Communication did not eliminate every coordination failure. And neither result supports "talk more and every group problem disappears." Communication helps when it resolves the uncertainty that's actually blocking coordination. It won't dissolve a real conflict. It won't fix a free-rider incentive. And notice one more thing. The farmers already saw the benefits and still held back. Shared goals alone don't guarantee action. Let's turn all of this into a tool.
