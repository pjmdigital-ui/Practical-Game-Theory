Research spotlight · Communication as coordination

Revealing intentions changed behavior

What it shows

In lab games, nonbinding messages often substantially improved coordination (Cooper et al.). In Senegal, revealing farmers' aggregate selling intentions more than doubled contributions, with no new incentive.

What it doesn't prove

That talking more fixes every group problem. Communication didn't remove every failure. It helps when it resolves the blocking uncertainty, not a real conflict or a free-rider incentive.

Internomics · Module 10 — Diagnose the Coordination Problem

The second spotlight is about communication as coordination. Russell Cooper, Douglas DeJong, Robert Forsythe and Thomas Ross ran lab coordination games where players could send nonbinding messages first. Those messages often substantially improved coordination. In some conditions, one-way communication increased selection of the better equilibrium, and in another type of game, two-way communication consistently produced it. In the field, Aflagah, Bernard and Viceisza worked with 79 groundnut cooperatives in Senegal, running coordination games with 839 farmers and then a natural field experiment with roughly 2,800 members. They asked members how much they intended to sell through the cooperative and revealed the group's aggregate intentions. Treated farmers contributed more than twice as much toward collective selling as the control group on average, and the effect was larger in bigger groups. No new incentive was added. What changed was information about what everyone else intended to do. What it doesn't prove: communication didn't eliminate every coordination failure, and neither result supports "talk more and every group problem disappears." It helps when it resolves the uncertainty that's actually blocking coordination. And notice, the farmers already saw the benefits and still held back. Shared goals alone don't guarantee action.
