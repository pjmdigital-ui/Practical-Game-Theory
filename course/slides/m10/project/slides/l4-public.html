Internomics · Module 10 — Diagnose the Coordination Problem

Here's a practical version of common knowledge. Think about the difference between these two situations. In the first a manager individually tells five employees, "You're all responsible for making this change." Five private conversations. Technically everyone knows. But each person walks away wondering. Did everyone else get the message? Are they actually going to do anything? In the second situation the manager gathers all five and says, "You five are responsible for this change. And each of you now knows who else is responsible." Same information. But the second statement creates something the first doesn't. Each person can now reason along these lines. They know I'm responsible. I know they're responsible. They know that I know they're responsible. That chain is what lets people act without waiting. The chapter makes the same point about a new system. Telling every employee privately to start Monday is one thing. A public kickoff is another. Every department switches Monday. Here's the plan. Here are the teams responsible. Here is what everyone else is doing. The company hasn't changed anyone's preferences. It has changed what people know about what everyone else knows and intends. Now we don't need to turn every office meeting into an exercise in epistemic game theory. Please don't do that. The point is simple. Sometimes coordination requires making expectations mutually visible. The common mistake is to send five careful private messages and think you've done the job. You've informed people. You haven't coordinated them. Next let's look at why "everyone knows what to do" is so often false.
