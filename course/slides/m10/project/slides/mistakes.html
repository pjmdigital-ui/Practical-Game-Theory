Internomics · Module 10 — Diagnose the Coordination Problem

Here are the mistakes I see most often. There are eight of them, so I've split them across two slides. First, treating every disagreement as a conflict. When you do that you end up spending your energy persuading someone who already wants what you want. Remember the dinner problem. Before you argue, ask whether you actually want incompatible things. Second, calling coordination failure a character flaw. This is the big one. Before you say "nobody takes initiative," ask whether there was a mutually understood assignment. And ask whether people were simply unwilling to act alone. Think of the customer nobody called. Nobody was lazy. Nobody owned it. Third, assuming a shared goal settles it. A shared goal tells people where they want to end up. It doesn't tell them how everyone gets there. And a shared goal can still hide a free-rider problem. The roommates all wanted a clean apartment. So run the simple test. Fourth, saying "everyone knows what to do." It's one of the most common phrases in any workplace. And it's often false. Ask instead whether you actually share the same expectation about what happens next. Remember Thursday. One person heard finished. The other heard draft review. Here's a question for you. Which of these four have you made in the last month? Be honest. I've made all of them. The point isn't to feel bad. It's to catch yourself the next time. Let's look at the other four.
