Example · The dinner problem

Same word, very different game

The situation: Two restaurants. We both prefer eating together to eating alone. I lean Italian. You lean steakhouse.

What matters most: Not our first choice. Ending up at the same restaurant. If we split up we both lose.

The contrast: One reservation is left and we each want it for separate events. Now our interests are directly opposed.

The lesson: Both get called a "disagreement." The first is coordination. The second is conflict.

Internomics · Module 10 — Diagnose the Coordination Problem

Here's the example the chapter opens with. Imagine you and I are trying to meet for dinner. There are two restaurants. We both prefer eating together to eating alone. I slightly prefer the Italian place. You slightly prefer the steakhouse. So what's the most important thing here? Probably not getting our first choice. It's ending up at the same restaurant. If I go to the Italian place and you go to the steakhouse, we both lose. We each eat alone at the place we supposedly preferred. That's a coordination problem. Now compare that with a different situation. There's only one restaurant reservation available. We both want it for separate events. Now our interests are directly opposed. If I get it you don't. In both cases you'd call it a disagreement. Same word. Very different game. And the fix is very different too. In the first case a quick text solves almost everything. "Let's do Italian tonight and steak next time." In the second case no text makes the conflict disappear. Somebody has to give something up. Here's the trap. When you're in the middle of it both feel the same. Both feel like friction. Both feel like the other person is being difficult. So the first move is to stop and ask which one you're in. Do we actually want incompatible things? Or do we want compatible things and just haven't lined up our actions? Let's put that on one line.
