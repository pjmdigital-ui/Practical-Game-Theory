Internomics · Module 10 — Diagnose the Coordination Problem

Let's run the test on the roommates. Everyone wants the apartment clean. That part is not in question. Now suppose three roommates are definitely going to clean it. There's no uncertainty at all. Everyone knows the plan. And the fourth roommate thinks, "Great. Then I don't have to." Look at what that tells you. That isn't a failure to coordinate. They coordinated just fine. Everyone knew exactly what was happening. If you asked the fourth roommate our simple test, the honest answer would be "No. If I knew the others would clean, I'd let them." The individual incentive is the problem. Same desired group outcome. Different strategic problem. Experimental research shows how this can unfold even when people are decent. Fischbacher and Gächter found that people often cooperate conditionally. They contribute if others contribute. But that conditional cooperation is imperfect. Over time contributions can decline. Eventually free riding can become widespread. And that happened even though most participants weren't simply behaving as pure selfish maximizers. So this isn't about bad people. It's about a structure that slowly rewards doing a little less. Here's why it matters for what you do next. The clearest chore chart in the world might not fix this house. They don't need more clarity. They already have it. They need something that changes the incentive or the accountability. That's a different toolbox from the one we used for coordination. So think about your own version of this apartment. Is there a shared space or shared task where clarity is fine but effort keeps sliding? Let's close the lesson.
