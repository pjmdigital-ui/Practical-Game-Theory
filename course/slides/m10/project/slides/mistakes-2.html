Internomics · Module 10 — Diagnose the Coordination Problem

Here are the other four. Fifth, telling people privately what needs to be public. Private messages leave each person wondering about the others. Did they get it too? Are they going to act? When mutual awareness matters, say it in front of everyone. That's the 3:00 meeting. It's also Dana's kickoff email. Sixth, over-optimizing among acceptable options. This one catches smart people. You have three good choices and you spend twenty minutes finding the best. But when failing to coordinate costs more than the difference among the options, stop optimizing and coordinate. Flip a coin if you have to. Seventh, prescribing "better communication" without a diagnosis. Ask what the communication needs to accomplish. Is it information? Credibility? Persuasion? Bargaining? Coordination? A thirty-second chat fixes the airport terminal mix-up. No amount of talking makes the hundred-thousand-dollar dispute disappear. Eighth, assuming a stable convention is a good one. Everyone may follow a rule only because everyone expects everyone else to. The fact that a group is coordinated doesn't mean it's coordinated on the right thing. "We've always done it this way" is a sign of a stable convention. It isn't proof of a good one. And sometimes the answer isn't better coordination inside the system. Sometimes the system itself needs redesigning. That's exactly where Module Eleven picks up. So here's a quick check. Which of these eight do you think your team makes most often? That's probably the best place to use your field assignment. Let's look at it.
