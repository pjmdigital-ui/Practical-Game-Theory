Internomics · Module 10 — Diagnose the Coordination Problem

A job title can be more than a description. It can be coordination technology. Look at the four tools on this slide. A role answers the question "Who is supposed to do what?" A deadline answers "When are we supposed to act?" A meeting agenda answers "What are we coordinating around?" And a chain of command answers "Who decides when disagreement remains?" Each one removes a question that people would otherwise have to guess about. Processes aren't automatically good. They can become bureaucratic, and we've all seen that. But the reason organizations create them is important. Repeated coordination is costly. If the same uncertainty appears every day, eventually someone creates a rule. The rule becomes a shortcut. Instead of everyone solving "Who's going to do this?" again and again, the convention answers it. This connects to leadership too. Leadership often involves less persuasion than people think. Sometimes the leader's main job is to make expectations clear enough that everyone can coordinate around them. Imagine a team entering a crisis. Ten people all want to help. If all ten independently decide what to do, they may get in each other's way. Then a leader says: you handle this. You call them. You stay here. Everyone meets back here at six. The leader hasn't necessarily changed anyone's motivation. They wanted to help before. What the leader created is a shared coordination structure. That's one reason leadership becomes more valuable as a group grows and the work gets more interdependent. So ask yourself. When your team is stuck, do they need inspiring? Or do they need someone to hand out the roles? Let's see what happens when nobody does.
