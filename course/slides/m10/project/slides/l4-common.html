Internomics · Module 10 — Diagnose the Coordination Problem

Now let's go one level deeper. Sometimes everyone has the necessary information and coordination still fails. Why? Because there's a difference between everyone knowing something and everyone knowing that everyone knows it. Game theorists call the stronger condition common knowledge. Ariel Rubinstein's famous Electronic Mail Game showed how sharp this difference can be. In his theoretical example, players could pile up many levels of "I know that you know that I know." And the situation still behaved very differently from one where the fact was truly common knowledge. That sounds abstract. So let's make it ordinary. Suppose a manager tells you privately that the meeting is moving to three o'clock. You know the meeting is at three. But you don't know whether everyone else was told. Should you show up at three? Probably. But you may still wonder what happens if everyone else comes at two. Now imagine the manager says it to a room containing the whole team. Everyone, we're moving the meeting to three. You saw everyone hear it. They saw you hear it. Everyone can reasonably expect everyone else to act on the same information. Here's the point. Nothing about the meeting changed. What changed was the mutual awareness of the information. That's why public announcements matter. So do shared dashboards. So do visible schedules and rituals and common procedures. They aren't just transmitting information. They create shared expectations. So here's a question for you. What important expectation in your life lives only in private messages?
