People coordinate far above chance

What it shows

Internomics · Module 10 — Diagnose the Coordination Problem

Two research spotlights this module. The first is about focal points. Schelling posed matching puzzles with no communication allowed, like choosing a meeting place in New York or picking the same positive number. People coordinated far above random chance by using shared culture, context and salience, and the Nobel committee specifically highlighted this part of his work. Later, Judith Mehta, Chris Starmer and Robert Sugden tested focal points experimentally in pure coordination games and found evidence that people use salience rather than choosing randomly among equivalent options. What it suggests: when a group has several workable options, the one everyone recognizes most easily has real strategic value. What it doesn't prove: that the most salient option is the best one. It may just be the easiest to coordinate around. And salience has limits. Experiments where players prefer different outcomes find lower coordination than pure coordination settings, and later studies, such as Parravano and Poulsen, and Rojo Arjona, Sitzia and Zheng, found that higher stakes or stronger salience didn't reliably help once conflicts of interest were built in.
