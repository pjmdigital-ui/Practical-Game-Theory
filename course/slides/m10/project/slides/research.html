Internomics · Module 10 — Diagnose the Coordination Problem

We have two research spotlights in this module. The first is about focal points. Here's what was done. Thomas Schelling posed matching puzzles where no communication was allowed. Choose a meeting place in New York. Pick the same positive number as a stranger. Later, Judith Mehta and Chris Starmer and Robert Sugden tested focal points experimentally. They used pure coordination games, where any option works as long as both players match. Here's what was found. In Schelling's puzzles people coordinated far above random chance. They did it by using shared culture and context and salience. The Nobel committee specifically highlighted this part of his work. Mehta and her colleagues found evidence that people really do use salience. They don't just pick randomly among equal options. What does it suggest? When a group has several workable options, the one everyone recognizes most easily has real strategic value. The existing standard. The obvious default. Now here's what it does not prove. It doesn't show that the most salient option is the best one. It may just be the easiest to coordinate around. Those are different claims. And salience has limits. Experiments where players prefer different outcomes find lower coordination than pure coordination settings. Later studies found that higher stakes or stronger salience didn't reliably help once conflicts of interest were built in. Those include work by Parravano and Poulsen. They also include work by Rojo Arjona and Sitzia and Zheng. So the misunderstanding to avoid is thinking a focal point can settle anything. It settles uncertainty. It doesn't settle conflict.
