Internomics · Module 10 — Diagnose the Coordination Problem

This one can be surprisingly hard for people. Two people have three equally acceptable options. They spend twenty minutes debating which one is best. But the cost of failing to coordinate is far larger than the difference among the options. At some point the strategic problem becomes very simple. Stop optimizing and coordinate. That's why arbitrary rules sometimes work so well. Alternate. Flip a coin. Rotate. Use alphabetical order. Let whoever cared most last time defer this time. The rule doesn't need to reveal some profound truth. It only needs to be a mutually understood way of selecting one option. Fifty-fifty is an interesting case. Why is an equal split so common? One explanation is fairness. People may simply prefer equal division. But there's another possibility. Fifty-fifty is also extremely easy for both sides to anticipate. Experiments on tacit bargaining have found that equal splits can work as powerful focal points. So fairness can be both a preference and a coordination device. We don't have to pick one explanation. Here's the story from the chapter I love. A couple has the same argument every week. Who's picking up the kids Thursday? Every week they renegotiate. Every week there's confusion. Every week somebody gets irritated. At some point almost any workable convention beats that. For example: whoever doesn't do Tuesday handles Thursday. The particular rule may not matter much. What matters is that everyone knows the rule before the conflict arises. So what are you renegotiating every week? Now let's talk about why standards matter so much.
