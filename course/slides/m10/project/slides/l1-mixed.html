Internomics · Module 10 — Diagnose the Coordination Problem

Real interactions are often messier than pure coordination. They contain coordination and conflict at the same time. The classic game theory example is called the Battle of the Sexes. The original name is dated. But the structure is genuinely useful, so stay with me. Two people want to spend the evening together. That part is aligned. But one of them prefers the concert. The other prefers the football game. That part is conflict. Now think about their worst outcome. It isn't going to the event they like less. It's each of them going to a different event and spending the evening apart. So they need to coordinate. And at the same time they disagree over which outcome to coordinate on. Game theorists call this a mixed-motive game. There's real shared interest in it. There's also real tension in it. And here's the misunderstanding to watch for. People tend to see only one half. Some see only the shared interest and say "We want the same thing, so why is this hard?" Others see only the tension and treat their partner like an opponent. Both miss the actual structure. You need to see both halves at once. The shared part tells you that you both lose if you split up. The conflict part tells you there's still something to settle about which way to go. This isn't just about date nights. It shows up at work and at home all the time. Let's look at where.
