Internomics · Module 1 — See the Game

When people hear the words game theory they usually picture chess. Or poker. Or business competition. Maybe two countries threatening each other. Those are certainly games. But game theory is much broader than that. At its most basic level it studies strategic interaction. That means situations where the choices of one decision-maker affect the outcomes available to others. The formal framework has four parts and they're on the slide. There are the players. There are their available strategies. There are their preferences or payoffs. And there are the outcomes that result when those choices meet. Here's what I want you to notice. Look at that list and tell me where the winner is. Where's the loser? They aren't there. Nothing in the definition says somebody has to beat somebody. A game is simply a situation where the outcome depends at least partly on the decisions of more than one person. That's the foundation of everything we'll do in this course. Think about your own week. How many of your conversations had an outcome that depended only on you? Probably none of the important ones. Your raise depends on your boss. Your project depends on your team. Dinner plans depend on your spouse. Every one of those is a game in this sense. And the common misunderstanding is to hear "game" and think "contest." That reading will lead you astray all through this course. So let's look at just how many different kinds of games there are.
