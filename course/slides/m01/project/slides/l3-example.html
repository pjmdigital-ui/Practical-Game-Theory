Example · The contractor negotiation

What happens if you both walk away?

The situation

You want a contractor to lower their price. They want you to accept it. You could spend all your energy on persuasion.

What happened

You ask about walking away. Maybe you have four other contractors. Maybe they have ten customers waiting. Maybe neither of you has a good option.

The lesson

The conversation sounds the same in every version. Each version is a different game. The right move changes with it.

Internomics · Module 1 — See the Game

Let's make it concrete. Here's the situation. You're negotiating with a contractor. You want them to lower their price. They want you to accept their price. The natural move is to spend all your time trying to persuade them. You list your reasons. You point to other quotes. You push. But there's another question you can ask. What happens if we both walk away? Look at the middle card. Maybe you have four other contractors you could call. Maybe they have ten other customers waiting. Maybe neither of you has a particularly good alternative. Or maybe one of you desperately needs this deal and the other doesn't. Now here's the thing that makes this example so useful. If you recorded the conversation in each of those versions it might sound identical. Same price on the table. Same back-and-forth. Same polite words. But each version is a different game. If you have four other contractors you can afford to hold firm. If they have ten customers waiting and you have nobody, pushing hard may just send them to the next customer. If neither of you has a good option, you both have a reason to find a deal that works. And the right move in one version could be the wrong move in another. That's the lesson on the right. Here's the mistake I see most. People prepare their arguments and never map the alternatives. So before your next negotiation, write down two lines. What happens to me if we walk away? What happens to them? Let's lock in the takeaway.
