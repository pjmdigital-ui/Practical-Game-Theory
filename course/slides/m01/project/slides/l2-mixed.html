Internomics · Module 1 — See the Game

Let me give you a picture of what "mixed interests" means. Imagine two business partners. They both want the company to succeed. So are they cooperating? Yes. But look at the row of cards. They might disagree about how much each one should be paid. They might disagree about who has decision-making authority. They might disagree about how much money to reinvest. They might disagree about how fast to grow. And they might disagree about who deserves the credit. So are they cooperating or competing? The honest answer is both. Their interests are neither completely aligned nor completely opposed. They're mixed. And that's much closer to the structure of most human interactions. This is the same mixture Thomas Schelling emphasized in his work. Real strategic situations usually contain common interests and conflicting interests at the same time. Here's why that changes how you talk. When a disagreement heats up we tend to ask "Who's right?" or "Who's trying to win?" Those questions push you toward a fight. Look at the right side of the slide. First ask where your interests are aligned. Then ask where they're different. That question does two things. It reminds both of you of the shared goal. And it makes the real differences specific enough to work on. The common mistake is to treat a mixed game like a pure contest. You end up fighting over the part you disagree on and forget the larger part you agree on. So try it now. Pick a running disagreement in your life. Write one line on where you're aligned. Write one line on where you differ. Next let's separate what people want from what they can actually do.
