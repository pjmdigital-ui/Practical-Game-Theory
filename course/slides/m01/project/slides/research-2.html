Internomics · Module 1 — See the Game

The second study comes from Charness and Sontuoso in 2023. It's a study of what they call frame-dependent rationalizability. That's a mouthful. Here's what they did. Participants played coordination games built around visual objects. The researchers varied which features of the choices players were made aware of. And here's what they found. Changing which frames players were aware of changed their strategic behavior. So what does that mean for us? People don't necessarily notice every strategically relevant feature of the situation in front of them. We miss things. That's a strong reason to ask yourself on purpose: what am I failing to notice about the game? Now the limits. This was a controlled lab coordination task. It wasn't a workplace conversation or a family dinner. And it doesn't show that any particular habit of mind improves everyday outcomes. There's also a useful counterweight here. Colin Camerer's review of behavioral game theory shows that framing and social preferences push real behavior away from simple game-theoretic predictions. So does learning. The models don't claim people are perfectly rational. They help you spot strategic forces you'd miss by looking only at words and personalities. So here's the honest standard for this module. Representing situations in terms of players and choices and payoffs is basic game theory. The evidence that construal affects play is strong. But the evidence that teaching people to see the game improves real-world communication has not been established. The Game Map is a practical synthesis. It is not a tested intervention.
