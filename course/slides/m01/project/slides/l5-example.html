Internomics · Module 1 — See the Game

This distinction is going to matter more and more as we go through the course. It's the difference between a person problem and a game problem. Here's the situation. A manager tells me "My employees aren't cooperating." And the conclusion comes fast. They're selfish. That's a person problem. Fix the people. Replace the people. Lecture the people. But look at the middle card. Before you go there, look at the structure. Perhaps the compensation system rewards employees for outperforming one another. If that's true, helping a colleague literally costs you money. Perhaps nobody knows who owns the task. Then everyone waits for someone else. Perhaps employees believe management will take credit for their ideas. Perhaps speaking up has been punished in the past. In any of those cases the behavior may be coming from the structure of the interaction. Put different people in the same structure and you may get the same result. That's the lesson on the right. And I want to be careful here. This doesn't mean the people have no responsibility. They still make choices. It means we shouldn't automatically diagnose a person problem when there may be a game problem. Why does that matter? Because the fix is in a different place. A pep talk about teamwork won't beat a pay system that punishes teamwork. One of the most powerful things game theory gives us is the ability to step back from individual behavior. You examine the environment producing it. Here's a question for you. Who in your life have you labeled as difficult? What would you see if you looked at their incentives instead?
