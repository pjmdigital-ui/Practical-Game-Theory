Internomics · Module 1 — See the Game

This is one of the most important questions you can ask in any strategic interaction. What happens if we don't agree? Most of us treat that as a backup plan. Something to think about only if the talk goes badly. Look at the first card. Your alternatives don't just matter after a negotiation fails. They change the negotiation itself. If you know you have a decent option elsewhere you carry yourself differently. You can afford to say no. You don't need to grab the first offer. And the other side can often sense that. The same is true in reverse. Their alternatives shape how they treat you. That's why the middle card matters. Bargaining theory treats outside options as an important part of the strategic environment. They're not an afterthought. They're part of the board. But here's the honest caveat on the third card. Their effect depends on the specific structure of the game. There's no simple rule that the person with the better alternative always wins. Sometimes a weaker alternative still holds up fine because the other side needs the deal badly too. Sometimes timing or information matters more. So treat alternatives as something to map, not a scoreboard. Here's the common mistake. People think hard about their own fallback and never ask about the other person's. That's only half the map. So ask both sides of the question. What happens to me if there's no agreement? What happens to them? Sometimes that question changes everything. Next let's turn that into a question about power.
