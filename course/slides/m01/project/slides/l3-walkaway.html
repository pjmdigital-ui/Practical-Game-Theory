Two questions about power

What happens if we don't agree?

Outside options shape the negotiation itself, not just what happens after it fails. Their effect depends on the game: there's no rule that the better alternative always wins.

Who needs whom more?

Power often comes from the structure of dependence and alternatives. That isn't a persuasion tactic. It's a property of the game.

Internomics · Module 1 — See the Game

Here are two of the most important questions you can ask in any strategic interaction. First: what happens if we don't agree? Your alternatives don't just matter after a negotiation fails. They change the negotiation itself. Bargaining theory treats outside options as part of the strategic environment, but their effect depends on the structure of the game, not on a simple rule that the person with the better alternative always wins. Second: who needs whom more? You need your supplier, your supplier needs your business. But maybe one of you has more alternatives. If I have no alternative and you have five, my position is very different than if I had five and you had none. That's why power isn't simply something one person possesses. It's a property of the game.
