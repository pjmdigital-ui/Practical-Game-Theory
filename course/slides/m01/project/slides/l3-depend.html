Internomics · Module 1 — See the Game

Here's another way to look at the same thing. Ask yourself one question. Who needs whom more? Look at the three cards. You need your employee. Your employee needs the job. You need your supplier. Your supplier needs your business. You want the relationship. The other person wants the relationship too. Almost every important relationship runs both ways like this. Need on each side. But the need is rarely equal. Perhaps one person has more alternatives than the other. That doesn't automatically decide the outcome. It does change the strategic environment. Now look at the dark panel. If I have no alternative and you have five, my position is very different than if I had five and you had none. Same people. Same conversation. Different game. This is why I want you to rethink the word "power." We usually talk about power as something a person has. Like a trait. Like charisma or a big title. But often power comes from the structure of dependence and alternatives. It sits in the situation more than in the person. That's the line in orange. It isn't a persuasion tactic. It's a property of the game. Here's why that's useful. If power lives in the structure you can work on the structure. You can build alternatives. You can reduce how much you depend on one person. You can notice how much they depend on you. Here's the misunderstanding to watch for. A confident tone doesn't create power. And a quiet person isn't automatically weak. So think about one relationship at work. Who needs whom more? Who has more alternatives? Let's put alternatives and dependence together in a story.
