Internomics · Module 1 — See the Game

I want to stay on this point for a moment because it trips people up. A game doesn't have to involve a winner and a loser. Look at the cards. Cooperation is a game. Coordination is a game. So is negotiation. So is a friendship. So is a business partnership. So is a family making a decision together. In every one of these the outcome depends on more than one person's choices. That's all it takes. The economist Thomas Schelling made this point central to his work. He emphasized that many real-world interactions contain both conflicting and common interests. They're rarely purely competitive. Two people in a friendship want the friendship to last. They may still want different things on a given Saturday night. Two business partners want the company to win. They may still disagree about how. Why does this matter so much? Because of how we're going to use this material. We're not using game theory as a cynical philosophy that says everyone is out to beat you. If you walk away from this course treating your spouse like an opponent you've missed the point. The aim is to see the structure clearly. Often that makes cooperation easier, not harder. Here's a question for you. Pick the most important relationship in your life. Where are your interests shared? Where do they pull apart a little? Both answers are part of the game. Now let's name the premise that sits underneath all of this.
