Research spotlight · Liberman, Samuels & Ross (2004)

The "Wall Street Game" vs. the "Community Game"

What it shows

Same Prisoner's Dilemma. Different name. The label changed cooperation more than observers expected. Reputations predicted poorly. The game people think they're in shapes how they play.

What it doesn't prove

That consciously mapping a game leads to optimal decisions. Or that the game is "only perception." A real structure sits underneath. Our readings of it can be right or wrong.

Internomics · Module 1 — See the Game

Here's our first research spotlight for this module. In 2004 Liberman, Samuels and Ross ran a study with a simple twist. Participants played essentially the same Prisoner's Dilemma. The participants included American college students. They also included Israeli pilots and instructors. The only thing the researchers changed was the name of the game. Some people were told they were playing the "Wall Street Game." Others were told they were playing the "Community Game." Same rules. Same payoffs. Different label. Here's what they found. The label had a substantial effect on whether people cooperated or defected. That effect was larger than observers expected. And there was a second finding I find just as striking. Judgments of each participant's reputation did a poor job of predicting who would cooperate. In other words the name of the game told you more than the person's reputation did. What does that suggest for real conversations? People's construal of the situation matters. That means what kind of game they believe they're in. It can change how they behave inside it. Two people can face nearly identical incentives and act very differently because they read the interaction differently. So how you identify the game matters. Now here's what it doesn't prove. It doesn't show that consciously mapping a game leads to optimal decisions. And it doesn't mean the game is only perception. There's still a real strategic structure underneath. Our interpretations of it can be accurate or inaccurate. Here's a question for you. When you walk into a meeting, what name are you silently giving the game? Let's look at the second study.
