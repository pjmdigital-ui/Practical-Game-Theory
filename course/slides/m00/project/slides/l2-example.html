Example · Asking for a raise

Before you say a word

The situation

You walk into your boss's office to ask for a raise.

What we usually do

Think about the words. How much to ask for. How to show our value. Which objections to expect.

The lesson

All of that matters. But the outcome depends on two people's decisions. Not just yours.

Internomics · Module 0 — Orientation

Here's the example the book opens with. Imagine you walk into your boss's office to ask for a raise. Before you say anything, what are you thinking about? If you're like most people, you're thinking about the words you're going to use. Maybe you're deciding how much to ask for. Maybe you're working out how to demonstrate your value. Maybe you're trying to anticipate the objections your boss will raise. All of that matters. I'm not going to tell you to skip it. But there's another way to look at this interaction. Your request doesn't exist in isolation. Your boss hears it and makes a decision. Your boss might agree. Your boss might reject it. They might ask for more information. They might make a counteroffer. They might delay the conversation or consult someone else. They might even test how badly you want the raise. Your move changes their decision problem. And their response changes yours. That's the essence of strategic interaction. So the outcome of this conversation isn't decided by your words alone. It depends on two people's decisions. Probably more than two. Here's the misunderstanding I want to head off. Calling this a game doesn't mean you're trying to beat your boss. It's a game in the game-theoretic sense. It's a situation where the outcome depends at least partly on the decisions of more than one person. You can both win here. In fact the best outcomes usually work for both of you. So before you rehearse your opening line, there's a set of questions to ask first. Let's look at them.
