Internomics · Module 0 — Orientation

One distinction before we go on. Game theory may help you understand why someone behaved badly. Maybe the incentives made it attractive. Maybe the information they had made mistrust rational. Understanding that doesn't mean excusing it. Understanding someone's incentives doesn't mean assuming their behavior is justified. Someone can have a perfectly understandable incentive and still make a bad decision. Someone can value something unhealthy. Someone can chase short-term gratification at enormous long-term cost. Someone can knowingly harm another person because they value something else more. People can lie. They can betray. They can exploit. Game theory doesn't remove moral responsibility from any of that. What it adds is another level of analysis. It asks what conditions made this behavior attractive. It asks what made it sustainable. It asks what makes it so hard to change. You can condemn an action and still understand its logic. In fact understanding the logic often helps you prevent it from happening again. Here's a way to practice this. When someone does something that frustrates you, your first question is usually "Why would they do that?" Try a different question. Ask "What payoff would make that behavior make sense?" That doesn't mean you'll agree with them. It doesn't mean you'll excuse them. It simply means you've stopped assuming your preferences are the universal standard for rational behavior. You're trying to see the game from their side. Think of someone whose behavior you've written off as simply wrong. What conditions might have made it attractive to them?
