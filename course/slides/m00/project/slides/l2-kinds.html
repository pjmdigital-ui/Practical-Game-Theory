Internomics · Module 0 — Orientation

A game doesn't have to be competitive. That's the most important thing to understand about the word. Families are games in this sense. So are friendships. So are teams and business partnerships. Some games are cooperative. Everyone gains by working together. Some are competitive. One side's gain really is the other side's loss. But most real-world games are mixed. We share some interests and not others. Thomas Schelling won the Nobel Prize in economics for work in this area. He emphasized exactly this point. Many real interactions contain both conflicting and common interests. They're rarely pure competition. The book gives a simple example. Imagine two business partners. They both want the company to succeed. So are they cooperating? Yes. But they might also disagree over how much each should be paid. They might disagree over who has decision-making authority. They might disagree over how much money to reinvest. That's a mixed game. Shared goals and real tension at the same time. And then there are coordination games. Everyone wants to end up in the same place. The problem is that nobody is sure where the others are going. We'll spend a whole module on those later. Here's why this matters. If you assume every game is a battle, you'll treat people like opponents. You'll miss the shared interests that could help you both. So think about a conflict you're in right now. Is it really pure competition? Or is it a mixed game where you both want some of the same things? Now let's talk about the word "player."
