Internomics · Module 0 — Orientation

When most people hear the words game theory, they picture chess or poker. Maybe business competition. Maybe two countries threatening each other. Those are certainly games. But game theory is much broader than that. At its most basic level it studies strategic interaction. That means situations where the choices of one decision-maker affect the outcomes available to others. So here's the definition we'll use all course long. A game is any situation where the outcome depends at least partly on the decisions of more than one person. That's it. The formal framework has four parts, and you see them on the screen. There are players. Those are the decision-makers. There are strategies. Those are the choices each player can make. There are payoffs. That's what each player prefers or values. And there are outcomes. That's what happens when everyone's choices combine. Notice what's missing from that definition. There's no winner and loser required. A game can involve cooperation. It can involve negotiation or compromise. It can be a friendship or a family decision. And you won't need any math in this course. Let me give you a quick way to test it. Your partner and you deciding where to go for dinner is a game. The outcome depends on both of your choices. Writing in your private diary is not a game. Nobody else's choice is involved. Here's the common misunderstanding. People hear "game" and think it means competition or trickery. It doesn't. It just means your outcome depends on someone else's decision too. And that opens up a much bigger range of games than most people expect.
