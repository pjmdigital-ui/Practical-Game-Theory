Internomics · Module 0 — Orientation

Every module has a research spotlight. And every spotlight has two sides. What the research shows. And what it does not prove. I'll always give you both. Here's the first one. Game theory gives us a formal language for strategic interaction. Players have choices and preferences. The outcome depends on how those choices interact. Thomas Schelling received the 2005 Nobel Prize in economics. He emphasized that most real-world interactions contain both conflicting and common interests. They're rarely purely competitive. Think back to the two business partners from earlier. They both want the company to succeed. They also disagree about pay and authority and reinvestment. That's what Schelling was pointing at. Shared interests and opposed interests at the same time. Why does this matter for us? Because it's the reason we don't treat the other person as an opponent by default. If most games are mixed, there's almost always something to build on. Now here's what it doesn't prove. Game theory was never created as a theory of better communication. And human beings are messier than the perfectly rational players in many mathematical models. So in this course research illustrates our thinking. It disciplines our thinking. It does not promise that any technique will work on any particular person. I want you to hold me to that standard. If I ever sound like I'm promising a guaranteed result, be skeptical.
