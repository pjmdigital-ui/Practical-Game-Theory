Research spotlight · Costa-Gomes & Weizsäcker

People don't always act on their own beliefs

What it shows

In 14 one-shot games people also stated what they expected opponents to do. On average they failed to best respond to their own stated beliefs in almost half the games.

What it suggests

Beliefs matter. But people don't always turn beliefs into consistent action. That includes you.

What it doesn't prove

That beliefs are irrelevant or people are random. Nor that knowing a belief lets you reliably predict behavior.

Internomics · Module 6 — Map Information & Beliefs

The second spotlight comes from Costa-Gomes and Weizsäcker. It was published in the Review of Economic Studies. Participants played fourteen one-shot strategic games. Separately they reported what they expected their opponents to do. Now think about what you'd predict. If people simply chose the best response to their own stated beliefs, the two should line up. If I say I expect you to go left, I should pick whatever works best against left. They didn't line up. On average participants failed to best respond to their own stated beliefs in almost half the games. And here's an interesting twist. Their stated beliefs often reflected deeper strategic reasoning than their actual choices did. They could think it through on paper. Their moves didn't always follow. So what does that suggest for us? Beliefs matter. But people don't always translate beliefs into consistent action. Knowing what someone believes is useful. It isn't a crystal ball. And that includes you. Your own stated beliefs may not be what's actually driving your moves. You might say you believe your colleague is on your side and still act guarded around them. Now the card on the right. This study doesn't show that beliefs are irrelevant. It doesn't show that people are random. And it doesn't support the idea that correctly identifying someone's belief lets you reliably predict their behavior. One more caution comes from related work by Rutström and Wilcox. They found that asking people for their beliefs with incentives could alter early play in one repeated game under some conditions. Even measuring beliefs can affect the game. So here's a question for you. Where do your actions not match what you say you believe? Now let's turn all of this into a tool.
