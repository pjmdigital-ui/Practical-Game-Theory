Research spotlight · Costa-Gomes & Weizsäcker

People don't always act on their own beliefs

What it shows

Across 14 one-shot games, participants also stated what they expected opponents to do. On average, they failed to best respond to their own stated beliefs in almost half the games.

What it doesn't prove

That beliefs are irrelevant, or that people are random. Nor that knowing someone's belief lets you reliably predict their behavior.

Internomics · Module 6 — Map Information & Beliefs

The second spotlight comes from Costa-Gomes and Weizsäcker, in the Review of Economic Studies. Participants played fourteen one-shot strategic games and separately reported what they expected their opponents to do. If people simply chose the best response to their own stated beliefs, the two should line up. They didn't. On average, participants failed to best respond to their own stated beliefs in almost half the games, and their stated beliefs often reflected deeper strategic reasoning than their actual choices. So beliefs matter, but people don't always turn beliefs into consistent action. Knowing what someone believes is useful. It isn't a crystal ball. And that includes you: your own stated beliefs may not be what's actually driving your moves. One more caution from related work by Rutström and Wilcox: asking people for their beliefs with incentives could alter early play in one repeated game under some conditions. Even measuring beliefs can affect the game.
