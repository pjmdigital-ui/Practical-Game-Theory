Internomics · Module 6 — Map Information & Beliefs

This distinction is important. When you don't know what someone is thinking, it's tempting to assume they're hiding something. Our minds go there fast. But look at the five cards. There are at least five reasons you might not know what someone is thinking. Sometimes they are deliberately withholding information. That does happen. But sometimes they don't know themselves. They haven't made up their mind yet. Sometimes the information simply isn't available. Nobody has it yet. Sometimes you just haven't asked. That's the simplest gap of all and the easiest one to close. And sometimes you've interpreted ambiguous evidence incorrectly. You saw something real and drew the wrong conclusion from it. Only the first of those five involves anyone hiding anything. Strategic thinking doesn't automatically assume deception. It asks a much healthier question. That's the dark card. What information do I actually have? And how confident should I be in the conclusions I'm drawing from it? Here's why this matters so much. If you jump to deception, you act like someone who's being deceived. You get guarded. You get suspicious. You start testing people. And they feel it. Now you've changed their behavior with a belief that might have been wrong from the start. That's the self-fulfilling loop from the last lesson again. Here's a quick question for you. Think of the last time you assumed someone was keeping something from you. Which of these five was it really? If you're honest, it may not have been the first one. That one question will save you from a lot of conflicts you'd otherwise start yourself. So what do you do instead? You treat your read as a hypothesis.
