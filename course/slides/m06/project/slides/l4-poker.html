Internomics · Module 6 — Map Information & Beliefs

Poker makes all of this intuitive. At a poker table you don't simply ask what cards your opponent has. You don't know. You can't know. So you ask a different set of questions. Look at the three cards. First you ask what they think I have. Then you ask what they think I think they have. And then you ask what they think I think they're going to do. That's strategic reasoning under uncertainty. And notice how different this is from the early modules. We aren't simply predicting what they'll do. We're predicting what they'll do based on what they believe. That's a whole new layer. Now look at the line at the bottom. This is why bluffing is possible. If you can convince someone that you have a strong hand when you don't, you've changed their beliefs. Their behavior changes. And because their behavior changes, your outcome changes. But there's a deeper point here. A bluff doesn't work because the statement is true. It works because the other player believes it. Or at least believes it enough to act differently. That distinction between truth and belief is central to strategic communication. It's the same distinction we started this module with. You see bluffs outside of poker all the time. A negotiator who acts like they're ready to walk. A competitor who announces a product that isn't built yet. Some of those are real and some aren't. What matters in the moment is whether the other side believes it enough to change course. Here's a question for you. When was the last time you changed your plan because of what you believed someone else was holding? Of course beliefs can be wrong. Let's look at what happens then.
