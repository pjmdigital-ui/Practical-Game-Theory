Internomics · Module 6 — Map Information & Beliefs

Here's the formal name for what we just saw. Information asymmetry simply means one player knows something relevant that another player doesn't. And once you start looking for it you see it everywhere. A doctor knows more about medicine than the patient. A mechanic knows more about cars than the customer. A lawyer knows more about the legal system than the client. A salesperson may know more about the product than the buyer. An employee knows more about their own effort than their manager does. And a child knows a lot more about what they did five minutes ago than their parent does. It sounds like a joke. But it has the same structure as the others. Notice something about this list. None of these relationships is a battle. Most of them are partnerships. The doctor wants you healthy. The employee wants to do good work. But in every one of them somebody is making decisions with less information than the other person has. And that changes the game. It changes what questions make sense. It changes how much weight you give to what you're told. It changes what the better informed person owes the other one. Here's a quick exercise. Pick one relationship from your own week. Ask which side of the gap you're on. Are you the doctor or the patient in that relationship? Most of us are on both sides in different parts of our lives. So the strategic question becomes this. How do you make decisions when someone else knows something you don't? The obvious answer is to ask them. Let's see why that's harder than it sounds.
