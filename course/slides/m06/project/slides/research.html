Research spotlight · Tadelis & Zettelmeyer

Same cars, different information

What it shows

A field experiment in wholesale car auctions randomly disclosed quality information. Good and bad news could both raise competition and revenue, because buyers were better matched to cars.

What it doesn't prove

That more information always improves outcomes. The effect depends on the game. It's evidence from one market setting, not a rule that disclosure always pays.

Internomics · Module 6 — Map Information & Beliefs

Here's our first research spotlight. Tadelis and Zettelmeyer ran a large-scale field experiment in wholesale car auctions, published in the American Economic Review. They randomly disclosed quality information about some vehicles. What they found surprises people: both good news and bad news could increase competition and revenue. Why? Because buyers differed in which cars suited them, and better information improved the matching between buyers and vehicles. The cars didn't change. The buyers didn't change. The information changed, and so the game changed. What it suggests for us: disclosing an unflattering fact isn't automatically a loss. It can help the right counterpart find you. And what it doesn't prove: it doesn't show that more information always improves outcomes. The effect depends on the game, and this is evidence from a specific market, not a universal rule that disclosure always pays.
