Higher-order beliefs

Sometimes you respond to what you think they know about what you know.

One level: I believe you think I'm bluffing.

Two levels: You believe I think you believe I'm bluffing.

Three levels: I believe you believe I think you're bluffing.

Internomics · Module 6 — Map Information & Beliefs

Now we get into something that can become surprisingly deep. You don't just have beliefs about the other person. You may have beliefs about what they believe about you. Look at the first line. I believe you think I'm bluffing. That's already a belief about a belief. But perhaps it goes further. You believe I think you believe I'm bluffing. And perhaps I believe you believe I think you're bluffing. It can continue indefinitely. Game theorists call these higher-order beliefs. They also call them belief hierarchies. Graduate game theory treats them as a formal part of incomplete-information models. We don't need to take the mathematics very far. The practical lesson is enough. It's the headline. Sometimes you're not responding to what someone knows. You're responding to what you think they know about what you know. That's a very different kind of interaction. Here's an everyday version. Imagine you think your manager believes you think she's micromanaging you. So you go out of your way to look relaxed about her check-ins. She notices you're acting a little stiff. Now she wonders what you think of her. Nobody said a word about micromanaging. And yet three layers of belief are shaping the conversation. Here's the misunderstanding to avoid. People think this kind of reasoning is overthinking. Sometimes it is. But in high-stakes moments it's often exactly what's driving the behavior. If you ignore it you'll misread people. Ask yourself this. In your most important relationship at work, what do you think they believe about you? The clearest place to see all this is a poker table. Let's go there.
