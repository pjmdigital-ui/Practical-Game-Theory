A belief can help create the outcome it fears

The situation

Internomics · Module 6 — Map Information & Beliefs

Here's the classic example. It's the bank run. Imagine you have money in a bank. You believe the bank may fail. You don't know whether that's true. Maybe it's perfectly healthy. But you also know something else. If enough other depositors withdraw their money, the bank could actually fail. So what do you do? You go to the bank and withdraw your money. That's the situation on the left. Now look at the middle. Other people see you doing it. They become nervous. They withdraw theirs. More people see those withdrawals. They become even more nervous. And suddenly a belief about what might happen helps create the very outcome people feared. That's the strategic power of beliefs. Here's the lesson on the right. The outcome isn't determined only by the bank's underlying financial condition. It's also affected by what depositors believe other depositors are going to do. This is one reason bank runs get studied as coordination problems and as dynamic games with imperfect information. MIT's game theory curriculum uses them as an example. Now notice something about your own decision in that story. You weren't really reacting to the bank. You were reacting to your belief about other people. Even if you thought the bank was fine, you might still withdraw if you thought everyone else was about to. That's a very different kind of reasoning. Ask yourself where this shows up in your world. Where are you acting on what you think others will do rather than on the facts? Let's slow the spiral down and look at each step.
