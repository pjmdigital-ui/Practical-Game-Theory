Common knowledge: we both know it. And we both know that we both know it.

Internomics · Module 6 — Map Information & Beliefs

There's another layer we need to introduce. Suppose you and I both know the meeting starts at nine. That's one level of knowledge. I know it. You know it. But do I know that you know it? And do you know that I know that you know it? That may sound ridiculous in everyday life. But in strategic situations it can matter enormously. Game theory calls this kind of recursively shared awareness common knowledge. You don't need the formal definition. Think of it like the line at the bottom. We both know something. And we both know that we both know it. And so on. Here's why it matters. Look at the left card. Imagine you're in a room with ten people. Someone announces that at exactly noon everyone is going to leave the building. Everyone hears it. And everyone sees that everyone heard it. Coordination is easy. Now look at the right card. Imagine only half the people hear it. Those who heard it know what's supposed to happen. But they don't know whether everyone else heard it. Should they get up at noon? Will they be the only ones walking out? Now coordination becomes difficult. Here's the subtle part. The problem isn't necessarily lack of information. Half the room has the information. The problem is lack of shared knowledge that the information is shared. This is the mistake behind so many workplace mix-ups. A team lead tells three of six people that Friday's meeting is canceled. The other three show up. The lead feels they communicated. They did. They just didn't make it common knowledge. Ask yourself where you rely on "I told them" without knowing that everyone knows.
