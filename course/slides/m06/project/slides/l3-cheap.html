Here's a concept that becomes very important when we get to communication. It's called cheap talk. In formal game theory cheap talk is communication that doesn't directly change the material payoffs of the game. Saying something doesn't cost you anything by itself. And it doesn't bind you to anything. MIT teaches it as its own framework for thinking about communication. Now here's where people go wrong. They hear the word cheap and assume it means worthless. Despite the name, cheap talk isn't necessarily useless. Words can be extremely valuable. Messages can carry meaningful information even when sending them is costless. That's especially true when the sender's interests are sufficiently aligned with the receiver's. Think about it this way. If we both want the same thing, I have no reason to mislead you. And you have good reason to listen. A coworker on your own team tells you the client meeting moved to three o'clock. That's cheap talk. It cost nothing to say. But you'd be foolish to ignore it, because you both want to be in that meeting at the right time. Now flip it. If our interests pull apart, my free words carry less weight. Not zero. Just less. So the strategic value of words depends partly on what incentives the speaker has to say them. Which brings us to the question in the dark card. Why should I believe you? Hold on to that question.
