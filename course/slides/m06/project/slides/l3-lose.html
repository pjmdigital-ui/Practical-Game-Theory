Here's the practical tool from the last slide. It's one question. What does this person have to lose by making this claim? I want you to get so used to asking it that it becomes automatic. If the answer is nothing, be more cautious about treating the statement as strong evidence. Notice I didn't say ignore it. I said be more cautious about treating it as strong evidence. Those are very different things. Let's try it on the example on the right. A coworker says, "Trust me. This vendor is the best option." Now run the question. What does your coworker have to lose by saying that? Maybe nothing at all. If the vendor disappoints, it's your project that suffers. Then the claim is fairly cheap. But maybe your coworker's name goes on the recommendation. Maybe they'll have to manage the vendor for the next year. Maybe they've told the whole team. Now they have something at stake if they're wrong. The same sentence just got heavier. Here's what I like about this question. It's not cynical. You aren't assuming the person is lying. You're just asking how much weight their words can bear. And it works in both directions. When you want someone to believe you, ask what you're putting at stake. If the honest answer is nothing, don't be surprised when they hold back. Think of one claim someone made to you this week that you simply accepted. What did they have to lose?
