This is where we meet one of the most important ideas in game theory. It's signaling. A signal is an action or a piece of information that can help another player infer something they don't directly observe. That's the definition at the top. Education is the classic example in economics. Look at the three cards. What's hidden is the candidate's underlying ability. The employer can't see it directly. The signal is a degree. A degree can provide information about the candidate. And why can it work? Michael Spence's classic job-market signaling model showed how education can function as a signal. It works when workers have different abilities and the cost of getting the education differs across types. In plain terms, the degree is harder to get for some people than for others. That difference is what lets it carry information. If it cost everyone exactly the same, it would tell the employer much less. Signaling has since become a major branch of game theory and information economics. MIT courses treat it as its own topic alongside incomplete information. Now here's the mistake I want you to avoid. Don't think a signal is proof. It isn't. A signal is evidence. And its value depends on how informative it actually is. A degree tells you something. It doesn't tell you everything. Someone without one may be brilliant. Someone with one may not be. Think about the signals you send every day. Your punctuality. Your follow-through. The way you prepare for a meeting.
