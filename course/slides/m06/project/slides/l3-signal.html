This is where we meet one of the most important ideas in game theory: signaling. A signal is an action or a piece of information that can help another player infer something they don't directly observe. Education is the classic example in economics. An employer can't directly observe a candidate's underlying ability. A degree can provide information about it. Michael Spence's job-market signaling model showed how education can work as a signal when workers have different abilities and the cost of getting that education differs across types. Signaling has since become a major branch of game theory and information economics. But don't make the mistake of thinking a signal is proof. It isn't. A signal is evidence, and its value depends on how informative it actually is.
