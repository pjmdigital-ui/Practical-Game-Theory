Remember Schelling's meeting example from Module Five? You and I agree to meet tomorrow. But we don't say where. That's the problem on the left. You might choose Grand Central. I might choose Times Square. Both are reasonable choices. But we're not coordinated. We spend the afternoon in different places. Now look at the right card. Suppose something makes one location particularly salient. It stands out. It may become what game theorists call a focal point. And this is where beliefs and common knowledge interact. We may each think, "They'll probably choose Grand Central." And because we both think that, we both choose it. Notice what solved the problem. It wasn't more information about the city. It was a shared expectation. Each of us predicted what the other would predict. The shared expectation helped create the coordination. Now look at the row along the bottom. This is one reason rituals matter strategically. It's why conventions matter. It's why public announcements and visible commitments matter. They create shared awareness about what people expect others to do. Think about a team that always reviews priorities first thing on Monday. Nobody has to wonder whether everyone else will show up ready. The ritual makes it common knowledge. Or think about a public commitment made in front of the whole team. It doesn't just inform people. It tells everyone that everyone else heard it too. So here's a question for you. Where in your work or family do people keep missing each other? Could a simple ritual or a public statement create the shared expectation you're missing? Now let's go back to a story about how beliefs can spiral.
