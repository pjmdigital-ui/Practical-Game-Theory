The late report

What happened: Jordan's quarterly report went out two days late. Dana's director raised it in front of other managers.

Earlier: Dana said "the director is watching this one" in a hallway chat Jordan missed. She skimmed his "Still waiting on finance's numbers" as routine.

Friday: Dana writes to Jordan. "We need to talk Monday about ownership."

Jordan's view: He rebuilt the model late Thursday after finance delivered. He thinks she just wants someone to blame.

Dana's view: Jordan didn't flag the risk because he doesn't take the director's priorities seriously.

Internomics · Module 6 — Map Information & Beliefs

Let's practice. Here's the scenario. Dana manages a five-person analytics team. On Friday the quarterly report Jordan owned went out two days late. Dana's director asked her about it in front of other managers. That stung. Now go back to earlier in the week. On Monday Dana had told the team the director was watching this one closely. But she said it in a quick hallway exchange. Jordan wasn't part of it. Mid-week Jordan sent a message. It said, "Still waiting on finance's numbers." Dana skimmed it on her phone and read it as a routine status update. On Friday afternoon Dana writes to Jordan. "We need to talk Monday about ownership." Now look at Jordan's side. He stayed late Thursday rebuilding the model after finance finally delivered. He reads Dana's message as a sign she thinks he's careless. Over the weekend he tells a friend, "She already knows finance was late. She just wants someone to blame." And here's Dana's side. She believes Jordan didn't flag the risk because he doesn't take the director's priorities seriously. Notice that neither of them is a villain. Each one is acting sensibly given what they know and believe. And yet they're heading into Monday on a collision course. Before we go to the questions, ask yourself which person you'd naturally side with. That's often a clue about which mistakes you tend to make yourself. Let's put the questions up.
