Internomics · Module 6 — Map Information & Beliefs

So the obvious advice is this. If you don't know something, just ask. It sounds easy. And sometimes it works perfectly well. But here's the problem. The other person gets to decide what to tell you. And they may have an incentive to give you information that benefits them. This is the moment communication becomes strategic. Let's go back to the used car. You ask the seller a direct question. Has this car ever had any major problems? The seller says no. Now look at what just happened. You've received information. You asked and you got an answer. Most people would feel like they've done their homework. But should you automatically believe it? Not necessarily. Think about it from the seller's side. If the car is fine, he says no. If the car has a hidden problem and he wants to sell it, he might also say no. Both kinds of seller can give you exactly the same answer. So the answer alone doesn't tell you which seller you're talking to. That doesn't mean the seller is lying. Plenty of sellers are honest. It means the word "no" can't carry the whole weight by itself. The statement has to be evaluated in the context of the game. Here's the misunderstanding to watch for. People think the opposite of naive trust is suspicion. It isn't. The opposite of naive trust is evaluation. You don't dismiss the answer. You weigh it. So how do you weigh it?
