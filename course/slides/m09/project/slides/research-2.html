Research spotlight · Aoyagi, Bhaskar & Fréchette

Noise makes leniency valuable

What it shows

Players sustained cooperation with perfect monitoring and with noisy monitoring. Under noise they used more complex and more lenient strategies. That limited the damage from errors.

What it doesn't prove

"Always forgive." A too-forgiving strategy can make exploitation profitable. Nor do Axelrod's tournaments make Tit for Tat best everywhere.

What it suggests: When misunderstandings are possible, a completely unforgiving strategy is fragile. Some tolerance and a path to repair can be strategically valuable.

Internomics · Module 9 — The Shadow of the Future

The second study is about noise. Here's the point. When people can't see clearly what happened, a bit of leniency helps keep cooperation alive. Masaki Aoyagi, V. Bhaskar and Guillaume Fréchette ran repeated Prisoner's Dilemma experiments under different kinds of monitoring. In one condition players could see exactly what had happened. In others past actions were observed imperfectly. People sustained cooperation in all three conditions. But their strategies changed when monitoring got noisy. Under imperfect monitoring players used strategies that were more complex and more lenient. That helped limit the damage from observational errors. That's a deeply useful finding. It tells us something about real relationships. When misunderstandings are possible, a completely unforgiving strategy can be fragile. If every apparent violation brings permanent retaliation, one mistake can destroy the relationship. Think back to the feud. That's what happens with zero leniency in a noisy world. Some tolerance and a path to repair can be strategically valuable. But here's what the study doesn't prove. It isn't evidence for "always forgive." Leniency helps in noisy settings. Exploitation is still possible. A strategy that's too forgiving can make exploitation profitable. And the same caution applies to Axelrod's tournaments. They don't show that Tit for Tat is the best strategy everywhere. Its success depended on the mix of strategies. It depended on the payoffs. And it depended on how errors were handled. So here's a question. How noisy are your most important relationships? How much leniency does that call for? Now let's turn all of this into a tool you can use.
