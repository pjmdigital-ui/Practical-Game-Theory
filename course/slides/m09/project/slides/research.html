Research spotlight · Dal Bó

A longer future reduced opportunism

What it shows

Pedro Dal Bó had people play repeated Prisoner's Dilemma games in the lab. He varied the chance of another round. A longer expected future significantly reduced opportunistic behavior.

What it doesn't prove

That repetition automatically produces cooperation. Later work with Guillaume Fréchette found the conditions more demanding. And lab games don't predict any one person.

What it suggests: When a next round becomes more likely, today's behavior carries more future consequences. That can make cooperation more attractive.

Internomics · Module 9 — The Shadow of the Future

Here's the point of this study. The length of the shadow really does change behavior. Pedro Dal Bó tested the idea in the laboratory. Participants played repeated Prisoner's Dilemma games. He varied the probability that another round would occur. That let him change how long the shadow of the future was expected to be. The result was clear. A longer expected future significantly reduced opportunistic behavior. The effect broadly followed the direction repeated-game theory predicts. That matters because it lets us make a more precise claim. The claim isn't just "people are nicer when they know they'll see you again." The strategic claim is this. When future interaction becomes more likely, the future consequences of current behavior matter more. And that can make cooperation more attractive. Notice the word "can." It's doing a lot of work. Here's what the study doesn't prove. It doesn't show that repeated interaction automatically produces cooperation. Later work by Dal Bó and Guillaume Fréchette looked at how cooperation develops as people gain experience. They found that cooperation can prevail. But the conditions were more demanding than cooperation simply being possible in theory. Their survey of the experimental literature reached a similar conclusion. High cooperation tends to emerge when cooperative strategies are robust to strategic uncertainty. That's uncertainty about what the other player is going to do. And remember these are laboratory games. They don't predict what any particular person will do. Here's a question for you. Where could you make the next round more likely or more visible?
