Research spotlight · Dal Bó

A longer future reduced opportunism

What it shows

Pedro Dal Bó had people play repeated Prisoner's Dilemma games in the lab while varying the chance of another round. A longer expected future significantly reduced opportunistic behavior.

What it doesn't prove

That repetition automatically produces cooperation. Later work with Guillaume Fréchette found the conditions more demanding. And lab games don't predict any one person.

Internomics · Module 9 — The Shadow of the Future

Pedro Dal Bó tested this idea in the lab. Participants played repeated Prisoner's Dilemma games, and he varied the probability that another round would occur, which changed how long the shadow of the future was expected to be. A longer expected future significantly reduced opportunistic behavior, broadly in the direction repeated-game theory predicts. So the claim isn't just "people are nicer when they'll see you again." It's that when future interaction becomes more likely, the future consequences of present behavior matter more, and that can make cooperation more attractive. Notice the word "can." Later work by Dal Bó and Guillaume Fréchette found cooperation can prevail as people gain experience, but the conditions were more demanding than cooperation simply being possible in theory. Their survey of the experiments found high cooperation tends to emerge when cooperative strategies are robust to strategic uncertainty. And these are laboratory games. They don't predict any particular person.
