Strategic thinking sometimes means resisting a short-term win.

Internomics · Module 9 — The Shadow of the Future

This is one of the most important practical lessons of repeated-game thinking. Winning the round can lose the game. Suppose you're arguing with your spouse. At some point they told you something painful and deeply personal. They trusted you with it. Now in the middle of the argument you realize you could use that against them right now. And it would work. You could end the argument. You could embarrass them. You could make them stop talking. Maybe you'd "win." But what game exists tomorrow? Perhaps they learn that vulnerability is dangerous with you. So next time they hide something. They disclose less. They trust less. The immediate move succeeded. The repeated-game strategy failed. Look at how much the win actually cost. It didn't cost one argument. It cost every future conversation where they might have told you the truth. That's a terrible trade. And it's an easy one to make in the heat of the moment. That's exactly when the short-term payoff looks biggest. This is why strategic thinking sometimes requires resisting a short-term win. The urge to win is strongest right when the future is hardest to see. Here's the common misunderstanding. People think a fair fight just means not raising your voice. But in a repeated game the deeper question is what your tactics teach. A tactic that works once and poisons trust is a bad tactic. So here's a question for you. What's the move you reach for when you want to win an argument? And what is it teaching the other person? Carry this lesson in one question.
