Internomics · Module 9 — The Shadow of the Future

Here's the point. In a repeated game the same action can mean completely different things. It depends on what came before it. Suppose someone cancels dinner with you. Look at the left card. If they've been reliable for ten years you might think, "Something must have come up." You don't feel hurt. You might even worry about them. Now look at the right card. If they've cancelled the last six times you may think, "Here we go again." Same action. Different history. Different belief. And a different likely response. That's another way repetition changes communication. Actions don't arrive alone. They arrive attached to everything that came before them. This works in your favor and against you. If you've built a long record of reliability, people will read your mistakes generously. They'll assume it was noise. That's one of the quiet rewards of a good track record. It buys you room for honest errors. But if you've built a pattern of letting people down, even your honest mistakes will look like more of the same. People won't give you the benefit of the doubt. Their reading isn't unfair. It's just based on the evidence you gave them. This also helps you tell a mistake from a pattern. One cancellation inside ten years of reliability is probably noise. Six in a row is data. Here's the common misunderstanding. People expect to be judged on this one action alone. They rarely are. So here's a question. When people in your life see your next move, what history will it arrive attached to? Let's pull this lesson together.
