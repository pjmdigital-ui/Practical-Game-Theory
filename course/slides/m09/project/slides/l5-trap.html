Internomics · Module 9 — The Shadow of the Future

A good reputation can create value. Here's the point. It can also create constraints. Suppose you've built your identity around "I never back down." Now every conflict creates pressure to keep that reputation. That pressure is there even when backing down would be wise. Or suppose your identity is "I'm always the nice one." Now asserting a legitimate boundary may feel inconsistent with who you've been. Reputation changes how other people expect you to behave. But sometimes it also changes how you feel obligated to behave. You become a prisoner of your own track record. This connects back to Module Four. There we learned that predictability can either create trust or make you exploitable. Reputation is essentially a form of strategic predictability. Look at the three cards. If your reputation is "keeps promises," that's useful predictability. People can build on it. If your reputation is "always gives in eventually," that's exploitable predictability. People learn to just push harder and wait. And if your reputation is "retaliates wildly against any perceived offense," people may avoid dealing with you altogether. So the strategic question isn't just how to build a strong reputation. It's this. What reputation do I want to be bound by? That's a much more interesting question. Here's the common misunderstanding. People think a strong reputation is always good. But strong in the wrong direction can box you in. So ask yourself this. Which reputation are you currently bound by? And is it one you'd choose today? Now let's look at the trap on the other side of patience.
