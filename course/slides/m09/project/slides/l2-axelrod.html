Internomics · Module 9 — The Shadow of the Future

Here's the point of this slide. A very simple reciprocal strategy did remarkably well when the game repeated. Robert Axelrod became famous for exploring this problem through computer tournaments. They were built around the iterated Prisoner's Dilemma. He invited researchers to submit strategies that would play against one another again and again. Some of the strategies were complicated. They tried to model their opponents. They used statistical inference. They tried to exploit patterns. Yet the strategy that did best in the first tournament was remarkably simple. It was called Tit for Tat. Look at the cards. In round one it cooperated. After that it did whatever the other player had done on the previous round. If you cooperated it cooperated. If you defected it defected. When Axelrod ran a larger second tournament with 62 entries, Tit for Tat won again. There's something beautiful about why it worked. Tit for Tat wasn't successful because it dominated everyone. A cooperative player couldn't be exploited by it and then keep getting away with it. It succeeded partly because it created an environment where cooperation could continue. And it still responded to exploitation. Notice that it was never the first to defect. It was nice and it was not a pushover. Hold onto both of those qualities. We'll come back to them in Lesson Four when we talk about boundaries. Here's a question for you. In your closest relationships do you start by cooperating? And do you respond when someone takes advantage? Now let me warn you about the most common way people misread this result.
