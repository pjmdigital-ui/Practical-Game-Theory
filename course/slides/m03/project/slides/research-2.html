People differ in how many steps ahead they think

What it shows

Stahl and Wilson found wide variety in reasoning depth. Camerer, Ho and Chong's cognitive hierarchy model fit an average of about 1.5 steps. In Nagel's beauty contest, people rarely jumped straight to equilibrium.

What it doesn't prove

These figures come from specific games and participant pools, not universal facts about anyone in your life. Backward reasoning is only as good as your model of the other player.

Internomics · Module 3 — Think in Responses

The second spotlight is about depth. In what researchers call level-k models, a level-one thinker asks, "What will they probably do?" A level-two thinker asks, "What do they think I will do?" Dale Stahl and Paul Wilson's experiments found wide variety. In one, their estimates classified roughly a quarter of participants as level-one, about half as level-two, and the rest as Nash-type. Colin Camerer, Teck-Hua Ho and Juin-Kuan Chong's cognitive hierarchy model fit an average depth of roughly one and a half steps across the games they studied. And Rosemarie Nagel's beauty contest guessing game showed that people's choices depend on their model of everyone else's reasoning, and that they rarely jump straight to the equilibrium answer. So the rule isn't "think ten moves ahead." It's one or two responses ahead, calibrated to the actual person. Assume five layers of reasoning in someone who just wants lunch, and you can outsmart yourself. And a warning: in McKelvey and Palfrey's centipede-game experiments, strict backward induction predicted play would stop immediately, yet people usually continued for several rounds, which made more sense if they allowed for the chance the other player might be altruistic. Your backward reasoning is only as good as your model of the other player.
