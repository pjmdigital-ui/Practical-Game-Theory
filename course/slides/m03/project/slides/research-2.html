Internomics · Module 3 — Think in Responses

The second spotlight is about depth. How many steps ahead do people actually think? Researchers use what they call level-k models. A level-one thinker asks: "What will they probably do?" A level-two thinker goes further and asks: "What do they think I will do?" Dale Stahl and Paul Wilson ran experiments and found wide variety. In one study their estimates classified roughly a quarter of participants as level-one. About half came out as level-two. The rest were Nash-type thinkers. Then Colin Camerer and Teck-Hua Ho and Juin-Kuan Chong built what they called a cognitive hierarchy model. Across the games they studied it fit an average depth of roughly one and a half steps. And Rosemarie Nagel's beauty contest guessing game showed that people's choices depend on their model of everyone else's reasoning. People rarely jumped straight to the equilibrium answer. So the rule isn't "think ten moves ahead." It's one or two responses ahead. Calibrated to the actual person. If you assume five layers of reasoning in someone who just wants lunch, you can outsmart yourself. Now for what it doesn't prove. These figures come from specific games and specific participant pools. They aren't universal facts about anyone in your life. And there's one more warning. In McKelvey and Palfrey's centipede-game experiments, strict backward induction predicted play would stop immediately. People usually continued for several rounds. That made more sense if they allowed for the chance the other player might be altruistic. Your backward reasoning is only as good as your model of the other player.
