Internomics · Module 3 — Think in Responses

Threats show exactly why backward reasoning matters. Suppose someone says to you: "If you do that, I'm going to walk away." Should you believe them? Most of us decide based on how it was said. Did they sound serious? Did they raise their voice? Did they look like they meant it? But you can't determine credibility from how forcefully they say it. You need to ask a different question. If I actually do that, will walking away be their best response at that moment? That's the first card. Picture the moment after you've done the thing. Now they have to choose. Walking away might cost them a lot. It might leave them worse off than staying. If so, the threat may not hold up when the moment arrives. That's the key idea. A threat isn't strategically meaningful just because someone made it. You have to examine the incentives at the point where it would actually be carried out. MIT's treatment of dynamic games uses backward reasoning partly for this reason. It separates credible future actions from threats that wouldn't make sense once the decision point arrives. This is one of the foundations of strategic credibility. We'll explore it more later in the course. And it works in the other direction too. That's the second card. Before you make a threat, ask whether you'd actually want to carry it out when the moment comes. If you wouldn't, the other person may figure that out. Then your threat costs you credibility instead of earning it. Here's a question for you. What threat have you made lately that you wouldn't really want to carry out?
