Research spotlight · 1 of 2

Looking ahead can be learned

Eric Johnson · Colin Camerer · Sankar Sen · Talia Rymon. A three-round bargaining game.

What it shows

Full backward induction predicted a first offer of about $1.25. An equal split was $2.50. People offered about $2.11. After training they offered computer opponents about $1.22.

What it doesn't prove

That lab bargaining mirrors everyday conversation. That the "theoretical" offer is wise against real people. Or that thinking further ahead always works out better.

What it suggests: "Then what?" isn't automatic. It appears to be teachable.

Internomics · Module 3 — Think in Responses

We have two research spotlights this module. The first one asks a simple question. Do people naturally look ahead? The study comes from Eric Johnson and Colin Camerer along with Sankar Sen and Talia Rymon. They studied a three-round sequential bargaining game. Here's how to read the numbers. Under full backward induction the first offer should have been about a dollar twenty-five. An equal split would have been two fifty. Participants actually offered about two dollars and eleven cents on average. That's much closer to fair than to the backward-induction answer. The researchers also tracked what information people chose to look at before deciding. That pattern showed limited look-ahead. People weren't reasoning all the way back from the end. Now here's the part I find encouraging. When participants were explicitly trained in backward induction, they learned it quickly. Their offers to computerized opponents moved close to the prediction. About a dollar twenty-two. So what does it suggest? "Then what?" isn't automatic. But it appears to be teachable. That's good news for everyone taking this course. Now let's be careful about what it doesn't prove. It doesn't prove that lab bargaining mirrors your everyday conversations. It doesn't prove the theoretical offer is the wise move against real people. Remember that the trained offers went to computers. And it doesn't show that thinking further ahead always produces better outcomes. Here's the misunderstanding to avoid. This isn't a case for calculating harder. It's a case that looking ahead is a skill you can practice. Next let's look at how far ahead people actually think.
