Looking ahead isn't automatic, but it can be learned

What it shows

In Johnson, Camerer, Sen and Rymon's three-round bargaining game, first offers averaged about $2.11, versus about $1.25 under full backward induction. After training, offers to computer opponents moved to about $1.22.

What it doesn't prove

That lab bargaining mirrors everyday conversation, that the "theoretical" offer is wise against real people, or that thinking further ahead always produces better outcomes.

Internomics · Module 3 — Think in Responses

Two research spotlights this module. The first is from Eric Johnson, Colin Camerer, Sankar Sen and Talia Rymon, who studied a three-round sequential bargaining game. Under full backward induction, the first offer should have been about a dollar twenty-five. An equal split would have been two fifty. Participants actually offered about two dollars and eleven cents on average, and the information they chose to look at before deciding showed limited look-ahead, not full backward induction. But when participants were explicitly trained in backward induction, they learned it quickly, and their offers to computerized opponents moved close to the prediction, about a dollar twenty-two. What it suggests: "Then what?" isn't automatic, but it appears to be teachable. What it doesn't prove: that lab bargaining mirrors your conversations, that the theoretical offer is the wise move against real people, since those trained offers went to computers, or that thinking further ahead always works out better.
