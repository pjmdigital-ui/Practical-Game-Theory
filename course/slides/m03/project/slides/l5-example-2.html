Example · The escalation trap

The same loop, scaled up

The situation

Someone speaks aggressively to you. You read it as disrespect. You respond aggressively.

What happens

They read your response as proof you're hostile. They escalate. You escalate. Neither of you is acting as you intended.

The lesson

Each move was understandable. The whole interaction got worse. Nobody had to decide to make it terrible.

Internomics · Module 3 — Think in Responses

Here's the more serious version. I call it the escalation trap. Someone speaks aggressively to you. Maybe it's a coworker. Maybe it's someone at home. You interpret it as disrespect. So you respond aggressively. That feels fair. They started it. Now look at it from their side. They interpret your response as confirmation that you're hostile. So they escalate. And you escalate. Soon both of you are behaving in ways neither of you intended at the beginning. Here's what makes this a trap. Each person is responding rationally to the previous move. Or at least understandably. If you asked either of them to explain any single step, they could. Every step has a reason. Yet the overall interaction keeps getting worse. That's one of the most important things strategic thinking can reveal. A bad outcome can emerge from a sequence of locally understandable responses. Nobody has to wake up and decide "Let's make this interaction terrible." The pattern can create itself. Notice the word interpret again. Both people were responding to what they believed the other's move meant. Not to some neutral record of events. Here's the hopeful part. If the pattern is built from responses, a different response can change its direction. Remember the hallway. Someone stops. In a tense conversation that might mean answering heat with a question instead of more heat. Here's a question for you. Think of an argument that got out of hand. Where was the one step where a different response could have turned it? That's our takeaway.
