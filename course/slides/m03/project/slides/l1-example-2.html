Example · The raise, revisited

Same sentence. Different game.

The situation

The same words go to two different bosses: "I have another job offer."

What happens

One boss desperately wants to keep you. The other has been thinking about replacing you. They hear the move very differently.

The lesson

Base your expectation on what you know about the other player. Not on how the line sounds to you.

Internomics · Module 3 — Think in Responses

Now let's make that concrete. Picture two bosses. You walk into each office and say exactly the same thing. "I have another job offer." The first boss desperately wants to keep you. You're hard to replace. Losing you would hurt. For that boss your sentence may work exactly the way you hoped. It may lead straight to a better offer. The second boss has already been thinking about replacing you. Maybe they've had doubts for a while. For that boss your sentence lands very differently. It may confirm what they already suspected. It may give them a reason to stop investing in you. It may even lead them to start preparing for your departure. Same sentence. Same delivery. Same confidence. Different game. And here's what I want you to notice. Nothing about the words decided the outcome. What decided it was the other player. Their payoffs. Their options. What they already believed about you. That's why the second habit matters so much. You don't just ask what to say. You ask who's hearing it and what it makes them want to do. A common mistake here is to rehearse the sentence over and over. People polish the wording as if the right phrasing guarantees the right response. It doesn't. The phrasing matters less than the game it walks into. So before you use leverage, ask yourself honestly which boss you have. Here's the takeaway for this lesson.
