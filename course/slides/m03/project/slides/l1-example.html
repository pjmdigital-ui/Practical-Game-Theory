Internomics · Module 3 — Think in Responses

Let's go back to our employee asking for a raise. You've seen this person before in this course. Suppose you decide you're going to tell your boss you have another job offer. At first glance that seems like a strong move. It communicates leverage. It says you have options. And a lot of negotiation advice would stop right there and tell you to deliver it with confidence. Game theory asks you to go one step further. Then what? Look at the list on the screen. Your boss might offer you more money. That's the response you're hoping for. But they might ask to see the offer. They might call your bluff. They might tell you they simply can't compete. They might start worrying that you're leaving. They might quietly decide you're no longer committed. Or they might start preparing for your departure. That's seven different responses to one sentence. Some of them get you what you want. Some of them leave you worse off than if you'd said nothing. So the value of your statement isn't built into the words. It depends on which response you expect. Here's my question for you. If you said this to your own boss tomorrow, which of these seven would you bet on? And what do you know about your boss that makes you think so? That second question is the real work. Your expectation should come from what you know about the other player. Let's see why that matters so much.
