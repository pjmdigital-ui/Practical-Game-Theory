Not "What do I want them to do?" Wanting a response and making it attractive are two different things.

Internomics · Module 3 — Think in Responses

Before you make an important move, I want you to ask one question. What does this make the other person want to do? That's subtly different from the question we usually ask. "What do I want them to do?" The difference sounds small. It isn't. Let's walk through the four cards. You may want them to cooperate. Fine. But does your action actually make cooperation more attractive for them? You may want them to trust you. Does your behavior give them a reason to? Wanting trust doesn't create it. You may want them to compromise. Does your proposal make compromise better than the other options they have? If their alternatives look better, your wish won't move them. And you may want them to stop pushing. Here's the one that catches people. Does your response make pushing more attractive or less? If pushing gets them what they want from you, they'll keep pushing. It doesn't matter how much you wish they'd stop. In every one of these cases the wish is on your side of the board. The incentive is on theirs. And people respond to incentives far more reliably than to wishes. So here's a question to try this week. Think of one thing you keep wanting from someone. More help at home. Faster replies from a colleague. Whatever it is. Now ask: what in my own behavior makes that response attractive to them? If the honest answer is "nothing," you've found the problem. Let's name that gap directly.
