Internomics · Module 3 — Think in Responses

There's an important qualification I need to make. Game theory often begins with models where players are assumed to choose whatever best serves their preferences. In those models everyone plays well. Real human beings don't always do that. Look at the list on the screen. We make mistakes. We misunderstand. We get emotional. We have incomplete information. We miscalculate. We sometimes act against our own interests. And sometimes we simply don't understand the game very well. I've done every one of these. So have you. Does that make game theory useless? No. It means you need to hold two things apart. The first is what someone would choose if they were optimizing based on their information. The second is what they're actually likely to choose. Those aren't always the same. Think about someone who's exhausted and embarrassed in a tense meeting. The optimal move for them might be to stay calm and ask a question. The likely move might be to snap. If you predict only the optimal move, you'll be surprised. If you predict only the emotional move, you'll miss the times they hold it together. Good prediction holds both in view. In fact understanding that difference is one of the reasons experimental game theory exists. Researchers put real people into games to see how they actually play. We'll look at some of that research later in this module. Here's a question for you. Who in your life do you keep predicting as perfectly rational?
