Example · The negotiation

Decide the last move first

The situation

You make an offer. They accept or reject. After a rejection you get one final response.

How you reason · Backward

1 · The end

If they reject, what will I do with my final response?

→

2 · Their move

Knowing that, will they accept or reject?

→

3 · Now

So what should my opening offer be?

The lesson

The game runs forward. You reason through it backward.

Internomics · Module 3 — Think in Responses

Here's the example from the book. You're negotiating with someone. The structure is simple. You make an offer. They can accept it or reject it. If they reject it, you get one final opportunity to respond. That's the whole game. Three steps. Now most of us think about only one question here. "What should I offer?" We stare at the first move and try to get it right in isolation. Backward induction asks you to start at the other end. Look at the first box. If they reject, what will I do with that final response? Will I walk? Will I improve the offer? Will I hold firm? Decide that first. Then look at the second box. Once you know what you'll do at the end, ask what they'll do knowing that. If they expect you to cave after a rejection, rejecting looks pretty attractive to them. If they expect you to hold firm, accepting may look better. And only once you know that do you reach the third box. So what should I offer now? Notice how the opening offer changed. It's no longer a guess. It's built on your read of the whole sequence. The game runs forward. Offer. Accept or reject. One final response. But you reason through it backward. You don't need to do this for every small decision. Nobody needs a strategy session to pick a lunch spot. But when the stakes are high, deciding your last move first changes your first move. Here's a question to try. Think of a negotiation coming up for you. Have you decided what you'll do if they say no? Here's the takeaway.
