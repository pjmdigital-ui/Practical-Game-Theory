Internomics · Module 12 — Strategic Judgment

Here's something that changed how I handle bad moments. The times when another person surprises you are some of the most strategically useful moments you'll ever get. Most of us treat surprise as a failure. Something to explain away. Something to be embarrassed about. I want you to treat it as a gift. Because surprise reveals gaps in your model. And every surprise comes with its own question. Look at the table. You thought they'd cooperate. They didn't. Ask: what did I misunderstand? You thought they'd walk away. They stayed. Ask: what payoff did I underestimate? Maybe there was something holding them there you didn't see. You thought the threat was empty. They followed through. Ask: what made the commitment stronger than I realized? Maybe their reputation was on the line. Maybe they had already told other people. You thought the new rule would improve behavior. It made things worse. Ask: what response did the design create that I failed to anticipate? Think of the manager who starts a public late list to fix missed deadlines. The deadlines get worse. That's not a reason to make the list bigger. It's a reason to ask what the list is teaching people to do. Maybe it rewards hiding problems instead of raising them. The book puts it simply. A person committed to learning should become curious when surprised. Not defensive. Curious. Here's the misunderstanding to watch for. People think the goal is to stop being surprised. It isn't. If you're never surprised, you're probably not looking closely. So when was the last time someone surprised you? Which of these four questions would have helped? There's one more way people avoid learning from surprise. They call the other person irrational.
