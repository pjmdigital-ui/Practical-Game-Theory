Research spotlight · Learning in games

Real players learn from feedback

What it suggests

Researchers such as Fudenberg and Levine argue that learning models improve game theory's descriptive power. Experiments show behavior can change substantially with experience instead of starting at equilibrium.

What it doesn't prove

That any one person will learn quickly, or learn the right lesson. Experience without feedback can entrench a wrong story. Learning models describe many players and rounds, not your next conversation.

Internomics · Module 12 — Strategic Judgment

The book's Conclusion introduces no new experiments. Its evidence comes from the research audit behind the whole framework. When that audit compared the eleven models with university game-theory curricula, it found those curricula include learning in games, not just equilibrium analysis. Researchers such as Fudenberg and Levine have argued that learning models improve game theory's descriptive power, and experimental evidence shows behavior can change substantially through experience rather than starting at equilibrium. Real players don't arrive knowing the solution. They adjust from feedback, and so should you. That's why the loop became a rule over the whole book instead of a twelfth model. What it doesn't prove: that any one person will learn quickly, or learn the right lesson. Experience without feedback can entrench a wrong story. One more note from that audit: eleven models is a design judgment, not an empirical discovery. The framework is meant as the smallest useful set of lenses, not all of game theory. Choosing lenses well matters more than owning more of them.
