Internomics · Module 12 — Strategic Judgment

Here's the second finding from the research audit. It answers a question some of you have probably asked. Why eleven? Why not fifteen? Or thirty? Game theory is a huge field. The audit looked at major areas of it. Bargaining. Coalitions. Dominance. Social dilemmas. Auctions. Voting. Networks. It asked whether any of them needed its own model. And it applied one test. Does the concept give an ordinary person a genuinely different and broadly useful way to see strategic human interaction? That's a high bar. The answer was to keep eleven models. The other ideas were folded in as supporting concepts. For example, outside options and coalitions live under See the Game. Dominance lives under Think in Responses. The difference between coordination and free riding lives under Diagnose the Coordination Problem. So what does that suggest? The framework is meant as the smallest useful latticework of game-theoretic lenses for human interaction. It isn't meant to be all of game theory. And that connects straight back to this module. Choosing lenses well matters more than owning more of them. A person with three lenses used wisely beats a person with thirty used all at once. Now here's what it doesn't prove. Eleven is a design judgment. It's not an empirical discovery. Nobody measured human interaction and found exactly eleven structures in it. The audit doesn't claim the models are exhaustive. And it doesn't claim that a situation will fit neatly into one of them. Real situations usually involve two or three at once. That's why judgment matters. Here's a question for you. Has a situation ever come up where none of the eleven lenses seemed to fit? What did you do? Next let's turn to your tool for this module.
