Internomics · Module 12 — Strategic Judgment

Here's something I love about looking back over the whole book. Think about how the questions changed. Most of us start with the questions on the left. They're natural. They're what anyone would ask. But notice that every one of them starts after we've already interpreted the situation. Instead of "What should I say?" we asked "What game am I in?" Instead of "Why are they being difficult?" we asked "What payoff would make this behavior make sense?" That one question turns irritation into curiosity. Instead of "What should I do?" we asked "What will they do if I do it?" Instead of "Why does this keep happening?" we asked "What equilibrium are we creating?" Instead of "Are they telling the truth?" we asked "What makes this claim credible?" You don't need to read minds. You need to read incentives and signals. Instead of "Why don't they just cooperate?" we asked whether this is conflict or a coordination failure or a free-rider problem. Those three need completely different fixes. And instead of "How do I motivate them?" we asked "What behavior does the game reward?" Notice the pattern. The questions on the left are about the other person's character. The questions on the right are about the structure. The left side tends to produce blame. The right side tends to produce options. Better thinking often begins with a better question. Here's a small exercise. Pick one question on the left that you've asked this month. Now ask its partner on the right about the same situation. What do you see now?
