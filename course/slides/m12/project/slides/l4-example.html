Example · The resistant customer

Hold several hypotheses at once

The situation

A customer who seemed interested suddenly becomes resistant.

What happens next

The weak move is to decide at once: "I know exactly what they're doing." The strong move is to keep several explanations open.

The lesson

You don't need to choose one yet. Ask questions and watch behavior that tells them apart.

Certainty feels good. Accuracy is better.

Internomics · Module 12 — Strategic Judgment

Here's the example the book uses for multiple hypotheses. Imagine a customer who suddenly becomes resistant. Things were moving along. They seemed interested. Now they're hesitating. They're slow to reply. They're raising concerns that weren't there last week. Your mind will hand you an explanation in about half a second. And it will feel true. That's the weak move. "I know exactly what they're doing." It feels great. It's satisfying. And it's often wrong. The best strategists do something different. They hold several models at once. They don't rush to pick one. Then they ask questions or watch for behavior that helps tell the models apart. That's much more powerful than certainty. Because certainty closes your eyes. Once you're sure it's a price objection, you'll start discounting. You'll never notice that the real issue was someone else's approval. Or that they'd already chosen a competitor and were just being polite. So the discipline is this. You don't need to choose one explanation immediately. You need to keep them open long enough to gather evidence. And notice this isn't indecision. You still act. But your action is designed to learn something. A good question is a move in the game. It tells you which world you're in. Here's the line I want you to remember. Certainty feels good. Accuracy is better. Let me ask you something. When you get resistance, how many explanations do you usually consider before you respond? If the honest answer is one, this slide is for you.
