Know which games you refuse to play

Sometimes the correct move is walking away

Walking away

You've communicated clearly. You've tested your assumptions. You've enforced boundaries and allowed for repair. The game is still destructive.

Stop playing. An outside option is part of the game.

Refusing the game

Some environments reward deception. Some reward constant status competition. Some reward betrayal.

Don't play. Change the rules. Find different players. Build something better.

Walking away isn't defeat. There is strategy in refusal too.

Internomics · Module 12 — Strategic Judgment

And sometimes the opposite of vulnerability is true. Look at the left card. You've communicated clearly. You've tested your assumptions. You've enforced boundaries. You've allowed for repair. You've changed your own behavior. You've considered the other person's incentives. You've done all of it. And the game remains destructive. At that point strategic judgment may tell you something hard. Stop playing. That's not defeat. I want to say that clearly because a lot of people feel like walking away means they lost. It doesn't. Remember See the Game. An outside option is part of the game. A refusal to play can itself be a move. There is no virtue in becoming brilliant at surviving a game that repeatedly damages you when a better alternative exists. Now the right card goes one level higher. Strategic judgment isn't only recognizing games. It's deciding which games deserve your participation. Some environments reward deception. Some reward constant status competition. Some reward betrayal. Some require you to violate your principles just to stay competitive. You may be able to become excellent at those games. You may even win them. But perhaps the wiser choice is not to play. Or to change the rules. Or to find different players. Or to build something better. That last one is Change the Game in its biggest form. Here's the misunderstanding to watch for. Walking away is not the first move. Look at everything that comes before it on the left card. It's what you do after you've genuinely tried. So here's a question to sit with. Is there a game in your life you've gotten very good at surviving? Should you still be playing it? Let's finish the lesson with the last example in the book.
