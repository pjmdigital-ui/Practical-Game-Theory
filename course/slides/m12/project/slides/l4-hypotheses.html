Internomics · Module 12 — Strategic Judgment

Here are the five hypotheses from the book for that resistant customer. One interpretation is a price objection. Another is that they don't trust you yet. Another is that they need someone else's approval. Another is that they're interested but trying to improve their negotiating position. And another is that they've already chosen a competitor and they're being polite. Now look at how different the right response is for each one. If it's price, you might talk about value or options. If it's trust, talking about price is pointless. You need to give them reasons to believe you. If it's approval, the person in front of you may not be the decision-maker. You need to understand who else is in the game. That's See the Game. If it's negotiating position, a quick discount teaches them that resistance works. That's Manage Predictability. And if they've already chosen a competitor, the most respectful thing might be to make it easy for them to say so. That's why guessing wrong is so expensive. Your best move for one hypothesis can be your worst move for another. So what do you do? You hold all five. Then you look for the question or the behavior that separates them. A question about who else is involved in the decision helps with the approval hypothesis. Watching whether they keep engaging tells you something too. You don't have to know the answer before you act. You have to act in a way that teaches you the answer. That's what the bottom-right card means. So think about a time you got resistance from someone. Which of these five did you assume? Did you ever test it? That leads us to the takeaway for this lesson.
