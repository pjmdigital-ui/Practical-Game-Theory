Research spotlight · Cheap talk

When the payoff for exaggerating rises

What it shows

Crawford and Sobel: how much information can be credibly passed depends on how aligned interests are. Cox and Stoddard: messages were largely truthful. When the incentive to exaggerate rose, truthfulness and its benefits declined.

What it doesn't prove

People don't always lie when interests conflict. Costless words aren't meaningless. Aligned interests don't guarantee truth.

Use it: when you hear a claim, ask how much the speaker gains from moving your decision.

Internomics · Module 7 — Separate Words From Credibility

The first spotlight is cheap talk. Vincent Crawford and Joel Sobel showed that the information a sender can credibly pass depends on how aligned the players' interests are. Joseph Farrell and Matthew Rabin argued that cheap talk is neither automatically truthful nor automatically meaningless. Caleb Cox and Brock Stoddard ran an experiment and found communication was largely truthful and improved efficiency. When the incentive to exaggerate increased, truthfulness declined. Here's what this does not prove. It does not show that people always lie when interests conflict. And it does not show that aligned interests guarantee the truth. Here's the second spotlight.
