Research spotlight · Cheap talk

When the payoff for exaggerating rises

What it shows

Crawford and Sobel: how much information can be credibly passed depends on how aligned interests are. Cox and Stoddard: messages were largely truthful. When the incentive to exaggerate rose, truthfulness and its benefits declined.

What it doesn't prove

People don't always lie when interests conflict. Costless words aren't meaningless. Aligned interests don't guarantee truth.

Use it: when you hear a claim, ask how much the speaker gains from moving your decision.

Internomics · Module 7 — Separate Words From Credibility

There are two research spotlights in this module. The first one is cheap talk. Three pieces of work fit together here. Vincent Crawford and Joel Sobel built a model. A better-informed sender talks to a receiver whose later action affects them both. In their model the amount of information that can be credibly passed depends on how aligned the players' interests are. And informative communication can still happen when interests partly conflict. Joseph Farrell and Matthew Rabin reviewed the cheap-talk literature. They argued that cheap talk is neither automatically truthful nor automatically meaningless. Then Caleb Cox and Brock Stoddard ran an experiment. Players got private information about whether contributing to a public good would be valuable. They could send costless messages first. The group gained from honesty. But individuals sometimes had an incentive to exaggerate. Communication was largely truthful and it improved efficiency. When the incentive to exaggerate increased, truthfulness declined. The benefits shrank. What does it suggest? The people didn't change. The payoff for exaggerating did. And communication changed with it. So when you hear a claim, ask how much the speaker gains from moving your decision. Now here's what this research does not prove. It does not show that people always lie when interests conflict. It does not show that costless words are meaningless. And it does not show that aligned interests guarantee the truth. Hold all three of those. They keep you away from both naive and cynical thinking. Here's the second spotlight.
