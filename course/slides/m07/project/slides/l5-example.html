Example · When you're the one telling the truth

"I know I'm honest" isn't evidence for them

The situation

You're selling your car. You know the transmission is perfectly fine. The buyer asks "Any transmission problems?" You say "None."

What happened

The buyer wants a mechanic to inspect it. You feel insulted. Why don't they trust me?

The lesson

The inspection isn't an accusation. It's a response to an information asymmetry.

Internomics · Module 7 — Separate Words From Credibility

Imagine you're selling your car. You know the transmission is perfectly fine. The buyer asks "Any transmission problems?" You say "None." And then you get offended when they want a mechanic to inspect it. I understand that feeling. You're telling the truth. And it feels like they just called you a liar. From your perspective the question is "Why don't they trust me?" Now look at it from theirs. "The seller knows more about this car than I do. And the seller benefits from the sale." Both perspectives make sense. That's the key. You aren't wrong to feel honest. They aren't wrong to want a check. The inspection isn't necessarily an accusation. It's a response to an information asymmetry. That's strategic thinking. Run it through what we've learned. Would a seller say "No transmission problems" anyway? Almost every seller would. So your true sentence sounds exactly like a dishonest seller's false one. The buyer can't tell you apart from the words. The mechanic is how they tell you apart. So what should you do instead? Welcome the inspection. Even better, offer it before they ask. Bring the service records. Mention the shop that did the last check. You're making the truth easier to evaluate. And the irony is that an honest seller loses nothing by doing this. Only a dishonest one does. Here's a question for you.
