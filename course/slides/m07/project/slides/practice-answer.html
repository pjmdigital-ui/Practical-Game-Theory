Internomics · Module 7 — Separate Words From Credibility

Here's how I'd read it. One. Almost any developer chasing this contract benefits from saying "never missed a deadline" and "best in the city." Reliable or not. The words don't distinguish dependable from undependable. That doesn't make Marcus a liar. It means the claims alone give Dana little reason to update. Two. Marcus's pitch is mostly cheap talk delivered with confidence. The deck took effort. But a weak developer could make one just as polished. So it doesn't separate types. The signing discount is pressure. It isn't evidence. Priya offers things that are harder to produce if her claims were false. Live sites. Callable references. Milestone pay. Milestone pay is far more attractive to someone who expects to deliver. And volunteering the risk works against her short-term interest. That makes her other statements more believable. Dana shouldn't read Priya's awkwardness as weakness. And she shouldn't read Marcus's polish as accuracy. Three. The partner is probably honest. Their incentives mostly line up with Dana's, though friendship may color the view. Knowledge is the weak spot. Knowing Marcus socially says little about his deadline record or his skill. "He'd never lie" addresses honesty. It doesn't address accuracy. Marcus could be sincere and still overestimate himself. Trust the partner to do what? Four. Ask Marcus for verification that doesn't breach an NDA. A public portfolio. A work sample. A general reference. Offer both candidates the same milestone structure and see how each responds.
