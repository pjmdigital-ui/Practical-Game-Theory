Internomics · Module 7 — Separate Words From Credibility

Let's take the two cases where those questions come apart. First. Expertise without alignment. Imagine you're buying a complicated financial product. The salesperson knows far more about it than you do. So the knowledge advantage is theirs. But the salesperson also receives a large commission if you buy it. So incentive alignment is uncertain. That doesn't mean you should ignore the salesperson. They may be extremely useful. They may explain things you'd never figure out alone. It means you separate two questions. How much do they know? And how much does their payoff depend on what I decide? Those are independent dimensions of credibility. Now reverse it. Alignment without expertise. Your brother genuinely wants what's best for you. You trust him completely. But he knows nothing about the medical decision you're considering. His interests may be perfectly aligned with yours. His knowledge isn't. So you shouldn't ask "Do I trust my brother?" and think you've solved the problem. You should ask a sharper question. Trust him to do what? Trust him to want the best for you? Probably. Trust him to diagnose a complex condition? Maybe not. Strategic thinking separates these questions. Here's where people go wrong. We let one dimension spill into the other. We like the brother so we take his medical opinion. We distrust the salesperson so we ignore real expertise. Both are mistakes. Next comes something that fools almost everyone. Confidence.
