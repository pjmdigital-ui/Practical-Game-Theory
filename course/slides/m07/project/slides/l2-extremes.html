Internomics · Module 7 — Separate Words From Credibility

Joseph Farrell and Matthew Rabin reviewed the game-theory literature on cheap talk. They argued that both of the common extremes are wrong. The first extreme says costless words are meaningless. If it costs nothing to say then it tells you nothing. That's wrong. Under the right incentive structure ordinary talk can transmit substantial information. The second extreme goes the other way. It says people will just tell each other the truth because talking is free and everyone benefits from good information. That's also wrong. Costless communication does not automatically produce efficient truth-telling. So where does that leave us? Somewhere in the middle. And the thing that decides where in the middle is the incentive structure. That distinction matters a lot in practice. Some people are naive. They hear words and they treat them as facts. Other people pride themselves on being hard to fool. They treat every statement as noise. Both of those people are making the same mistake. They're judging the words without looking at the game. The naive person trusts words in games where the sender gains from shading the truth. The cynic throws away good information in games where everyone wants the same outcome. Here's a question for you. Which way do you lean? Most of us lean one way with strangers and the other way with people we like. Words can be cheap and still useful. Now let's see what happens in the lab when you change the incentives.
