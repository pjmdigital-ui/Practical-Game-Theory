Internomics · Module 7 — Separate Words From Credibility

The second spotlight is signaling. Michael Spence's work was recognized as part of the 2001 Nobel Prize in economics. He showed how education can help employers distinguish among workers whose productivity they can't observe directly. Under certain conditions. That qualifier matters. Then Dorothea Kübler, Wieland Müller and Hans-Theo Normann tested versions of that job-market signaling game in the lab. What did they find? More productive worker types invested in education more often. And employers paid higher wages to those who invested. So the basic mechanism showed up. That's encouraging. But separation was incomplete. Some types didn't sort the way the theory predicted. And wages didn't converge perfectly to the theoretical equilibrium. That's exactly the kind of evidence I want you to hold onto. The theory gives us a lens. Real people approximate it imperfectly. What does this suggest for your life? Behavior that's harder to produce for someone whose claim is false tells you more than self-description. A work sample. A warranty. A verifiable track record. Those beat "I'm great at this." Now here's what it does not prove. It does not show that observable actions perfectly reveal hidden qualities. And it does not show that a costly action proves a claim is true. Remember the twenty million dollar ad campaign. Signals contain information. They don't perfectly reveal reality. So treat a good signal as strong evidence. Never as a guarantee.
