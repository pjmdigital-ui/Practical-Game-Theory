Internomics · Module 7 — Separate Words From Credibility

Game theorists have studied this problem formally. Imagine two players. One knows something the other doesn't. We call the informed player the sender. The other one is the receiver. The sender communicates a message. The receiver then makes a decision. But here's the important part. The sender cares about what decision the receiver makes. Now communication becomes strategic. Vincent Crawford and Joel Sobel built a classic model of exactly this situation. They called it strategic information transmission. A better-informed sender talks to a receiver whose later action affects both of them. One of the model's central insights is this. The amount of information that can be credibly transmitted depends partly on how closely the two players' interests are aligned. When interests line up the sender has little reason to shade the truth. As interests pull apart the words carry less. That's an enormously useful idea. It tells us you shouldn't evaluate a message without considering the sender's incentives. Notice how often you're the receiver in this picture. A vendor tells you a delivery date. A colleague tells you a project estimate. A seller tells you about a used car. In each case the sender knows more than you do. And in each case they care what you decide. So here's a question for you. In the next important conversation you have this week, who is the sender? And what do they want you to decide? Now a warning before you take this too far.
