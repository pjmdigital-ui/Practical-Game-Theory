Internomics · Module 7 — Separate Words From Credibility

Suppose someone tells you something false. Does that automatically mean they lied? No. A lie requires some form of intentional deception. They may simply be wrong. So credibility analysis needs one more distinction. Truthfulness asks whether they report what they actually believe. Accuracy asks whether what they believe is actually correct. Those are separate. Someone can be truthful but inaccurate. That's the friend who sincerely gives you bad advice. And someone can occasionally be accurate while being dishonest. A liar gets lucky sometimes. Why does this matter? Because when a claim turns out to be false we tend to jump to a personality judgment. "They lied to me." Sometimes that's right. Often it isn't. Often they believed it. Their knowledge was the weak link. Not their character. And the fix is completely different. You don't need to stop trusting their honesty. You need to stop relying on their knowledge in that area. That's why I don't want you to reduce credibility to a personality judgment. It's a problem of knowledge. It's a problem of incentives. It's a problem of evidence and reliability. Think of someone whose advice burned you once. Was it a lie? Or were they sincere and wrong? Your answer changes how you should treat their next claim. It also changes how you should talk to them about it. Now let's pull knowledge and incentives apart. Because they can point in opposite directions.
