Example · Two credibility problems

One question blurs them together

The situation

A friend says an investment is "guaranteed to go up." A salesperson pitches a financial product.

What's going on

The friend is sincere but knows almost nothing about investing. The salesperson knows a lot and earns a commission.

The lesson

The friend has a knowledge problem. The salesperson has an incentive problem. "Do I trust them?" can't tell them apart.

Internomics · Module 7 — Separate Words From Credibility

Let's put two people side by side. A friend sincerely tells you "That investment is guaranteed to go up." Maybe they believe it completely. But perhaps they know almost nothing about investing. Honesty isn't the same thing as accuracy. They aren't lying to you. They may simply be wrong. Now the salesperson for that complicated financial product. High knowledge. Uncertain alignment. They know the product inside and out. And a commission rides on your decision. Those are two completely different credibility problems. The friend's problem is knowledge. The salesperson's problem is incentives. Now watch what happens if all you ask is "Do I trust them?" You probably trust your friend. So you take the tip. You probably don't trust the salesperson. So you tune out everything they say. You've got it backwards on both counts. The friend's sincerity doesn't make the tip accurate. And the salesperson's commission doesn't make their knowledge useless. A better approach looks like this. With the friend you thank them and go check the claim with someone who actually knows. With the salesperson you use their knowledge to understand the product. Then you get the recommendation verified by someone whose payoff doesn't depend on your choice. Same goal in both cases. Different fix. That's what separating the dimensions buys you. Here's a question for you. Who in your life gets your trust on topics they don't actually know? Let's lock this in.
