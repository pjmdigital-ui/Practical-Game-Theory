Example · The on-time promise

A better story won't fix a history

The situation

You tell a client "We're going to deliver this project on time." You were late on the last three projects.

What happened

You explain it beautifully. You tell a story. You handle every objection. They still don't believe you.

The lesson

It's a credibility problem. Milestones they can check might help where more rhetoric won't.

Internomics · Module 7 — Separate Words From Credibility

Here's the second example. Imagine you're trying to convince a client of one thing. "We're going to deliver this project on time." You explain it beautifully. You tell a story. You use powerful language. You handle every objection. The client still doesn't believe you. So you conclude "I need a more persuasive argument." Maybe not. What if you've missed the real problem? Perhaps you've been late on the last three projects. The client understands your words perfectly. They simply don't believe them. And honestly, why would they? They have three rounds of evidence. Your speech is one round of cheap talk. More rhetoric may accomplish nothing. In fact a really polished pitch can make it worse. It can look like you're trying to talk past the history. The strategic problem isn't "How do I say this better?" It's "How do I make this claim more credible?" So what might actually help? Milestones they can check. Short check-ins with something real to show at each one. Early warning when something slips instead of a surprise at the end. Maybe a structure where the risk of lateness lands on you instead of them. Each of those gives the client something besides your word. And each one is easier to offer if you really will deliver. That's what makes it informative. Over time you're rebuilding the history. Here's a question for you. Where are you trying to out-talk a track record right now? Let's close the lesson.
