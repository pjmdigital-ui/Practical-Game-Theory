Three structures of interest

Opposed

What benefits me hurts you. Zero-sum games like chess are the strong form. They're rarer than we imagine.

Aligned

One of the most important lessons in game theory is that interests have different structures. I want you to recognize three broad patterns. First, opposed interests. What benefits me hurts you. The strong form is a zero-sum game. That's where one player's gain is exactly the other player's loss. Chess is the obvious example. If I win, you lose. There isn't much room for us to create extra value together. But here's the thing. Pure zero-sum situations are far less common in everyday relationships than we imagine. Second, aligned interests. We both benefit from the same outcome. Picture two employees trying to get a difficult project over the line. If it succeeds, they both win. Their main problem may not be conflict at all. It may be coordination. And that matters because the fix isn't persuasion or bargaining. The fix is figuring out how to get our actions lined up. Third, mixed interests. This is where most of human life actually happens. We both want the thing to succeed. We just disagree about how. So here's the question I want you to swap in. Stop asking how you beat them. Start asking where your interests are actually aligned and where they're opposed. That question opens up possibilities the first one closes off. Let's look more closely at mixed interests.
