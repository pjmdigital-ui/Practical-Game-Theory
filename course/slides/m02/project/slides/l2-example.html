Example · Buying a house

One deal, more than one dimension

The situation

You want to buy a house for $500,000. The seller wants $550,000.

What happened

You conflict over price. You align on the sale itself. You both want it to happen.

The lesson

This isn't a purely competitive game. Name where you conflict and where you align.

Opposed on price + aligned on closing = mixed interests.

Internomics · Module 2 — See the Payoffs

Here's how this looks in practice. Let's say you want to buy a house. You want to pay five hundred thousand dollars. The seller wants five hundred and fifty thousand. There's an obvious conflict. You want a lower price. They want a higher price. If that's all you see, you walk in ready for battle. But look again. There's also a powerful area of alignment. You both want the transaction to happen. You want the house. They want to sell it. Neither of you wants to start over with someone new. So this isn't a purely competitive game. You have conflicting interests over price. And you have aligned interests over completing the sale. That's a mixed-interest game. This is incredibly common in human interaction. And it changes how you behave in the room. If you only see the conflict, you treat the seller like an enemy. You posture. You dig in. You risk blowing up a deal you both want. If you see both parts, you can be firm on price and still protect the thing you share. You can say something like this. We both want this to close. Let's figure out the number. That's not soft. It's accurate. Here's the misunderstanding to watch for. Being firm on one dimension doesn't require being hostile on all of them. Now let's take it one step further with a business deal.
