The Ultimatum Game

Why would anyone turn down free money?

The setup

One person proposes how to split a sum of money. The other accepts or rejects. If they reject it both get nothing.

The simple prediction

Accept almost any positive amount. $1 out of $100 still beats $0.

What people do

They often reject offers they see as unfair. Even when that leaves them with nothing.

If people sacrifice money to punish unfairness, fairness is part of the game.

Internomics · Module 2 — See the Payoffs

Here's another famous experiment. It's called the Ultimatum Game. One person receives a sum of money. They have to propose how to divide it with a second person. The second person has two choices. They can accept the proposal. Or they can reject it. If they reject it, both receive nothing. Now think about what a simple economic model predicts. The second person should accept almost any positive amount. If I offer you one dollar out of a hundred, you should supposedly prefer one dollar to zero. A dollar is a dollar. But that's not what people consistently do. Across many experiments people frequently reject offers they see as unfair. They do it even when rejecting leaves them with nothing. So why? Because the payoff may include something beyond money. People can care about fairness. They can care about reciprocity. They can care about punishing someone who treated them badly. They can care about dignity. They can care about how another person treats them. Here's the misunderstanding I want you to avoid. Some people hear this and say the math was useless. It wasn't. Quite the opposite. The experiment teaches us something about the actual payoff structure. If people consistently sacrifice money to punish perceived unfairness, then fairness is part of the game. We'll look at the research itself later in this module. For now ask yourself this. When have you walked away from a deal because of how it was offered?
