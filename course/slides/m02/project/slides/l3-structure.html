Internomics · Module 2 — See the Payoffs

One of the most famous examples in game theory shows why payoffs matter so much. It's called the Prisoner's Dilemma. Two people each get a choice. They can cooperate or they can defect. Now the labels aren't the important part. The payoff structure is. Here's how it works. If both cooperate, both do reasonably well. If one defects while the other cooperates, the defector does particularly well. The cooperator suffers. And if both defect, both do worse than they could have done by cooperating. Sit with that for a second. Each person on their own is tempted to defect. Defecting protects them if the other person defects. And it pays extra if the other person cooperates. But when both follow that logic, they both end up worse off. That's the dilemma. Now here's what I really want you to notice. The players don't have to be bad people. They don't even have to dislike each other. The structure of the payoffs creates pressure toward a particular behavior. That's one of the most profound ideas in game theory. Think about what that means for how you judge people. You see someone at work hoarding information or cutting corners. Your first instinct is to blame their character. Sometimes that's right. But it's worth asking a different question first. What is the game rewarding them for? And if the structure is part of the problem, can the structure change? It can.
