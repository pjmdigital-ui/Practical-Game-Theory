The Prisoner's Dilemma: structure, not character

Both cooperate

Both do reasonably well

One defects

The defector does particularly well; the cooperator suffers

Both defect

Both do worse than if they'd cooperated

The players don't have to be bad people. And payoffs can change: swap individual bonuses for a team bonus, and the people don't change. The game does.

Internomics · Module 2 — See the Payoffs

One of the most famous examples in game theory shows why payoffs matter so much. Two people each choose to cooperate or defect. The labels aren't the important part. The payoff structure is. If both cooperate, both do reasonably well. If one defects while the other cooperates, the defector does particularly well and the cooperator suffers. If both defect, both do worse than they could have by cooperating. And notice: the players don't have to be bad people. They don't even have to dislike each other. The structure of the payoffs creates pressure toward a particular behavior. Which also means the structure can change. Picture two employees competing for a promotion under individual rewards. Management switches to a bonus paid only if the whole team hits its target. The people didn't change. The game did. So before trying to change someone's behavior, understand what the current environment rewards.
