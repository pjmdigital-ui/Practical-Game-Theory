Autonomy can be part of the payoff

What it shows

In Falk and Kosfeld's 2006 experiment principals could impose a minimum performance requirement. Most agents then performed worse. Many read the control as distrust and a limit on their autonomy.

What it doesn't prove

Not that oversight always backfires. The evidence is good but context-dependent. Treat findings like these as possibilities. Not universal laws.

Internomics · Module 2 — See the Payoffs

The second spotlight comes from Armin Falk and Michael Kosfeld. It was published in the American Economic Review in 2006. They ran what's called a principal-agent game. Think of a principal as a boss and an agent as the person doing the work. In this game the principals could impose a minimum performance requirement on the agents. You'd expect that control to push performance up. It didn't. Instead of performing better under control, most agents reduced their performance in response to it. And many of them described the control in a telling way. They saw it as a signal of distrust. They saw it as a limit on their autonomy. So what does it suggest? Changing someone's material options can also change what the outcome means to them. A rule meant to secure performance can read as "I don't trust you." And once it reads that way, the payoff has changed. Now what doesn't it prove? It doesn't prove that oversight always backfires. The evidence is good but it's context-dependent. You may have heard of the daycare study by Gneezy and Rustichini. A fine for late pickup was followed by more late pickups. It's a vivid example that incentives can change social meaning. But a later replication attempt found that fines did deter. So treat these findings as possibilities. Not universal laws. Here's the question for you. Where have you added a rule or a check that might be read as distrust? Now let's turn all of this into your tool.
