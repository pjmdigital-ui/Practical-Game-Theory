Research spotlight · Falk and Kosfeld

Autonomy can be part of the payoff

What it shows

In Falk and Kosfeld's 2006 experiment, when principals imposed a minimum performance requirement, most agents performed worse, not better. Many read the control as distrust and a limit on their autonomy.

What it doesn't prove

Not that oversight always backfires; the evidence is context-dependent. Treat findings like these as possibilities, not universal laws.

Internomics · Module 2 — See the Payoffs

The second spotlight comes from Armin Falk and Michael Kosfeld, published in the American Economic Review in 2006. They ran a principal-agent game where principals could impose a minimum performance requirement on agents. Instead of performing better under that control, most agents reduced their performance in response to it. Many described the control as a signal of distrust and a limit on their autonomy. What it suggests: changing someone's material options can also change what the outcome means to them. A rule meant to secure performance can read as "I don't trust you." What it doesn't prove: that oversight always backfires. The evidence is good but context-dependent. You may have heard of the daycare study by Gneezy and Rustichini, where a fine for late pickup was followed by more late pickups. It's a vivid example that incentives can change social meaning, but a later replication attempt found that fines did deter. So treat these as possibilities, not laws.
