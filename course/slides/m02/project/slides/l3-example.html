Example · The lucrative job

"Irrational" to whom?

The situation

You're offered a lucrative job. Taking it would mean missing your children's lives.

What happened

You turn it down. From a purely financial view an observer calls it irrational.

The lesson

Your priorities include family. The observer misunderstood your payoff function.

"Not rational" often means "not maximizing the thing I assumed they cared about."

Internomics · Module 2 — See the Payoffs

This distinction matters enormously. In everyday language we often say, "That person isn't being rational." What we frequently mean is something else. We mean, "That person isn't maximizing the thing I assumed they cared about." Those aren't the same thing at all. Here's an example. Suppose you're offered a lucrative job. But taking it would require you to miss your children's lives. The travel or the hours would take you away from them. So you turn it down. Now picture someone looking at that choice from a purely financial perspective. To them it looks irrational. You left real money on the table. But if your priorities include family, the decision makes perfect sense. In fact it might be the most rational thing you've ever done. So the problem wasn't that you made an irrational decision. The problem was that the observer misunderstood your payoff function. A payoff function is just the full set of things a person values and how they weigh them. The observer only counted one item on your list. This is one of the most useful ways game theory can change how we read people. Rational doesn't mean selfish. And it doesn't mean money. It means pursuing what you actually value. So here's my question for you. Who in your life have you called irrational lately? What might be on their list that isn't on yours? Now I need to draw an important boundary.
