Behavioral economics adds an important qualification here. Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky developed what's called prospect theory. It showed that people's choices often depend on more than objective outcomes. They also depend on how gains and losses are framed relative to a reference point. A reference point is the baseline we judge from. Very often it's an expectation. If I expected a big bonus, a smaller one can feel like a loss. Even though it's still money in my pocket. And losses can carry more psychological weight than equivalent gains in many contexts. Now I want to be careful here. Look at the third card. This doesn't mean people always hate losses more than they like gains. You'll hear that said as if it were a law of nature. It isn't. Human behavior is more complicated than that. But it gives us a useful lens. The same objective outcome can produce different decisions. It depends on what someone perceives themselves as gaining or losing. So when someone reacts more strongly than you expected, ask yourself a question. What were they comparing this to? What was their reference point? If you know that, a reaction that seemed out of proportion may suddenly make sense. We'll come back to this when we get to framing later in the course.
