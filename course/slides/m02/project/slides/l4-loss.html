Behavioral economics adds an important qualification. Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky's prospect theory showed that people's choices often depend not only on objective outcomes but on how gains and losses are framed relative to a reference point. Losses can carry more psychological weight than equivalent gains in many contexts. That doesn't mean people always hate losses more than they like gains. Human behavior is more complicated than that. But it gives us a useful lens. Suppose two people are offered the exact same deal. One sees, "I'm getting ten thousand dollars." The other sees, "I'm losing five thousand compared with what I expected." Same objective outcome. Different perceived payoff. And because decisions are made on perceived outcomes, behavior can differ. Reality still matters. But your perception of the game influences how you play it, and their perception influences how they play it.
