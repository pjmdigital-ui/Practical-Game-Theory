Research spotlight · The Ultimatum Game

Fairness is part of the payoff

What it shows

Since Güth, Schmittberger and Schwarze (1982), responders often reject offers they see as unfair, even when that leaves them with nothing. In one meta-analysis, proposers offered about 40% on average, and about 16% of offers were rejected.

What it doesn't prove

Not that people always choose fairness over money, or that money doesn't motivate. The weight on fairness varies across people, cultures, situations and stakes.

Internomics · Module 2 — See the Payoffs

Our first research spotlight is the Ultimatum Game. One person proposes how to divide a sum of money. The other either accepts, and both get the proposed amounts, or rejects, and both get nothing. A money-only model says accept anything positive, since one dollar beats zero. People often don't. Güth, Schmittberger, and Schwarze's 1982 experiment is the classic starting point. A later meta-analysis pooled 37 papers with 75 experimental results: proposers offered about 40 percent of the pie on average, and responders rejected about 16 percent of offers, less often when offers and total stakes were larger. Slonim and Roth ran versions in Slovakia with stakes varied by a factor of 25, and raising the stakes had only a small effect on inexperienced players. What it suggests: the payoff isn't only "How much did I get?" It can also be "How was I treated?" What it doesn't prove: that people always choose fairness over money. Higher stakes reduce some of these effects without eliminating them.
