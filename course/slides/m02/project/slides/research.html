Research spotlight · The Ultimatum Game

Fairness is part of the payoff

What it shows

Güth, Schmittberger and Schwarze (1982) is the classic start. Responders often reject offers they see as unfair. Even when that leaves them with nothing. In one meta-analysis proposers offered about 40% on average. About 16% of offers were rejected.

What it doesn't prove

Not that people always choose fairness over money. Not that money doesn't motivate. The weight on fairness varies across people and cultures. It varies with situations and stakes.

Internomics · Module 2 — See the Payoffs

Our first research spotlight is the Ultimatum Game. You met it in lesson three. Here's the research behind it. One person proposes how to divide a sum of money. The other person either accepts or rejects. If they accept, both get the proposed amounts. If they reject, both get nothing. A money-only model says accept anything positive. One dollar beats zero. People often don't. The classic starting point is the 1982 experiment by Güth, Schmittberger and Schwarze. Since then the finding has been replicated and extended across many settings. A later meta-analysis pooled 37 papers with 75 experimental results. On average proposers offered about 40 percent of the pie. Responders rejected about 16 percent of offers. Rejection was less common when offers were larger. It was also less common when the total stakes were larger. Slonim and Roth ran versions in Slovakia. They varied the stakes by a factor of 25. Raising the stakes had only a small effect on inexperienced players. Rejections fell somewhat. And experienced proposers gradually offered less. So what does it suggest? The payoff isn't only "How much did I get?" It can also be "How was I treated?" If you price only the material outcome, an insulted person's walkout will look irrational when it isn't. Now what doesn't it prove? It doesn't prove that people always choose fairness over money. It doesn't prove that money doesn't motivate people. The weight on fairness varies. Higher stakes reduce some of these effects without eliminating them. Our second spotlight is about autonomy.
