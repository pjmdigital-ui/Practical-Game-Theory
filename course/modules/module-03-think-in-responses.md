## Why this module matters

In Module 1 you learned to see the game. In Module 2 you learned to see the payoffs. This module is about the idea that sits at the heart of the whole book: **your move is only half of the move.** The other half is what the other person does in response, and their response depends, at least partly, on what you did.

Most of us treat communication as a straight line: I say something, they hear it, outcome. Real interaction is a chain: I act, they respond, I respond to their response. When we forget that, we judge our moves by how they feel to make ("I was just being honest") instead of by what they cause. We confuse **intention** with **incentive**.

The shift is simple to say and hard to practice. Before an important move, don't stop at "What should I say?" Ask what it's likely to make them do, what you'll do then, and, when stakes are high, where the sequence leads. Once another person is involved, your move doesn't end when you stop talking. Your move becomes their situation.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this module you will be able to:

1. **Explain** what a best response is and why the value of any move depends on the other person's likely reply.
2. **Distinguish** between what you intend a move to produce and what the move actually makes attractive for the other person.
3. **Predict** a set of plausible responses to a planned move using the other person's goals, information, options, and constraints, rather than your own preferences.
4. **Reason backward** from where a sequence is likely to end, and use that reasoning to test whether a threat or commitment is credible.
5. **Map** a past or upcoming interaction as a sequence of interdependent decisions, identify the turning point, and spot escalation loops before they take over.

## Lessons

### Lesson 3.1 — Your move is only half of the move (~8 min)

**Teaching points**
- Communication isn't a straight line ("I say it → they hear it → outcome"). It's a chain: I act → they respond → I respond to their response → they respond to mine.
- A **best response** is the action that gives a player the best outcome given what the other player is doing. Nash equilibrium is built from it: each player's strategy is a best response to the others, so no one gains by changing alone.
- You don't need the math. You need two habits: "If I do this, what are they likely to do next?" and "If they do that, what will I do?"
- Restaurant example: if your friend is definitely going to the Italian place, your best response may be to go there too; if they're going to the steakhouse, it changes. Your best move is never determined in isolation.

**Example to use:** The employee who plans to say, "I have another job offer." Then what? The boss might offer more money, ask to see the offer, call the bluff, say they can't compete, worry you're leaving, decide you're no longer committed, or start preparing for your departure. A boss desperate to keep you and a boss already considering a replacement will respond very differently. Same sentence, different game.

**On-screen takeaway:** > **Your move is only half of the move. The other half is their response.**

### Lesson 3.2 — Intention vs. incentive (~8 min)

**Teaching points**
- Ask "What does this make the other person want to do?", not just "What do I want them to do?"
- You may want cooperation, trust, compromise, or for them to stop pushing. The test is whether your action makes that response more attractive than their alternatives.
- You can intend a response without creating the conditions that make it rational.
- People don't respond only to your words. They respond to the game those words are entering.

**Example to use:** A sales manager says, "I want you to collaborate more," while compensation rewards each person for outperforming colleagues. The manager sincerely wants collaboration, but each salesperson's best response may be to protect their own leads. They heard the message perfectly; their incentives make another response more attractive.

**On-screen takeaway:** > **People respond to the game your words enter, not just to your words.**

### Lesson 3.3 — Predicting responses well (~10 min)

**Teaching points**
- Don't ask "What should they do?", "What would a reasonable person do?", or "What would I do?" Ask: "Given their goals, information, options, and constraints, what is the most likely response?"
- The projection trap: "If I were them, I'd never do that" puts *your* preferences into *their* position. Use their payoffs (Module 2).
- People aren't perfectly rational. They get emotional, miscalculate, and misread the game. Separate what someone would choose if optimizing from what they're actually likely to choose.
- **Rationalizability** as a shortcut: don't waste energy predicting moves that make no strategic sense. If Choice A is worse for you than Choice B no matter what they do, eliminate A first.

**Example to use:** A customer refuses your offer. Instead of "They're being unreasonable," ask what would make sense with their objectives and constraints. Maybe they don't trust the delivery date, have another supplier, fear looking foolish, need their boss's approval, don't see the value, or are testing your flexibility. You're now searching the game instead of judging the person.

**On-screen takeaway:** > **Put their preferences into their position, not yours.**

### Lesson 3.4 — Think backward from the end (~9 min)

**Teaching points**
- An angry email isn't just an email. If you reply angrily, they may escalate; then you're both defending yourselves instead of solving the problem. You've created a sequence.
- **Backward induction**: ask where the sequence eventually leads, then reason back. "If they reject, what will I do? Knowing that, what will they do? So what should I do now?"
- The Final Conversation: the same disagreement plays differently if it's your last conversation ever versus the start of twenty years working together. The best response to a move depends partly on what comes after it (more in Module 9).
- Threats: "If you do that, I'm walking away" can't be judged by how forcefully it's said. Ask, "If I actually do that, will walking away be their best response?"

**Example to use:** Run the negotiation from the chapter: you make an offer, they accept or reject, and if they reject you get one final response. Have learners decide that last move first, then ask what the other side will do knowing it, and only then choose the opening offer.

**On-screen takeaway:** > **Judge a threat by the incentives at the moment it would be carried out.**

### Lesson 3.5 — Loops, escalation, and the limits of prediction (~9 min)

**Teaching points**
- Interactions are loops: I act → you respond → I *interpret* your response → I act again. We respond to what we believe an action means (Module 6).
- **The escalation trap**: a bad outcome can emerge from a sequence of locally understandable responses. Loops can settle into stable but bad patterns (equilibrium, Module 5), so ask, "What pattern will my action create?"
- More thinking isn't always better. No 47-step tree for "What do you want for dinner?" Ask, "What response could materially change the outcome?"
- Your model is a hypothesis. Use **model → act → observe → update**. When surprised, ask "What did I misunderstand about the game?" The response is data.
- They're predicting you, too. If you always give in to pressure, people learn that pressure works (Module 4).

**Example to use:** Two people in a hallway step right, then left, then right again, bouncing side to side. Neither wanted it; each was responding to the other. Scale it up: someone speaks aggressively, you read disrespect and respond in kind, they read hostility and escalate, and soon both of you are acting in ways neither intended.

**On-screen takeaway:** > **A bad outcome can emerge from a sequence of locally understandable responses.**

## Key terms

| Term | Plain-language meaning |
|---|---|
| Best response | The action that gives a player the best outcome given what the other player is doing. |
| Nash equilibrium | An outcome where each player's strategy is a best response to the others, so no one benefits by changing alone. |
| Intention vs. incentive | The gap between the response you want and the response your action actually makes attractive. |
| Rationalizability (iterated elimination of dominated strategies) | Ruling out moves that make no strategic sense, such as a choice that's worse for you no matter what the other person does. |
| Backward induction | Starting from the final decision in a sequence and reasoning back to what to do now. |
| Credible threat | A threat the person would actually want to carry out when the moment arrives, regardless of how forcefully it was stated. |
| Feedback loop | A cycle in which each person interprets and responds to the other's last move. |
| Escalation trap | A worsening pattern built from responses that each seem understandable on their own. |
| Model → act → observe → update | Treat your prediction as a hypothesis, compare it with what happens, and revise your understanding of the game. |

## Research spotlight

**1. People don't naturally look far ahead, but they can learn to.** Eric Johnson, Colin Camerer, Sankar Sen, and Talia Rymon studied a three-round sequential bargaining game. Under full backward induction, the first offer should have been about $1.25; an equal split would have been $2.50. Participants actually offered about $2.11 on average, and the information they chose to inspect before deciding showed limited look-ahead rather than full backward induction. When participants were explicitly trained in backward induction, they learned it quickly, and their offers to computerized opponents moved close to the theoretical prediction (about $1.22).

*What it suggests:* "Then what?" isn't automatic for most of us, which is why it's worth practicing, and the skill appears to be teachable.

*What it does NOT prove:* That lab bargaining behavior mirrors everyday conversation, or that the "theoretical" offer is the wise move against real people (the trained offers were made to computer opponents). It also doesn't show that thinking further ahead always produces better outcomes.

**2. People differ in how many steps ahead they think.** In level-k models, a level-1 thinker asks, "What will they probably do?"; a level-2 thinker asks, "What do they think I will do?" Dale Stahl and Paul Wilson's experiments found wide variety; in one, their estimates classified roughly 24% of participants as level-1, 49% as level-2, and 27% as Nash-type. Colin Camerer, Teck-Hua Ho, and Juin-Kuan Chong's cognitive hierarchy model (Quarterly Journal of Economics) fit an average depth of roughly 1.5 steps across the games they studied. Rosemarie Nagel's "beauty contest" guessing game (American Economic Review, 1995) showed that people's choices depend on their model of everyone else's reasoning, and that they rarely jump straight to the equilibrium answer.

*What it suggests:* The rule isn't "think ten moves ahead." It's "think one or two responses ahead, calibrated to the actual person." If you assume someone is reasoning five layers deep when they just want to leave the meeting and eat lunch, you can outsmart yourself.

*What it does NOT prove:* These figures come from specific games and participant pools; they are not universal facts about people or about anyone in your life. And McKelvey and Palfrey's centipede-game experiments add a warning: strict backward induction predicted play would stop immediately, yet participants usually continued for several rounds, which made more sense if they allowed for a chance the other player might be altruistic. Backward reasoning is only as good as the model of the other player feeding it.

## The tool: The Response Map

Part 1 is the chapter's practical exercise for an interaction that went sideways. Part 2 turns the chapter's questions into a pre-move check.

**Part 1 — Rewind a past interaction**

Pick a recent interaction that didn't go the way you wanted. Don't judge who was right yet; just write the sequence.

1. What did I do? ______________________________
2. What did they do? ______________________________
3. What did I do next? ______________________________
4. What did they do next? ______________________________
5. (Continue if needed) ______________________________

- At which point did the interaction change direction? ______________________________
- What could I have predicted about their response before making my move? ______________________________

**Part 2 — Map an upcoming move**

1. **My planned move** (what I'm about to say or do): ______________________________
2. **What I want them to do** (my intention): ______________________________
3. **Their side of the board.** Given *their* preferences, not mine:
   - Their goals: ______________________________
   - What they know (and don't): ______________________________
   - Their options: ______________________________
   - Their constraints (boss, money, time, reputation, emotions): ______________________________
4. **What does this move make them want to do?** Several plausible responses, each marked:
   - Response A: ______________________________ (likely / possible / unlikely)
   - Response B: ______________________________ (likely / possible / unlikely)
   - Response C: ______________________________ (likely / possible / unlikely)
5. **Intention check:** Does my move actually make the response I want more attractive than their alternatives? ______________________________
6. **If they do that, what will I do?** (One line per response above.) ______________________________
7. **Where does the sequence lead?** (High stakes only.) ______________________________
8. **Eliminate:** Is any option worse for me regardless of what they do? ______________________________
9. **Threat check:** If a threat is involved (theirs or mine), would carrying it out be the best response when the moment comes? ______________________________
10. **Pattern and relevance:** What pattern will this move create? Which response could materially change the outcome? ______________________________
11. **Afterward — update:** What did they actually do? Where was my prediction off, and what did I misunderstand about the game? ______________________________

## Practice exercises

### Exercise A — Analyze the scenario

Dana leads a five-person operations team at a logistics company. At her first team meeting she said, "If something goes wrong, tell me right away. No surprises." Two weeks later, Marcus, a junior coordinator, admitted he had booked a shipment to the wrong warehouse, delaying a client order by two days. Dana was under pressure from her own boss that week. She raised her voice, called it "exactly the kind of careless mistake I can't afford," and later cited the error, by name, in the weekly team meeting. Marcus fixed the booking and apologized, and Dana felt she had set a clear standard. A month later, a much larger error surfaced only when the client called to complain; two team members had known about it for a week. Dana told her boss, "I don't understand it. I specifically told them to come to me."

1. What did Dana intend her reaction to produce, and what did it actually make attractive for the team?
2. Write the sequence of moves. Where did the interaction change direction?
3. Why is "I specifically told them to come to me" beside the point strategically?
4. Reasoning backward: if Dana wants early disclosure, what must the team expect, and where does that expectation come from?
5. What could Dana do now, and what responses should she anticipate?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

1. **Intention vs. incentive.** Dana intended to set a high standard. But her response raised the cost of disclosure: an angry reaction plus public embarrassment, witnessed by the whole team. Hiding or delaying bad news became the more attractive response. She didn't want concealment, but her move made it rational.

2. **The sequence.** Dana: "Tell me right away." → Marcus discloses. → Dana explodes and names him publicly. → The team learns disclosure is punished. → Next error: two people sit on it for a week. → The client finds out first. The turning point was Dana's response to the disclosure, the moment her stated rule and her actual behavior pointed in opposite directions.

3. **Words vs. the game they enter.** Her team heard her perfectly. They responded to the game her words entered, and her own reaction rewrote that game. Today's response became tomorrow's incentive.

4. **Backward reasoning.** Desired behavior: early reporting. Required expectation: disclosure will be heard and worked through, not punished. Source of that expectation: Dana's observable responses to disclosure, especially the most recent, most public one. The question isn't "What phrase will make them open up?" but "What does my response pattern teach them to expect?"

5. **Next move.** Dana could tell the team plainly that her reaction to Marcus made it harder to bring problems forward, then respond differently the next time bad news arrives: thank the person, focus on the fix, handle performance concerns privately. Likely responses: cautious skepticism (one statement won't outweigh one vivid example) or a small "test" disclosure. That test is the critical branch; her response to it is what actually changes the game. She should model → act → observe → update, and if reporting stays slow, ask what else (workload, peer dynamics) makes silence attractive. The goal isn't controlling the team; it's an honest environment in which reporting problems is a reasonable choice for them.

</details>

### Exercise B — Your own game

Choose a live situation where you'll make an important move in the next week or two: a request, hard feedback, a boundary, a negotiation, or a reply you're tempted to send fast.

1. Complete Part 2 of the Response Map. If you catch yourself writing "If I were them, I'd...", rewrite it from their payoffs.
2. List at least three plausible responses and mark each likely, possible, or unlikely. Replace certainty with branches.
3. For the most likely response, write what you'll do, then what they'll likely do next. Stop there unless the stakes are high.
4. Intention check: does your move make the response you want genuinely more attractive to them? If not, what change (timing, setting, framing, what you offer or ask) would?
5. Elimination check: cross off any option that's worse for you no matter how they respond.
6. Afterward, complete step 11 and write one sentence on what you misunderstood about the game, if anything.

### Exercise C — Partner drill / role-play

**Format:** Pairs (or one learner with an AI practice partner), 12–15 minutes.

**Setup:** Jordan, a small business owner, wants to cut a freelance designer's monthly rate. Riley, the designer, wants to keep it.

- **Jordan (private):** You want a 15% cut and have told yourself you'll "walk away" if Riley refuses. But replacing Riley would take at least six weeks, and your product launch is in four.
- **Riley (private):** Other clients fill about 70% of your capacity. Losing Jordan would hurt but not sink you. You suspect Jordan has a launch coming but don't know when.

**Round 1 (4 min):** Jordan opens: "I need a 15% cut, or I'll have to find someone else." Play it out.

**Round 2 (4 min):** First, 60 seconds of silent prep. Jordan: "If Riley says no, will walking away really be my best response? What does my opening make Riley want to do?" Riley: "If I refuse, what will Jordan actually do at that moment?" Replay with new openings.

**Debrief (5 min):**
- Was Jordan's Round 1 threat credible? How did Riley decide?
- What did each player predict, and how did it compare with what happened?
- Where did either player project their own preferences onto the other?
- Did Jordan's Round 2 opening make the response Jordan wanted more attractive to Riley, or just state it more firmly?
- Ethics check: did anyone make a threat they didn't believe? What might that cost in an ongoing relationship?

## Common mistakes

- **Judging a move by your intention** — "I was only being honest" may be true, but strategy asks what your move makes the other person want to do.
- **Projecting your preferences** — "If I were them, I'd never..." uses your payoffs. Use their goals, information, options, and constraints.
- **Assuming perfect rationality** — People get emotional and misread the game. Separate what's optimal for them from what's likely.
- **Judging threats by forcefulness** — Check whether carrying out the threat would be their best response when the moment arrives.
- **Overthinking** — Skip the 47-step tree. Focus on responses that could materially change the outcome.
- **Overpredicting** — Your model is a hypothesis. When surprised, ask "What did I misunderstand about the game?", not "Why are they irrational?"
- **Forgetting they're predicting you** — A predictable response, like always giving in to pressure, becomes part of their strategy.
- **Mistaking influence for control** — You can shape the environment; other people still choose their own responses.

## Field assignment

**This week, run "Then what?" before three moves that matter** (a request, a reply to a frustrating message, feedback, a "no"). Before each, take 60 seconds to write:

- My move:
- What I want them to do:
- Their two or three most plausible responses (with likely / possible / unlikely):
- If they do the most likely thing, what will I do?

Afterward, record what they actually did and whether your prediction was right, partly right, or wrong. Once this week, also notice a feedback loop in progress and note whether it was escalating or settling.

**Debrief questions for next week:**
1. How often did your prediction match the response? When it didn't, what had you misunderstood about the game?
2. Did asking "Then what?" change any move before you made it? What did you change, and what happened?
3. In the feedback loop you observed, where could one well-chosen response have changed the direction of the sequence?
