# Module 6 — Map Information & Beliefs

> **The question:** "What do they know—or believe—that I don't?"

| | |
|---|---|

| **Time** | ~45 min of video lessons · ~60 min of workbook · one week of field practice |

## Why this module matters

You and another person can be standing in exactly the same situation and still be playing very different games, because you have different information. You may know something they don't. They may know something you don't. You may both know something, but one of you may not know that the other knows it. And sometimes what matters most isn't what is actually true. It's what each person believes is true.

Take the used car. The seller knows the car has a serious mechanical problem; you don't. You think you're negotiating over "What is this car worth?" The seller knows you're really negotiating over "How much can I get you to pay before you discover the problem?" Same car, same conversation, different information, and therefore a very different game. Reverse it: if you've already been approved for financing elsewhere and the seller doesn't know, your alternatives look different to you than they do to the seller. The information structure changes the game.

The previous modules taught you to see the game, the payoffs, the responses, the patterns others learn about you, and the equilibrium those patterns create. This module goes one level deeper: to the beliefs underneath the behavior. Communication is, in large part, an attempt to change what another person knows or believes. Before you can communicate well, you need an honest map of who knows what, who believes what, and what you are merely assuming.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this module you will be able to:

1. Distinguish what is actually true, what each player knows, what each player believes, and what you are assuming.
2. Identify information asymmetries in everyday interactions and ask what each side knows that the other doesn't.
3. Evaluate a statement or signal by asking what the speaker gains, what it costs them, and why you should believe it.
4. Recognize higher-order beliefs and the difference between shared information and common knowledge.
5. Treat your interpretations of other people's behavior as revisable hypotheses, and name the evidence that would change your mind.

## Lessons

### Lesson 6.1 — Information Is Not the Same as Truth (~10 min)

**Teaching points**
- There is what is actually true, and there is what each player believes is true. They aren't necessarily the same.
- People make decisions based on the game as they perceive it, not the game as it objectively is.
- Beliefs about what other people will do can change what you do, and that can help create the very outcome people feared.
- This is not "perception is reality." Truth determines whether a belief is accurate, and inaccurate beliefs can collapse when reality produces contradictory evidence. The precise claim: perception is part of the causal structure of strategic interaction.

**Example to use:** The bank run. You believe your bank may fail; you don't know whether it's true, but you know that if enough depositors withdraw, it *could* fail. So you withdraw. Others see you, get nervous, and withdraw too. The outcome is shaped not only by the bank's actual condition but by what depositors believe other depositors will do. Pair it with the company doing well while employees believe layoffs are coming: they start job hunting, stop taking risks, and become less committed, which may then affect the company.

**On-screen takeaway:** > **People make decisions based on the game as they perceive it.**

### Lesson 6.2 — The Information Gap (~10 min)

**Teaching points**
- Information asymmetry: one player knows something relevant that another doesn't. It is everywhere: doctor and patient, mechanic and customer, lawyer and client, employee and manager, child and parent.
- Information has strategic value. That's why people ask questions, investigate, stay silent, reveal selectively, observe, and test one another. They're trying to improve their model of the game.
- "Just ask" has a problem: the other person gets to decide what to tell you, and they may have an incentive to tell you what benefits them.
- In formal terms, players can have private information about their "type." In plain terms, a type is something important about me that you don't know: skilled or inexperienced, patient or desperate, willing to walk away or not. You have to infer it, and I know you're trying to.

**Example to use:** The job interview. The candidate knows how capable and hardworking they really are; the employer has to infer it from résumés, references, work samples, and answers. But the employer also knows the candidate has an incentive to present themselves favorably, so claims aren't automatically treated as facts. The employer has to separate information from self-interested persuasion.

**On-screen takeaway:** > **How do you make decisions when someone else knows something you don't?**

### Lesson 6.3 — Signals and Cheap Talk (~10 min)

**Teaching points**
- A signal is an action or piece of information that helps another player infer something they can't directly observe. Michael Spence's job-market model showed how education can function as a signal when acquiring it costs different types differently.
- A signal is evidence, not proof. Its value depends on how informative it actually is.
- Costly signals can be more informative because different types of players face different costs or benefits from sending them. The everyday question: what does this person have to lose by making this claim?
- Cheap talk is communication that doesn't directly change the material payoffs. It isn't useless. It can carry real information, especially when the speaker's interests are aligned with the listener's. The key question is: why should I believe you?
- The same words can carry different information depending on the speaker, incentives, history, context, alternatives, and consequences.

**Example to use:** "I'm extremely confident" costs almost nothing to say. Putting $10,000 of your own money behind the prediction is different. Similarly, "I really value our relationship" is a cheap statement, while repeated sacrifices, kept commitments, and showing up when it's hard are far more informative. And when two salespeople each say "This is our best price," the one with a reputation for transparency is telling you more than the one known for exaggeration.

**On-screen takeaway:** > **A signal is evidence, not proof.**

### Lesson 6.4 — Beliefs About Beliefs and Common Knowledge (~8 min)

**Teaching points**
- Higher-order beliefs (belief hierarchies): you have beliefs about what the other person believes about you, and so on. "I believe you think I'm bluffing."
- Sometimes you're not responding to what someone knows. You're responding to what you think they know about what you know.
- A bluff works not because it's true but because the other player believes it enough to act differently.
- Mistaken beliefs can sustain a stable pattern. If an equilibrium rests on incorrect beliefs, changing the information may change the equilibrium.
- Common knowledge: we both know something, and we both know that we both know it, and so on. Coordination can fail not from lack of information but from lack of shared knowledge that the information is shared.

**Example to use:** Two departments. A believes "B will never cooperate," so A stops sharing information. B sees this, concludes "They don't trust us," and stops sharing too. A says, "See? We were right." Neither started with malice; the belief changed behavior and the behavior reinforced the belief. For common knowledge, use the room of ten people: if everyone hears "At noon we all leave the building," coordination is easy. If only half hear it, those who heard don't know whether everyone else did.

**On-screen takeaway:** > **Beliefs can influence actions, and actions can reinforce beliefs.**

### Lesson 6.5 — Hypotheses, Not Facts (~7 min)

**Teaching points**
- Don't confuse uncertainty with deception. You may not know what someone is thinking because they're withholding it, because they don't know themselves, because the information isn't available, because you haven't asked, or because you've misread ambiguous evidence.
- The healthier question: what information do I actually have, and how confident should I be in the conclusions I'm drawing from it?
- Treat your first interpretation as a hypothesis. What evidence would support it? What would contradict it? What else could explain the behavior?
- Humans are good at forming an interpretation and then hunting for confirmation. Your model of the other player should be revisable.

**Example to use:** You text someone, "Can we talk?" No reply. You know very little, but your mind generates explanations: angry, busy, didn't see it, avoiding you, something happened. That belief drives your next move (another text, withdrawal, anger), and now your belief about their behavior is influencing their future behavior. Uncertainty can create conflict even when neither person intends it.

**On-screen takeaway:** > **Your model of the other player should be revisable.**

## Key terms

| Term | Plain-language meaning |
|---|---|
| Information structure | Who knows what in an interaction. Change it and you can change the game. |
| Information asymmetry | One player knows something relevant that another player doesn't. |
| Incomplete information | Uncertainty about other players' payoffs or types. |
| Type | Something important about a player that others don't directly observe and have to infer (skill, patience, desperation, trustworthiness, willingness to walk away). |
| Signal | An action or piece of information that helps another player infer something they don't directly observe. Evidence, not proof. |
| Costly signal | A signal that would cost the sender something, often more informative because different types face different costs for sending it. |
| Cheap talk | Communication that doesn't directly change the material payoffs. It can still be informative, especially when interests are aligned. |
| Higher-order beliefs | Beliefs about what others believe, including what they believe about your beliefs. Also called belief hierarchies. |
| Common knowledge | We both know something, we both know that we both know it, and so on. Especially important for coordination. |
| Self-fulfilling belief | A belief that changes behavior in a way that produces the evidence that seems to confirm it. |

## Research spotlight

**Tadelis and Zettelmeyer, wholesale auto auctions (American Economic Review).** The researchers ran a large-scale field experiment in wholesale car auctions, randomly disclosing quality information about some vehicles. Both good and bad news could increase competition and revenue, because buyers differed in which cars suited them, and better information improved the matching between buyers and vehicles. The cars didn't change. The buyers didn't change. The information changed, and so the game changed.

*What it suggests for real conversations:* Changing the information structure can change behavior and outcomes, sometimes in ways people don't expect. Disclosing an unflattering fact isn't automatically a loss; it can help the right counterpart find you.

*What it does NOT prove:* It does not show that more information always improves outcomes. The effect depends on the game. It's evidence from a specific market setting, not a universal rule that disclosure always pays.

**Costa-Gomes and Weizsäcker, stated beliefs and play (Review of Economic Studies).** Participants played 14 one-shot strategic games and separately reported what they expected their opponents to do. If people simply chose the best response to their own stated beliefs, the two should line up. They didn't: on average, participants failed to best respond to their own stated beliefs in almost half the games, and their stated beliefs often reflected deeper strategic reasoning than their actual choices.

*What it suggests for real conversations:* Beliefs matter, but people don't always translate beliefs into consistent action. Knowing what someone believes is useful, not a crystal ball. That includes you: your own stated beliefs may not be what is actually driving your moves.

*What it does NOT prove:* It doesn't show that beliefs are irrelevant, or that people are random. And it doesn't support the claim that correctly identifying someone's belief lets you reliably predict their behavior. A related methodological note in the chapter's research (Rutström and Wilcox) found that asking people for their beliefs with incentives could alter early play in one repeated game under some conditions, a reminder that even measuring beliefs can affect the game.

## Practice exercises

### Exercise A — Analyze the scenario

Dana manages a five-person analytics team. On Friday, the quarterly report Jordan owned went out two days late, and Dana's director asked her about it in front of other managers. Dana had told the team on Monday that "the director is watching this one closely," but she said it in a quick hallway exchange that Jordan wasn't part of. Jordan had sent a message mid-week saying "Still waiting on finance's numbers," and Dana, skimming it on her phone, read it as a routine status update. On Friday afternoon, Dana writes to Jordan: "We need to talk Monday about ownership." Jordan, who stayed late Thursday rebuilding the model after finance finally delivered, reads the message as a sign that Dana thinks he's careless. Over the weekend, he tells a friend, "She already knows finance was late. She just wants someone to blame." Dana, meanwhile, believes Jordan didn't flag the risk because he doesn't take the director's priorities seriously.

1. Sort what Dana knows (facts) from what she believes and what she's assuming.
2. What information asymmetries exist in each direction?
3. Identify at least one higher-order belief and one place where Dana treated shared information as common knowledge.
4. How could the beliefs on both sides become self-fulfilling?
5. What information would change either person's decision, and how should Dana open Monday's conversation?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

1. **Dana's facts:** the report was two days late; the director raised it publicly; Jordan sent a mid-week message mentioning finance's numbers. **Her beliefs:** Jordan didn't flag the risk; he doesn't take the director's priorities seriously. **Her assumptions:** that Jordan knew the director was watching closely, and that his message was routine. Her confidence in "he doesn't take it seriously" sits at "I suspect," but she's acting as if it's "I know."

2. **Jordan knows what Dana doesn't:** finance delivered late, he worked late Thursday, and his mid-week message was meant as a warning. **Dana knows what Jordan doesn't:** the director's heightened attention and the public embarrassment on Friday. Each is reacting to a different game.

3. **Higher-order belief:** Jordan believes Dana *already knows* finance was late and is choosing to blame him anyway. He's responding not to what Dana knows but to what he thinks she knows. **Common knowledge failure:** Dana said "the director is watching" in a hallway Jordan wasn't part of. The information existed on the team, but it wasn't shared knowledge that everyone had it. She assumed it was in Jordan's information set without evidence.

4. If Dana walks in treating her hypothesis as fact, Jordan will likely become defensive and guarded, which Dana may read as confirmation that he doesn't care. Jordan, expecting blame, may stop sending early warnings ("What's the point?"), which confirms Dana's belief that he doesn't flag risks. This is the two-departments pattern in miniature.

5. Decision-relevant information: when finance actually delivered, what Jordan's mid-week message was meant to signal, and whether Jordan knew about the director's attention. Dana should open by naming facts and asking, not concluding: "The report went out two days late and the director raised it with me. I'm not sure I have the full picture. Walk me through what happened with finance's numbers, and tell me if you'd heard the director was watching this one." She should also share what she knows, since Jordan can't respond to information he never had. Neither of them needs to deceive or pressure anyone; both need a more accurate map.

</details>

### Exercise B — Your own game

Pick a live situation where someone's behavior is confusing, frustrating, or worrying you: a coworker who has gone quiet, a family member who reacted oddly, a client who stopped responding, a negotiation that stalled.

1. Write one sentence describing what happened, using only observable facts. No adjectives about motives.
2. Write your current interpretation. Label it: "Hypothesis."
3. Work through all five parts of the Information Map.
4. List at least three other explanations for the behavior. Use the chapter's list: are they withholding, do they not know themselves, is the information unavailable, have you not asked, or have you misread ambiguous evidence?
5. For your hypothesis, write one piece of evidence that would support it and one that would contradict it.
6. Identify one thing you know that they may not, and decide whether sharing it would change the game for the better.
7. Decide on a single next step that gathers decision-relevant information honestly, such as a direct question or a check-in, rather than a move based on an untested belief.

### Exercise C — Partner drill / role-play

**Time:** 15 minutes. **Players:** two people (or one person plus an AI practice partner), plus an optional observer.

**Setup:** A small business owner (Player 1) is considering hiring a freelance designer (Player 2) for a three-month website project.

**Player 1 privately knows:** The budget is firm at the quoted amount, but the deadline is more flexible than the posting suggests. A previous freelancer missed deadlines badly, so Player 1 is nervous about reliability. Player 1 has one other candidate, who is cheaper but less experienced.

**Player 2 privately knows:** They're strong at design but have never built a site this size. They have another offer that starts in six weeks and would overlap. They really want this client for their portfolio.

**Round 1 (5 min):** Have the conversation. Each player tries to learn what matters and decides what to share. No lying about material facts; you may keep things private.

**Round 2 (5 min):** Stop. Each player silently fills in Part 1 of the Information Map: what I know, what I think they know, what I think they believe about me. Then compare maps out loud. Where were you right? Where were you wrong?

**Round 3 (5 min):** Replay the last part of the conversation, this time asking the one question whose answer would most change your decision.

**Debrief questions**
- Which of your beliefs about the other person turned out to be assumptions?
- Which claims did you treat as strong evidence, and which as cheap talk? What made the difference?
- What did you keep private, and was that different from deceiving? Did the other person have what they needed for an informed choice?
- What single piece of information changed the game most when it came out?

## Common mistakes

- **Promoting a hypothesis to a fact.** Forming an interpretation is natural. The error is treating it as settled before you have evidence. Label it a hypothesis, name what would contradict it, and update as information arrives.
- **Assuming "they know."** "He knows why I'm upset" is often an assumption. A person cannot respond to information they never had. Ask what evidence you have that it's in their information set.
- **Taking statements at face value, or dismissing them entirely.** Words aren't automatically facts, and cheap talk isn't automatically worthless. Evaluate the speaker, incentives, history, and context, and ask why you should believe it.
- **Treating a signal as proof.** A degree, a confident tone, or a single gesture is evidence. Its weight depends on how informative it is and what it cost to send.
- **Assuming uncertainty means deception.** Not knowing what someone thinks doesn't mean they're hiding something. They may not know themselves, the information may not exist yet, or you may not have asked.
- **Confusing shared information with common knowledge.** Telling half the room isn't the same as everyone knowing that everyone knows. If coordination matters, make the information public and mutual.
- **Saying "perception is reality."** Beliefs drive behavior, but truth still decides whether beliefs are accurate, and false beliefs can collapse when reality pushes back.
- **Gathering more information instead of the right information.** Ask what would actually change your decision, and go after that.

## Field assignment

This week, catch yourself in one moment of confusion about someone's behavior: an unanswered message, a cool response in a meeting, an unexpected "no." Before you react, take two minutes and run the Information Map, at least Part 1 and the confidence labels in Part 2. Then take one honest step to get decision-relevant information: ask a direct question, share something you know that they might not, or verify a claim you were relying on.

Record: the situation; your first interpretation; your confidence label before; what you learned; and whether your belief survived, changed, or collapsed.

**Debrief questions for next week**
1. How far apart were your first interpretation and what you eventually learned?
2. Did you discover any information the other person lacked, or any you lacked? What changed once it was shared?
3. Where else in your life might a belief, yours or theirs, be sustaining a pattern that new information could change?

## The question to carry

> **"What do they know—or believe—that I don't?"**
>
> Then ask: What do I actually know? What am I assuming? What might they be assuming? What information would change either of our decisions? And when someone tells you something important, don't stop at "What did they say?" Ask: **"Why should I believe it?"**
