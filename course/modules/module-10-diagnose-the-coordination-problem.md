# Module 10 — Diagnose the Coordination Problem

> **The question:** Are we actually in conflict—or are we failing to coordinate?

| | |
|---|---|
| **Book reading** | Chapter 10, "Diagnose the Coordination Problem" |
| **Time** | ~40 min of video lessons · ~60 min of workbook · one week of field practice |
| **Tool you'll build** | The Coordination Diagnostic |

## Why this module matters

Sometimes two people end up with a bad outcome even though neither of them wanted it. Not because one betrayed the other. Not because their interests are opposed. Not because someone failed to persuade. Sometimes the problem is much simpler: they failed to coordinate.

That distinction matters. Misdiagnose a coordination problem as a conflict, and you can spend enormous energy persuading someone who already wants essentially what you want. Misdiagnose a free-rider problem as coordination, and you can write the clearest plan in the world and watch it fail, because everyone knows exactly what to do and still has a reason not to do it.

This module trains one mental shift. Instead of asking "Why won't they cooperate?", you separate three possibilities: **conflict** (we genuinely want different outcomes), **coordination failure** (we could both benefit from compatible actions, but we aren't aligned on what each of us will do), and **social dilemma** (we all like the collective outcome, but each of us has an incentive to let others carry the cost). They look remarkably similar from the outside. They are not the same game, and they do not have the same solution.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this module you will be able to:

1. Distinguish conflict, coordination failure, and social dilemma using the chapter's simple test.
2. Identify focal points and conventions that help a group select one workable option among several.
3. Explain the difference between everyone knowing something and everyone knowing that everyone knows it.
4. Locate where coordination is likely to break, especially at handoffs, ambiguous deadlines, and unowned tasks.
5. Match an intervention (shared plan, public commitment, clear owner, standard, deadline, focal point, or information about others' intentions) to the problem you have actually diagnosed.

## Lessons

### Lesson 10.1 — Not Every Disagreement Is a Conflict (~8 min)

**Teaching points**
- A coordination game is a situation in which players benefit when their actions fit together. In a pure coordination game, which outcome you land on may matter less than everyone choosing the same one.
- Driving on the right and on the left both work. The disaster is half the people choosing each.
- So the question is often not "Which option is objectively better?" but "Which option can we all reliably choose together?"
- Real life is often mixed. In the Battle of the Sexes structure (the name is dated; the structure is useful), two people want to spend the evening together but prefer different events. They need to coordinate, and they disagree over which equilibrium to coordinate on. Founders, co-parents, and departments live here.
- "We all want the same thing" doesn't settle the strategic problem. Ask: where exactly do our interests align, and where do they diverge?

**Example to use:** The dinner problem. We both prefer eating together to eating alone; I slightly prefer the Italian restaurant, you slightly prefer the steakhouse. What matters most is ending up at the same restaurant. Contrast it with one available reservation that we both want for separate events: same word, "disagreement," very different game.

**On-screen takeaway:** > **Not every disagreement is a conflict of interests.**

### Lesson 10.2 — Focal Points and Conventions (~9 min)

**Teaching points**
- Thomas Schelling asked: you must meet someone in New York City tomorrow and cannot communicate. Where do you go? People look for the obvious: a landmark, a central station, a conventional time. They ask, "What place will the other person think I will think is obvious?" These are **focal points**.
- Players don't need to prefer the focal point. They only need to believe the other person will recognize it too. Asked to match a positive whole number, many people pick 1. It isn't mathematically superior; it stands out.
- When several solutions would work, the easiest one for everyone to recognize may beat the theoretically perfect one. A slightly inferior standard used by everyone can be worth more than a superior standard used by half the group. Quality still matters; coordination itself also has value.
- Conventions save strategic energy. Without them, groups re-solve the same game daily: Who runs the meeting? Who follows up? A convention says, "This is what we do here."
- Fifty-fifty can be a coordination device as well as a fairness preference: it is extremely easy for both sides to anticipate.
- Sometimes the best choice is simply to pick something: alternate, rotate, flip a coin. The rule just needs to be a mutually understood way of selecting one equilibrium.
- Bad conventions can be stable too. Everyone may follow a rule only because everyone expects everyone else to.

**Example to use:** The project-management system. System A is slightly better; System B is already used by everyone. You could spend weeks proving A's features, but if half the organization stays on B, you've created a coordination failure.

**On-screen takeaway:** > **The easiest solution for everyone to recognize may beat the theoretically perfect one.**

### Lesson 10.3 — Strategic Uncertainty (~8 min)

**Teaching points**
- Ordinary uncertainty asks, "Will it rain?" **Strategic uncertainty** asks, "What are the other players going to do?"
- When switching alone is costly, everyone waits for everyone else. Management concludes "employees resist change," when perhaps each employee was willing to change, conditional on everyone else changing too.
- Sometimes people aren't unwilling to cooperate. They're unwilling to cooperate alone.
- A shared goal tells people where they want to end up. It doesn't tell them how everyone will get there.
- Cheap talk, weak as proof (Chapter 7), can be powerful as coordination. Different jobs.
- Before saying "We need better communication," ask what it should accomplish: information, credibility, persuasion, bargaining, or coordination? Two people at different airport terminals can fix their problem in thirty seconds. Two people arguing over a $100,000 payment can talk for hours and only clarify the disagreement.

**Example to use:** Five departments each prefer the new reporting procedure if all five use it, but switching first means extra work, so each waits. When management publicly announces "Every department switches Monday" and everyone can see the migration plan, the calculation changes, not because anyone was persuaded, but because uncertainty about the others dropped.

**On-screen takeaway:** > **Sometimes people aren't unwilling to cooperate. They're unwilling to cooperate alone.**

### Lesson 10.4 — Common Knowledge, Owners, and Handoffs (~8 min)

**Teaching points**
- There's a difference between everyone knowing something and everyone knowing that everyone knows it. The stronger condition is **common knowledge**.
- "We're moving the meeting to 3:00," said privately, leaves you wondering whether others heard. Said to the whole room, nothing about the meeting changes, but the mutual awareness does. Public announcements, dashboards, visible schedules, and rituals can create shared expectations, not just transmit information.
- "Everyone knows what to do" is often false. The question isn't "Have I said it?" but "Do we share the same expectation about what happens next?" Two people both hear "Thursday"; one means finished, the other means draft review.
- "Paul owns this by 3 PM" changes the game: Paul knows, everyone knows, and Paul knows everyone knows.
- Coordination often fails at the handoff. Inspect not just each person's performance but the connection between tasks.
- Roles, deadlines, agendas, and chains of command are coordination technology. Leadership often means creating a shared coordination structure ("You call them. Everyone meets back here at six") rather than persuading.

**Example to use:** The project nobody owned. Everyone agrees to follow up with a customer today. Tomorrow: "Did anyone call them?" Silence. Sales thought customer service would; customer service thought sales owned it; the owner thought the sales manager had assigned it. Before judging character, ask: was there actually a mutually understood assignment?

**On-screen takeaway:** > **Sometimes coordination requires making expectations mutually visible.**

### Lesson 10.5 — Coordination or Social Dilemma? (~7 min)

**Teaching points**
- Shared goals don't always mean coordination. Four roommates want a clean apartment, but each would rather someone else cleaned. That's a free-rider, public-goods problem: a **social dilemma**.
- The simple test: "If I knew for certain that everyone else was going to cooperate, would I want to cooperate too?" Yes, absolutely: likely coordination. "I'd still be better off letting everyone else do the work": social dilemma.
- The fix changes with the diagnosis. Coordination calls for clearer expectations, shared information, a focal point, a standard. Free riding may require changing incentives, monitoring, reputation, repeated interaction, commitments, or the rules.
- Focal points have limits. When players strongly disagree about which outcome they prefer, salience may not be enough. A focal point can solve uncertainty; it cannot erase incompatible interests.
- Coordination is morally neutral; groups can coordinate around harm. Keep the foundation: truth, autonomy, dignity, informed choice.

**Example to use:** If three roommates will definitely clean, the fourth may think, "Great. Then I don't have to." They coordinated just fine. The individual incentive is the problem.

**On-screen takeaway:** > **Diagnose first. The same symptom can come from three different games.**

## Key terms

| Term | Plain-language meaning |
|---|---|
| Coordination game | A situation in which players benefit when their actions fit together. |
| Coordination failure | We could both benefit from compatible actions, but we aren't aligned on what each of us will do. |
| Conflict | We genuinely want different, incompatible outcomes. |
| Social dilemma / free-rider problem | We all like the collective outcome, but each of us has an incentive to let others carry more of the cost. |
| Mixed-motive game | Players want to coordinate but disagree about which outcome to coordinate on (e.g., Battle of the Sexes). |
| Focal point | An option that stands out so each player expects the other to recognize it too. |
| Convention | A shared rule that solves a repeated coordination problem: "This is what we do here." |
| Strategic uncertainty | Uncertainty about what the other players will do. |
| Common knowledge | Everyone knows something, and everyone knows that everyone knows it. |

## Research spotlight

**1. Focal points: Schelling; Mehta, Starmer, and Sugden**

*What was done.* Schelling posed matching puzzles without communication, such as choosing a meeting place in New York or picking the same positive integer. Judith Mehta, Chris Starmer, and Robert Sugden later tested focal points experimentally in pure coordination games, where any option works as long as both players match.

*What was found.* People coordinated far above random chance by using shared culture, context, and salience; the Nobel committee specifically highlighted this part of Schelling's work. Mehta, Starmer, and Sugden found evidence that people use salience rather than choosing randomly among equivalent options.

*What it suggests.* When a group has several workable options, the one everyone recognizes most easily (the existing standard, the obvious default) has real strategic value.

*What it does NOT prove.* It doesn't show the most salient option is the best one; it may just be easiest to coordinate around. And salience has limits: experiments with asymmetric payoffs, where players prefer different outcomes, find lower coordination than pure coordination settings, and later studies (such as Parravano and Poulsen; Rojo Arjona, Sitzia, and Zheng) found that higher stakes or stronger salience did not reliably help once conflicts of interest were built in.

**2. Communication as coordination: Cooper et al.; the Senegal cooperatives**

*What was done.* Russell Cooper, Douglas DeJong, Robert Forsythe, and Thomas Ross ran lab coordination games where players could send nonbinding messages first. In the field, Aflagah, Bernard, and Viceisza worked with 79 groundnut cooperatives in Senegal, running coordination games with 839 farmers and then a natural field experiment with roughly 2,800 members. They asked members how much they intended to sell through the cooperative and revealed the group's aggregate intentions.

*What was found.* Nonbinding communication often substantially improved coordination: one-way communication increased selection of a Pareto-superior equilibrium in some conditions, and in another type of game two-way communication consistently produced the Pareto-dominant one. In Senegal, treated farmers contributed more than twice as much toward collective selling as the control group on average, and the effect was larger in bigger groups. No new incentive was added; what changed was information about what everyone else intended to do.

*What it suggests.* When people already want an outcome but doubt others will act, making intentions visible can move behavior.

*What it does NOT prove.* Communication did not eliminate every coordination failure. Neither result supports "talk more and every group problem disappears." Communication helps when it resolves the uncertainty actually blocking coordination; it won't dissolve a real conflict or a free-rider incentive. And shared goals alone don't guarantee action: the farmers already saw the benefits and still held back.

## Practice exercises

### Exercise A — Analyze the scenario

Dana runs a twelve-person marketing team at a regional credit union. Three months ago, leadership approved a shared content calendar to replace the team's spreadsheets and email threads. Dana's kickoff email said, "Everyone should start using the new calendar when you can." Today only two people use it. The others say they like the tool but keep their spreadsheets "because that's where everyone else's dates are." Last week two staff members scheduled competing promotional emails for the same morning. A member newsletter also went out without its compliance review: the writer assumed the designer had sent it for sign-off, and the designer assumed the writer had. Dana's director says, "Your team doesn't take ownership and resists change. Motivate them." Dana is drafting a speech about accountability.

1. Is the calendar problem primarily conflict, coordination failure, or social dilemma? Apply the simple test.
2. What is blocking adoption, and why didn't the kickoff email solve it?
3. Where exactly did the newsletter process break?
4. Is a motivational speech the right intervention? What would you do instead?
5. Could any part of this turn out *not* to be coordination?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

1. **Mostly coordination failure.** "If I knew everyone else would use the calendar, would I?" The team's own words ("that's where everyone else's dates are") suggest yes. They aren't opposed to the tool; they're unwilling to switch alone, because a calendar one person uses is worse than spreadsheets everyone checks.

2. **Strategic uncertainty.** "When you can" gave no shared date, no plan, and no visible sign anyone else was switching. Each person read it privately and couldn't tell whether the others would act, so everyone waited. From outside, that looks like resistance to change.

3. **The handoff, with no clear owner.** Nobody opposed the review. Each held a reasonable but incompatible expectation about who sends it. The individual tasks were done; the connection between them failed.

4. **No, motivation isn't the bottleneck.** Better: announce in a team meeting, so everyone sees everyone hear it, "Starting Monday the 3rd, the shared calendar is the only calendar; spreadsheets are retired." Name an owner for keeping it current, define what "scheduled" means, and review the calendar briefly at each weekly meeting so people can see others using it. The calendar becomes the focal point, which also prevents colliding sends. For the newsletter: "The writer submits for compliance review; nothing is released without sign-off."

5. **Possibly.** If, after expectations are public and clear, some people still skip the calendar because updating it is extra work that mostly helps others, that looks like a social dilemma. Then clarity may not be enough; Dana may need to build updating into the workflow or make it visible. Diagnose first, and re-diagnose if the fix doesn't hold.

</details>

### Exercise B — Your own game

Pick a live situation where people "can't seem to get on the same page": a household chore, a team handoff, a family schedule, a volunteer group.

1. Describe the bad outcome in one sentence, without blaming anyone's character.
2. Work through the Coordination Diagnostic.
3. On step 2, answer honestly for yourself first. If your answer is "I'd rather someone else did it," say so; it changes the diagnosis.
4. Write the one sentence that makes the next move obvious, in the style of "Paul owns this by 3 PM."
5. Decide how you'll make it mutually visible: in front of everyone, in a shared document, on a visible schedule.
6. If it's something you renegotiate every week, draft a convention everyone can know before the next conflict arises.

### Exercise C — Partner drill / role-play

**Format:** Pairs or small groups, 12–15 minutes; an AI practice partner can play Player B.

**Setup:** A shared office kitchen keeps getting left messy. Player A facilitates a short conversation to fix it. Player B privately draws one card:

- **Card 1 — Coordination:** "I'd happily clean on a set day, but there's no schedule and I never know if someone already did it."
- **Card 2 — Social dilemma:** "If others clean, I'll enjoy it and not bother."
- **Card 3 — Mixed:** "I support a rotation, but I want Fridays excluded since I'm remote then; others want everyone equal on every day."

**Rules:** Player A has 7 minutes to diagnose the card using questions, not accusations, including some version of the simple test and at least one question about what B is uncertain about. Player B answers honestly in character without naming the card. A then proposes one intervention.

**Debrief (5 minutes):**
- Which card did A guess, and what evidence led there?
- Did the intervention fit? (Card 1: schedule, owner, public visibility. Card 2: something that changes incentives or accountability. Card 3: a fair selection rule among options people disagree about.)
- How would the wrong card's intervention have failed?
- Swap roles and draw again.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating every disagreement as conflict** — You end up persuading someone who already wants what you want. First ask whether you actually want incompatible things.
- **Calling coordination failure a character flaw** — Before "Nobody takes initiative," ask whether there was a mutually understood assignment, and whether people were simply unwilling to act alone.
- **Assuming a shared goal settles it** — A shared goal tells people where they want to end up, not how everyone gets there. And it can still hide a free-rider problem; run the simple test.
- **Saying "everyone knows what to do"** — Ask instead, "Do we actually share the same expectation about what happens next?"
- **Telling people privately what needs to be public** — Private messages leave each person wondering about the others. Say it in front of everyone when mutual awareness matters.
- **Over-optimizing among acceptable options** — When failing to coordinate costs more than the difference among options, stop optimizing and coordinate.
- **Prescribing "better communication" without a diagnosis** — Ask what communication must accomplish. Talking won't dissolve a genuine conflict.
- **Assuming a stable convention is a good one** — Everyone may follow it only because everyone expects everyone else to. Sometimes the system needs redesigning (Module 11).

## Field assignment

This week, pick one recurring friction point you've been treating as a conflict or a motivation problem: a task that falls through the cracks, a failing handoff, a schedule you renegotiate every week.

1. Run the Coordination Diagnostic before saying anything, and commit to a diagnosis.
2. If it's coordination, make one expectation mutually clear (owner, time, standard) where everyone involved can see everyone else receive it, or propose a simple convention.
3. If it's a social dilemma or conflict, don't force a coordination fix; note what kind of intervention it really needs.
4. Record what you said, where, what happened over the following days, and whether the problem recurred.

**Debrief questions for next week:**
- Did your initial assumption match your final diagnosis? What changed your mind?
- Did a public, explicit expectation produce a different result than past private reminders?
- If the problem returned despite clear, mutually known expectations, what does that suggest: a hidden incentive, a real conflict, or a game that needs redesigning?

## The question to carry

> **Are we actually in conflict—or are we failing to coordinate? If it's coordination: What expectation needs to become mutually clear? Make the next move obvious to everyone.**
