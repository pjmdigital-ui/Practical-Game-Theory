> **The question:** What's really going on here?

## Why this module matters

Most communication advice begins with a deceptively simple question: *What should I say?* Before asking for a raise, you rehearse your words and your number. That matters, but it skips what comes first. What does your boss actually want? What happens if they say no? What alternatives does each of you have? What will your boss probably do after you speak?

Once you ask those questions, the conversation becomes a game. Not a contest against your boss, but a game in the game-theoretic sense: a situation in which the outcome depends, at least partly, on the decisions of multiple people. Your words enter a system that already holds incentives, expectations, information, constraints, alternatives, history, timing, and other decision-makers.

This module teaches the course's first mental shift: **don't start with the words. Start with the situation.** It isn't a cynical view that everyone is manipulating everyone else. It's simpler: other people are decision-makers, and their decisions affect your outcomes. Seeing the game often makes cooperation *easier*, because you begin to understand why another person's behavior makes sense from where they stand.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this module you will be able to:

1. **Define** a game in the game-theoretic sense and explain why a game does not have to be competitive.
2. **Identify** every player who can affect an outcome, including people who aren't in the room.
3. **Distinguish** what a person wants from what they can actually do, and stated constraints from real ones.
4. **Map** an interaction using the nine questions of the Game Map: WHO, WHAT, OPTIONS, CONSTRAINTS, INFORMATION, TIMING, ALTERNATIVES, DEPENDENCIES, and THEN WHAT.
5. **Diagnose** whether a recurring problem is a person problem or a game problem before deciding how to respond.

## Lessons

### Lesson 1.1 — From "What should I say?" to "What's really going on?" (10 min)

**Teaching points**
- Game theory studies strategic interaction: situations in which one decision-maker's choices affect the outcomes available to others. The formal framework describes players, strategies, payoffs, and outcomes.
- A game doesn't need a winner and a loser. Cooperation, coordination, friendship, partnerships, and family decisions are all games in this sense. Thomas Schelling emphasized that real interactions usually contain *both* common and conflicting interests.
- The starting premise isn't "everyone is manipulating everyone." It's: **other people are decision-makers, and their decisions affect your outcomes.**
- Nash's equilibrium idea captures the core point: each person's behavior has to be understood in relation to everyone else's.

**Example to use:** The raise request. Before rehearsing a word, ask: What does your boss want? What happens if they say no, or if you turn down a counteroffer? Who has the authority to decide, and who else might influence it? The "conversation" turns into a structure with several players, options, and consequences that run well past the first sentence.

**On-screen takeaway:** > **Don't start with the words. Start with the situation.**

### Lesson 1.2 — Players, wants, and options (10 min)

**Teaching points**
- The person you're talking to may not be the whole game. Ask: *Who can actually affect the outcome?* Sometimes the people who matter most aren't in the room.
- A player is simply a decision-maker. Your spouse, customer, boss, and you are all players, and real people care about fairness, relationships, status, identity, security, and reputation, not just money.
- Interests are usually mixed. Before asking "Who's right?", ask: *Where are our interests aligned, and where are they different?*
- Look at what *everyone* can do. **Wanting something and being able to do something are not the same thing.** Game theory keeps available choices separate from preferences.

**Example to use:** Negotiating with a salesperson. You think it's you vs. the salesperson. But they may need a manager's approval, the company may have a pricing policy, another customer may want the same product, and you may have three other suppliers. The strategic situation looks very different from the surface conversation.

**On-screen takeaway:** > **Who can actually affect the outcome?**

### Lesson 1.3 — Constraints, alternatives, and dependence (10 min)

**Teaching points**
- Constraints are part of the game. "I simply can't go below $10,000" might be a bluff, a real company minimum, or an unwillingness to go lower. These look identical on the surface and are completely different strategically. Separate what people **say** constrains them from what actually **constrains** them.
- Ask: *What happens if we don't agree?* Outside options shape the negotiation itself, not just what happens after it fails. But their effect depends on the game's structure. There's no rule that "the person with the better alternative always wins."
- Power often comes from the structure of **dependence and alternatives**. Ask: *Who needs whom more?* That isn't a persuasion tactic. It's a property of the game.

**Example to use:** The contractor negotiation. You want a lower price; they want theirs. Instead of only persuading, ask what happens if you both walk away. Maybe you have four other contractors and they have ten customers waiting. Maybe neither has a good alternative, or one of you badly needs the deal. Each version is a different game, even though the conversation sounds the same.

**On-screen takeaway:** > **Wanting something and being able to do something are not the same thing.**

### Lesson 1.4 — Timing, moving games, and the game behind the conversation (10 min)

**Teaching points**
- **When you act matters.** Responding on the spot, seeing the other side's offer before making yours, and choosing without knowing what they'll do are different games. Game theorists separate simultaneous-move from sequential games because timing and observability change what's possible.
- A game isn't static. Your move can change what the other person knows, believes, and expects, and which choices remain open. A broken promise, a revealed fact, or a public commitment reshapes the board.
- The stated issue may not be the whole issue. "I don't care about the money" may be about recognition. The lesson isn't "assume everyone is lying," which is paranoia. It's: **don't assume the surface description tells you everything about the strategic structure.** Stay curious.
- Games can sit inside games, and you can have the perfect words and still be playing the wrong game.

**Example to use:** Two people argue about which business strategy to use. Underneath may be other questions: who gets final authority, who gets credit or blame, and who is setting a precedent. The visible disagreement is only one layer.

**On-screen takeaway:** > **You can have the perfect words and still be playing the wrong game.**

### Lesson 1.5 — Person problem or game problem? Building the Game Map (8 min)

**Teaching points**
- A game doesn't have to be competitive. You can view a marriage or a friendship through game theory without treating the other person as an opponent.
- Before diagnosing a **person problem**, check for a **game problem**. Behavior often comes from the structure of the interaction. That doesn't remove responsibility, but it changes where you look for a fix.
- The Game Map trains your *attention*. It isn't a rigid nine-step procedure. With practice, you notice these things automatically. That's what a mental model does.
- The first mental shift: from **Me → What should I say? → Them** to **Me → My move → Their response → My next move → Their next response → Outcome**.

**Example to use:** A manager says, "My employees aren't cooperating," and concludes they're selfish. But maybe the pay system rewards outperforming each other, nobody knows who owns the task, or speaking up has been punished before. Likewise, you think a coworker is procrastinating while they think you'll take over if they start. You both want the project done, but your expectations of each other create the problem.

**On-screen takeaway:** > **Before you diagnose a person problem, check for a game problem.**

## Key terms

| Term | Plain-language meaning |
|---|---|
| Game | A situation in which the outcome depends, at least partly, on the decisions of multiple people. It can be cooperative, competitive, or mixed. |
| Player | Any decision-maker in the interaction who can affect the outcome, including people who aren't in the room. |
| Mixed interests | Interests that are neither fully aligned nor fully opposed. |
| Available actions (strategies) | What each player can actually do, as distinct from what they want to do. |
| Constraint | Anything that limits a player's choices, such as a policy, budget, approval requirement, or veto. A stated constraint and a real constraint aren't always the same. |
| Outside option / alternative | What happens to each player if the interaction produces no agreement. |
| Dependence | Who needs whom, and who has alternatives. Power often comes from this structure rather than from personality. |
| Simultaneous vs. sequential game | Choosing without seeing the other's move, versus one moving first and the other responding. |
| Game behind the conversation | The structure beneath the stated topic, such as trust, status, credit, or precedent. |
| Person problem vs. game problem | Blaming behavior on someone's character, versus recognizing that the structure of the interaction may be producing it. |
| Game Map | The chapter's nine-question perception tool: WHO, WHAT, OPTIONS, CONSTRAINTS, INFORMATION, TIMING, ALTERNATIVES, DEPENDENCIES, THEN WHAT. |

## Research spotlight

**1. The "Wall Street Game" vs. the "Community Game" (Liberman, Samuels, and Ross, 2004)**

*What was done:* Participants, including American college students and Israeli pilots and instructors, played essentially the same Prisoner's Dilemma. The only change was the name. Some were told they were playing the "Wall Street Game" and others the "Community Game."

*What was found:* The label had a substantial effect on whether people cooperated or defected, larger than observers expected. Meanwhile, judgments of participants' prior reputations did a poor job of predicting who would cooperate.

*What it suggests for real conversations:* People's *construal* of the situation, meaning what kind of game they believe they're in, can materially change how they behave inside it. Two people can face nearly identical incentives and act very differently because they read the interaction differently. How you identify the game matters.

*What it does NOT prove:* It doesn't show that consciously mapping a game leads to optimal decisions. It also doesn't mean the game is "only perception." There's still an underlying strategic structure, and people's interpretations of it can be accurate or inaccurate.

**2. Awareness of the frame changes strategic play (Charness and Sontuoso, 2023)**

*What was done:* In a study of "frame-dependent rationalizability," participants played coordination games built around visual objects. The researchers varied which features of the choices players were made aware of.

*What was found:* Changing which frames players were aware of changed their strategic behavior.

*What it suggests for real conversations:* People don't necessarily notice every strategically relevant feature of the situation in front of them. That's a strong reason to ask yourself on purpose: *What am I failing to notice about the game?*

*What it does NOT prove:* It was a controlled lab coordination task, not a workplace or family conversation, and it doesn't show that any particular habit of mind improves everyday outcomes.

**A counterweight.** Colin Camerer's review of behavioral game theory shows that framing, social preferences, and learning can push real behavior away from simple game-theoretic predictions. The models don't claim people are perfectly rational. They help you spot strategic forces you'd miss by looking only at words and personalities.

**The evidence standard for this module:** Representing situations in terms of players, choices, payoffs, information, and timing is basic game theory, and the evidence that construal affects play is strong. Evidence that teaching people to "see the game" improves real-world communication **has not been established**. The Game Map is a practical synthesis, not a tested intervention.

## The tool: The Game Map

Use this before any important interaction. Write short answers and don't aim for perfection. The goal is to train your attention, not to complete a form.

**WHO?**

- Who can actually affect the outcome, including anyone not in the room? ______________________________

**WHAT?**
- What does each person want? ______________________________
- Where are our interests aligned? ______________________________
- Where are they different? ______________________________

**OPTIONS?**
- What can each player actually do? ______________________________

**CONSTRAINTS?**
- What limits those choices? ______________________________
- Which constraints are stated, and which do I know are real? ______________________________

**INFORMATION?**
- Who knows what? ______________________________
- Who doesn't know what? ______________________________

**TIMING?**
- Who moves first? ______________________________
- Who responds? ______________________________
- What can each person observe before choosing? ______________________________

**ALTERNATIVES?**
- What happens if there is no agreement, for me and for them? ______________________________

**DEPENDENCIES?**
- Who needs whom? ______________________________
- Who has alternatives? ______________________________

**THEN WHAT?**
- If I make this move, what is the other person likely to do next? ______________________________
- And what would I do after that? ______________________________

**Final check**
- Could there be a game behind the conversation, such as credit, trust, status, authority, or precedent? ______________________________
- Is this a person problem or a game problem? ______________________________
- What am I failing to notice about the game? ______________________________

## Practice exercises

### Exercise A — Analyze the scenario

Dana runs operations at a regional distribution company. For six weeks she has been pushing Marcus, the warehouse lead, to adopt a new scheduling system. Marcus keeps saying it "isn't ready" and that his team "doesn't have time to learn it." Dana has started to see him as stubborn and plans to tell him at Thursday's one-on-one that the switch is happening next Monday, no exceptions. Here's what Dana hasn't thought about. Marcus's quarterly bonus is tied to on-time shipment rates, and any slowdown during the learning period will hit his numbers. The software vendor's onboarding team is booked for the next three weeks. Two of Marcus's most experienced pickers have quietly told him they'd rather leave than learn "another system." Dana's own boss, the VP, approved the system and asked her for a progress update at the end of the month. Marcus knows other warehouses in the area are hiring leads.

1. Who are the players in this game, and which ones aren't in Thursday's meeting?
2. Where are Dana's and Marcus's interests aligned, and where do they differ?
3. What constraints shape Marcus's options, and what alternatives does each of them have if they don't reach agreement?
4. If Dana announces "next Monday, no exceptions," what is Marcus likely to do next? Then what?
5. Is this primarily a person problem or a game problem?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

**1. Players.** In the room: Dana and Marcus. Outside it: the VP, who wants an update; the two senior pickers, who could quit; the vendor's onboarding team, whose availability sets the real start date; and local employers hiring leads, part of Marcus's outside option. Dana has been treating this as Dana vs. Marcus. It's a much bigger game.

**2. Interests.** Aligned: both want the warehouse to run well, both want to keep good people, and neither wants a failed rollout. Different: Dana needs visible progress for her VP by month's end, while Marcus needs to protect on-time rates that drive his bonus and to keep his best pickers. Their interests are mixed, not opposed.

**3. Constraints and alternatives.** Marcus's bonus structure penalizes a slowdown. The vendor can't onboard anyone for three weeks, so "next Monday" may not even be feasible. Marcus can't force veteran pickers to stay. If there's no agreement, Marcus can leave for another warehouse, the pickers can leave, and Dana faces a VP update with a stalled rollout and possibly a staffing gap. Dana's alternative to Marcus is replacing a lead mid-rollout, which is costly. Dependence runs both ways, and Marcus has more alternatives than Dana has assumed.

**4. Then what?** A hard Monday deadline is a commitment Dana may not be able to keep, since the vendor isn't available. Marcus could comply on paper while shipments slip, escalate, start interviewing, or watch his pickers resign. Each makes Dana's month-end update worse.

**5. Diagnosis.** Mostly a game problem. Marcus's "stubbornness" is a reasonable response to an incentive system that punishes the change, plus real staffing risk. He still has responsibility, but the structure is driving the behavior. A better first move: ask Marcus what would make the rollout workable, check the vendor's timeline, and raise the bonus-metric conflict with the VP. That changes the game instead of escalating inside it.

</details>

### Exercise B — Your own game

Pick a live situation you've been thinking about mostly in terms of *what to say*: a conversation you're dreading, a request, or a recurring friction.

1. Describe the situation in one sentence, as you've been seeing it.
2. Fill out the full Game Map. Circle any answer you're unsure of. Those are your information gaps.
3. Name one player you hadn't been considering, and one constraint or alternative that changes the picture.
4. Write the stated issue and one possible game behind it. What would tell you whether the stated issue is the real one?
5. Answer THEN WHAT for two different opening moves. Which leaves both of you with better options?
6. Finish this sentence: "I was about to walk into this thinking only about ______________________________. What's really going on is ______________________________."

### Exercise C — Partner drill / role-play

**Format:** Pairs, about 15 minutes. One person plays the Employee and one plays the Manager. An observer is optional, or you can practice with an AI practice partner by giving it the Manager's private brief.

**Setup (both see this):** The Manager has just sent the Employee a text: *"We need to talk about your performance."* The meeting starts in five minutes.

**Employee's private brief:** You don't know what this is about. You have an unmentioned informal offer from another company, and you think your major project last month went unnoticed.

**Manager's private brief:** You wrote that text in a hurry. You actually want your strongest performer, the Employee, to take over a struggling project. Your director is pressing you to fix it this quarter, and you can't offer a raise until the next budget cycle.

**Round 1 (3 minutes):** Each player fills out a quick Game Map from their own position, noting what they know, don't know, and assume.

**Round 2 (5 minutes):** Hold the conversation. The Employee's job is to understand the game before defending themselves: ask what the Manager wants and who else is involved.

**Round 3 (5 minutes), debrief:**
- Employee: How much of your map was wrong? What did you assume from the wording of the text?
- Manager: How did your words change the Employee's decision problem? What would you send instead?
- Both: How did the players outside the room (the director, the other company) and each side's constraints shape the conversation? Where were your interests aligned?

If time allows, switch roles and rerun it with the Manager stating the real purpose up front. Notice how the game changes.

## Common mistakes

- **Starting with the words.** Rehearsing the perfect sentence before understanding the situation. Words enter a system of incentives, information, and alternatives. Map the situation first.
- **Treating the person in front of you as the whole game.** Managers, policies, partners, spouses, other customers, and alternative suppliers may all affect the outcome. Ask who can actually affect it.
- **Assuming "game" means "competition."** Most interactions mix common and conflicting interests. Seeing the game should make cooperation easier.
- **Confusing wanting with being able.** Someone may want to give you a refund or a raise and still not be able to. Look at their available actions and constraints.
- **Assuming a stated constraint is a lie, or that it's true.** "I can't go below $10,000" could be a bluff, a real policy, or a preference. Ask what actually constrains them.
- **Ignoring what happens if there's no agreement.** Alternatives and dependence shape the conversation itself, though a better alternative doesn't automatically win.
- **Becoming paranoid about hidden motives.** Sometimes the stated issue really is the issue. Stay curious instead of suspicious.
- **Diagnosing a person problem when it's a game problem.** Before concluding someone is selfish or lazy, check whether incentives, ownership, or history are producing the behavior.
- **Running the Game Map as a rigid script.** It trains attention. The goal is to notice these things automatically over time.

## Field assignment

**This week: map one conversation before you have it.**

Choose one meaningful interaction coming up this week. Before it happens, spend 10 minutes on a Game Map instead of a script, and mark which answers are assumptions rather than facts.

During and after the conversation, observe and record:
- Any player who turned out to matter that you hadn't listed
- Any constraint or alternative that was different from what you assumed
- One moment where the other person's response changed your next move (THEN WHAT in action)
- Whether the stated topic was the whole issue, or whether there was a game behind the conversation

**Debrief questions for next week:**
1. What did you notice about the situation that you would have missed if you'd only prepared what to say?
2. Which assumption was most wrong, and what made the other person's behavior more reasonable from their position?
3. Was the main obstacle a person problem or a game problem, and what would change the game next time?
