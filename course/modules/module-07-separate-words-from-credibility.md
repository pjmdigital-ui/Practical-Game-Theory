# Module 7 — Separate Words From Credibility

> **The question:** What makes this message credible?

| | |
|---|---|
| **Book reading** | Chapter 7, "Separate Words From Credibility" |
| **Time** | ~45 min of video lessons · ~60 min of workbook · one week of field practice |
| **Tool you'll build** | The Credibility Test |

## Why this module matters

In Module 6 you learned that people decide based on what they know, what they believe, and what they think others believe. That creates a new problem. Suppose I know something you don't, and I tell you. Should you believe me? That depends, because I'm not simply a source of information. I'm also a player in the game, with interests and incentives, and what I want you to believe may affect what you decide.

Consider "Trust me. I'll take care of this." From a business partner who has kept every major commitment for fifteen years, it means one thing. From a stranger you're about to wire $50,000 to, it means something else. Same words, completely different credibility. Credibility isn't contained inside the sentence. It comes from the game surrounding it: history, incentives, evidence, knowledge, reputation, consequences, verification, and what the speaker stands to gain or lose.

This module trains one habit: separating what someone says from what makes it credible. It replaces "Do I believe them?" with "What makes this believable?" And it turns the lens on you as a speaker. When you're telling the truth and still not being believed, the problem is often not your wording. The other person may simply have no good reason to believe you yet.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this module you will be able to:

1. **Distinguish** how persuasive a message is from how credible the underlying claim is.
2. **Evaluate** a claim along separate dimensions: honesty, knowledge, and incentives.
3. **Apply** the "Would they say this anyway?" test to spot statements that carry little information on their own.
4. **Explain** why a costly signal is informative only when it is easier or more worthwhile for someone whose claim is true.
5. **Design** ways to make your own truthful claims easier for others to verify.

## Lessons

### Lesson 7.1 — The Game Around the Sentence (9 min)

**Teaching points**
- Credibility lives in the surrounding game, not in the words. Persuasion and credibility are different: a message can be persuasive without being credible, and credible without being persuasive.
- The formal setup: an informed **sender** communicates, a **receiver** decides, and the sender cares what the receiver decides. That makes communication strategic.
- Crawford and Sobel's model shows that how much information can be credibly transmitted depends partly on how closely the players' interests are aligned. So don't evaluate a message without considering the sender's incentives.
- Avoid paranoia. "They benefit from saying it, therefore it's false" is not strategic thinking.

**Example to use:** A stranger tells you the train station is two blocks east. Their payoff barely depends on which way you walk, so the words are informative. Now the same person is selling you their car and says the best offer they've had is $20,000. The answer affects how much they receive. That doesn't mean they're lying. It means the statement alone gives you less reason to update than it would if their incentives were neutral or aligned.

**On-screen takeaway:** > **They benefit from my believing this, so the statement by itself is weaker evidence. Not false. Weaker.**

### Lesson 7.2 — Cheap Talk Isn't Worthless Talk (8 min)

**Teaching points**
- **Cheap talk** is costless, nonbinding communication. Saying it doesn't directly change the material structure of the game.
- "If talk is cheap, talk is meaningless" is wrong. Farrell and Rabin argued both extremes fail: cheap talk doesn't automatically produce truth-telling, but it isn't necessarily meaningless.
- In experiments, communication was largely truthful until the incentive to exaggerate rose. Credibility is partly a property of incentives.

**Example to use:** You can see the GPS and I can't. You say "Turn left." It costs you nothing and is highly informative because we both want to arrive. Now we're racing to get there first. Same words, different strategic meaning. I have to ask, "What do you gain if I believe you?" Communication didn't change; the game did.

**On-screen takeaway:** > **Words can be cheap and still useful.**

### Lesson 7.3 — Honest, Informed, and Aligned Are Different Things (9 min)

**Teaching points**
- Don't stop at "Are they honest?" Also ask: Are they informed (in a position to know)? What are their incentives (what do they gain or lose depending on what I believe)?
- **Expertise without alignment:** a salesperson for a complex financial product knows far more than you and earns a large commission if you buy. Don't ignore them; separate "How much do they know?" from "How much does their payoff depend on my decision?"
- **Alignment without expertise:** your brother wants the best for you but knows nothing about your medical decision. Ask, "Trust him to do what?"
- **Truthfulness versus accuracy:** a false statement isn't automatically a lie. Someone can be truthful but wrong.
- **Credibility is not confidence.** Certainty and eye contact are not evidence, and confidence can itself be strategic. Don't confuse the delivery of a message with the evidence behind it.

**Example to use:** A friend sincerely tells you an investment is "guaranteed to go up," while knowing almost nothing about investing. Honesty isn't accuracy. Contrast the salesperson: high knowledge, uncertain alignment. Two different credibility problems that one "Do I trust them?" blurs together.

**On-screen takeaway:** > **Don't ask "Do I trust them?" Ask "Trust them to do what?"**

### Lesson 7.4 — Signals, Evidence, and "Would They Say This Anyway?" (11 min)

**Teaching points**
- Ask: **Would they say this anyway?** If nearly everyone benefits from saying it whether or not it's true, the words don't distinguish between cases. "I'm extremely hardworking" is an example; a demanding work sample is not.
- Evidence that is harder for different types of people to produce equally moves us from cheap talk to **signaling**. Spence showed how behavior can carry information that words alone do not.
- Cost alone doesn't make a signal credible. A terrible company can spend $20 million on advertising. Ask why the signal would be easier, cheaper, or more worthwhile for someone whose claim is true.
- Actions can be strategic too. Treat behavior as **evidence, not proof**.
- Verification (inspections, audits, references, independent reviews) changes the information environment. Reputation is a summary of previous rounds of the game, and credibility can collapse faster than it builds.

**Example to use:** Two contractors both say, "We stand behind our work." Contractor A wants full payment upfront, offers no references or warranty, and disappears after the job. Contractor B has hundreds of verifiable reviews, a written warranty, twenty years in the community, and fixes problems at their own expense. B has created evidence that would be hard to maintain if the claim were repeatedly false. Pair it with the manager who says "Challenge me," then sidelines the employee who did. The observed consequence of disagreement now outweighs the invitation.

**On-screen takeaway:** > **Why would this signal be easier for someone whose claim is true?**

### Lesson 7.5 — The Speaker's Side: Make Truth Easier to Verify (8 min)

**Teaching points**
- A persuasion failure may be a credibility failure. If the listener understood you and still doesn't believe you, the question isn't "How do I say this better?" It's "How do I make this claim more credible?"
- "I know I'm honest" isn't evidence for them. They may lack your information, have been misled before, or see that you benefit from being believed.
- When you're telling the truth, make it easier to evaluate: evidence, demonstrations, references, guarantees, documentation, and consistency between words and behavior.
- Signaling asks, "What does my action tell you about me?" Commitment asks, "How does my action change my future options?" Module 8 covers commitment.
- Ethics: never manufacture the appearance of truth. Fake reviews, fabricated evidence, and staged signals are deception. And don't become cynical. You can trust and still understand the game.

**Example to use:** You're selling your car and know the transmission is fine. The buyer wants a mechanic to inspect it, and you feel insulted. From their side, the seller knows more and benefits from the sale. The inspection isn't an accusation; it's a response to an information asymmetry. Similarly, a client who doesn't believe your on-time promise after three late projects won't be moved by a better story, but milestones they can check might help.

**On-screen takeaway:** > **Ask "What reason do they have to believe me?"**

## Key terms

| Term | Plain-language meaning |
|---|---|
| Credibility | How much reason a claim gives you to update, given the speaker's knowledge, incentives, evidence, behavior, and history. Not the same as persuasiveness. |
| Sender / receiver | The informed player who communicates, and the player who then decides. Communication is strategic when the sender cares what the receiver decides. |
| Cheap talk | Costless, nonbinding communication. It can still carry real information when interests are aligned. |
| "Would they say this anyway?" | A test of whether a statement distinguishes true from false cases. If everyone would say it regardless, the words alone carry little information. |
| Signal | An observable action that conveys information about something hidden, especially one that is easier or more worthwhile for someone whose claim is true. |
| Verification | Inspections, audits, certifications, references, or independent reviews that change the information environment around a claim. |
| Reputation | A summary of information from previous rounds of the game. It accumulates and can collapse. |
| Truthfulness vs. accuracy | Do they report what they believe? Is what they believe correct? |
| Commitment | An action that changes what you can rationally do later, as opposed to a signal, which tells others something about you. |

## Research spotlight

**1. Cheap talk: Crawford and Sobel; Farrell and Rabin; Cox and Stoddard**

*What was done.* Crawford and Sobel modeled a better-informed sender communicating with a receiver whose later action affects both. Farrell and Rabin reviewed the cheap-talk literature. Cox and Stoddard ran an experiment in which players received private information about whether contributing to a public good would be valuable and could send costless messages first. The group gained from honesty, but individuals sometimes had an incentive to exaggerate.

*What was found.* In Crawford and Sobel's model, how much information can be credibly passed depends on how aligned the players' interests are, and informative communication can still occur when interests partly conflict. Farrell and Rabin argued that cheap talk is neither automatically truthful nor automatically meaningless. In Cox and Stoddard's experiment, communication was largely truthful and improved efficiency. When the incentive to exaggerate increased, truthfulness declined and the benefits shrank.

*What it suggests.* The people didn't change; the payoff for exaggerating did, and communication changed with it. When you hear a claim, ask how much the speaker gains from moving your decision.

*What it does NOT prove.* It does not show that people always lie when interests conflict, that costless words are meaningless, or that aligned interests guarantee truth.

**2. Signaling: Spence; Kübler, Müller, and Normann**

*What was done.* Michael Spence's signaling work, recognized in the 2001 Nobel Prize in economics, showed how education can, under certain conditions, help employers distinguish among workers whose productivity they can't observe. Kübler, Müller, and Normann tested versions of this job-market signaling game in the lab.

*What was found.* More productive worker types invested in education more often, and employers paid higher wages to those who invested. But separation was incomplete, and wages didn't converge perfectly to the theoretical equilibrium.

*What it suggests.* Behavior that is harder to produce for someone whose claim is false (a work sample, a warranty, a verifiable track record) tells you more than self-description.

*What it does NOT prove.* It does not show that observable actions perfectly reveal hidden qualities, or that a costly action proves a claim true. Signals contain information; they don't perfectly reveal reality.

## The tool: The Credibility Test

Use this for high-stakes claims, not for directions to the restroom.

**The claim (in their words):** ______________________________

**What decision of mine does it affect?** ______________________________

**Part 1 — The eight questions**

1. **Do they know?** Are they in a position to possess this information? ______________________________
2. **What do they want me to believe?** ______________________________
3. **What happens if I believe them?** How does my response affect their payoff? ______________________________
4. **Would they say this even if it were false?** If yes, the words alone may not be highly informative. ______________________________
5. **What evidence supports the claim?** Assertion: ______________________________ Verification: ______________________________
6. **What does their behavior suggest?** (Evidence, not proof.) ______________________________
7. **What does their history suggest?** ______________________________
8. **What would make this claim costly to fake?** ______________________________

**Part 2 — Separate the dimensions** (high / medium / low / unknown)

- Honesty: ______ because ______________________________
- Knowledge: ______ because ______________________________
- Incentive alignment: ______ because ______________________________

**Part 3 — Persuasive versus credible**

- How convincing did it feel (confidence, charisma, emotion)? ______________________________
- How credible is the underlying claim? ______________________________
- Did it change my mind, or give me good reason to change my mind? ______________________________
- What verification is available, and is it worth getting? ______________________________

**Part 4 — Speaker's side (when you're the one making the claim)**

- What reason does the other person have to believe me? ______________________________
- Is this a message problem or a credibility problem? ______________________________
- How can I make my truthful claim easier to evaluate? ______________________________

## Practice exercises

### Exercise A — Analyze the scenario

Dana runs a small marketing agency and is choosing a web developer for a large client project. Marcus is polished and energetic. He says, "I've never missed a deadline, and I'm the best React developer in this city." He never hesitates, has a beautiful slide deck, and offers 10 percent off if Dana signs this week. Asked for references, he says past clients are "under NDA, but trust me, they were thrilled." Priya is quieter and a bit awkward. She says, "I think I can hit your timeline, but the payment integration is the risky part, and I'd want a week to scope it." She sends three live sites she built, names two former clients Dana can call, and proposes milestone payments tied to working deliverables. Dana's business partner, who knows Marcus socially, says, "Marcus is a great guy. He'd never lie to you."

1. Apply "Would they say this anyway?" to Marcus's two main claims.
2. Which parts of each pitch are cheap talk, and which are signals or verification?
3. Separate honesty, knowledge, and incentives for the partner's endorsement.
4. What should Dana do next, without becoming cynical?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

1. Almost any developer chasing this contract, reliable or not, benefits from saying "never missed a deadline" and "best in the city." The words don't distinguish dependable from undependable. That doesn't make Marcus a liar; it means the claims alone give Dana little reason to update.

2. Marcus's pitch is mostly cheap talk, delivered with confidence. The deck cost effort, but a weak developer could make one just as polished, so it doesn't separate types. The signing discount is pressure, not evidence. Priya offers things harder to produce if her claims were false: live sites, callable references, and milestone pay. Milestone pay is far more attractive to someone who expects to deliver. Her volunteered risk also works against her short-term interest, which makes her other statements more believable. Dana shouldn't read Priya's awkwardness as weakness or Marcus's polish as accuracy.

3. The partner is probably honest. Knowledge is uncertain: knowing Marcus socially says little about his deadline record or skill. Incentives are mostly aligned with Dana, though friendship may color the view. "He'd never lie" addresses honesty, not accuracy. Marcus could be sincere and still overestimate himself. Trust the partner to do what?

4. Ask Marcus for verification that doesn't breach an NDA: a public portfolio, a work sample, or a general reference. Offer both candidates the same milestone structure and see how each responds. Actually call Priya's references. This isn't distrust of Marcus, who may be excellent. It's a reasonable response to an information asymmetry where both candidates benefit from being believed.

</details>

### Exercise B — Your own game

Pick a live situation where a claim matters to a decision you're about to make: a vendor's promise, a colleague's estimate, a contractor's quote, or advice from family.

1. Write the claim in the speaker's exact words and run Parts 1 through 3 of the Credibility Test.
2. Circle the weakest link: knowledge, incentives, evidence, behavior, or history.
3. Identify one piece of verification you could get this week, and decide whether the stakes justify it.

Then flip it. Pick a situation where you're telling the truth but aren't being believed, or soon need to be.

4. Complete Part 4. Honestly: would someone in your position say this even if it were false?
5. Write two ways to make your claim easier to evaluate, at least one the other person can check without relying on your word.
6. Check the ethics: does each step make the truth easier to see, or only make you look credible? Keep only the first kind.

### Exercise C — Partner drill / role-play

**Setup (15 minutes, pairs or threes).** One **seller**, one **buyer**, an optional **observer**. The seller is selling a used bicycle at a fixed price.

**Private information.** The seller secretly draws a card. Card 1: "The bike is in excellent condition." Card 2: "The frame has a hairline crack. You needn't volunteer it, but you may not state a direct falsehood if asked."

**Round 1 (4 min).** The seller makes claims; the buyer asks questions but may not request verification. The buyer then guesses the card and rates confidence from 1 to 10.

**Round 2 (4 min).** New card draw. The buyer may now request verification: an inspection, a return window, a call to the previous owner. A Card 2 seller must honor any guarantee they offer.

**Debrief (5–7 min).**
- In Round 1, did the buyer's guess track the truth, or the seller's confidence?
- Which statements would the seller have made regardless of the card?
- In Round 2, which offers were easier for a Card 1 seller? Why did those carry more information?
- Card 1 sellers: how did it feel to be doubted while telling the truth? What helped?

**AI version.** Ask an AI to play a seller who secretly picks a card and reveals it only at the end.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating "they benefit from saying it" as "it's false."** That's paranoia. The correct conclusion is that the statement alone is weaker evidence.
- **Treating cheap talk as meaningless.** When interests align, costless words like directions or "Turn left" are highly informative.
- **Reducing credibility to "Are they honest?"** Ask about honesty, knowledge, and incentives separately, and ask "Trust them to do what?"
- **Confusing confidence with evidence.** Smooth delivery can be strategic, and an awkward, nervous person can be completely correct.
- **Assuming any costly signal is credible.** A $20 million ad campaign doesn't prove a product is good.
- **Answering a credibility problem with more rhetoric.** If they understood you and still don't believe you, make the claim more credible instead of more eloquent.
- **Being offended by verification.** An inspection is a response to an information asymmetry, not an accusation.
- **Becoming cynical.** Most people cooperate and tell the truth. Don't confuse trusting someone with failing to think, but keep trusting.
