# Module 11 — Change the Game

> **The question:** Do I need a better move—or a better game?

| | |
|---|---|
| **Book reading** | Chapter 11, "Change the Game" |
| **Time** | ~55 min of video lessons · ~60 min of workbook · one week of field practice |
| **Tool you'll build** | The Game-Change Audit |

## Why this module matters

For ten modules you have learned to see more clearly inside a strategic interaction. Those questions help you make better moves. But eventually you reach a more powerful question: do you need a better move, or a better game? You can become extraordinarily skilled at playing a game that should never have been designed that way in the first place.

Picture a sales organization where management keeps saying, "We want everyone to collaborate," while publicly ranking salespeople every month and paying bonuses only to the top performers. People guard information, management holds a teamwork workshop, and nothing changes, because the game is saying something different from the speech. Management says "cooperate." The incentive system says "your coworkers are your competitors." At that point the strategic question is no longer "How can we persuade these people to collaborate?" It is "What behavior does our system actually reward?"

This module shifts you from fixing people to examining systems, and from reacting to bad outcomes to redesigning the structure producing them. Because designers usually hold more power than players, every lever here is tied back to the book's foundation: truth, autonomy, dignity, and informed choice.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this module you will be able to:

1. **Distinguish** a "better move" problem from a "better game" problem by asking what behavior the current structure actually rewards, rather than what it claims to reward.
2. **Explain** mechanism design and incentive compatibility in plain language, using the "one cuts, the other chooses" example.
3. **Predict** how a metric or incentive could be gamed, and how effort may shift away from valuable work that is harder to measure.
4. **Identify** the design levers in a recurring problem: rules, payoffs, information, timing, options, authority, players, observability, defaults, feedback, friction, and exit.
5. **Design** a small, ethical, testable change to a game you are part of, and plan how you will observe and update it.

## Lessons

### Lesson 11.1 — From playing the game to designing it (~12 min)

**Teaching points**
- Most of game theory asks: given the game, what will the players do? Mechanism design reverses the question: given the outcome we want, how should we design the game?
- Leonid Hurwicz, Eric Maskin, and Roger Myerson received the 2007 Nobel Prize in Economic Sciences for laying the foundations of mechanism design. You don't need the mathematics; the mental shift is what matters.
- Don't fix the player before you inspect the game. We tend to explain behavior through character ("they're lazy," "they're dishonest," "they're gaming it"). Strategic thinking adds one more question: what does the game make attractive?
- Behavior emerges from the interaction between people and structure. Sometimes perfectly ordinary people behave differently when the rules change.
- Incentives speak louder than mission statements. Say "tell us the truth" but retaliate against bad news, and the reward wins. Incentives don't explain everything; people have values. But if you repeatedly pay for a behavior, expect more of it.

**Example to use:** The cake. Two children need to divide a cake and each wants the larger piece. Instead of dividing it perfectly and refereeing the complaints, the parent changes the mechanism: one child cuts, the other chooses first. The cutter now has every reason to make the pieces equal. The parent didn't change anyone's personality or give a lecture on fairness; the rules made the children's self-interest produce the fair outcome.

**On-screen takeaway:** > **Instead of constantly fighting incentives, sometimes redesign them.**

### Lesson 11.2 — Incentives work, which is exactly why they are dangerous (~12 min)

**Teaching points**
- Changing an incentive can change behavior substantially (see the Pakistan study in the Research spotlight).
- Designing incentives is dangerous precisely because incentives work. Once a measurement is tied to money, status, or punishment, people optimize against the scoreboard, and the measure may stop representing what you cared about.
- The metric is not the goal. You want learning; you measure test scores. You want a healthy relationship; you measure who apologized first.
- The principal-agent problem: an agent acting for a principal has somewhat different incentives and often knows more about their own actions.
- Multitask incentives: strongly reward the easiest task to measure and effort can drift away from valuable work that is harder to see.
- The key question: "What exactly am I rewarding?" Not what you intend to reward, but what behavior actually improves the metric.

**Example to use:** The customer-service department. Management says solving the customer's problem is the top priority, but representatives are evaluated mainly on average call time. A complicated customer becomes strategically expensive, so people rush, transfer calls, and end conversations early. Then management asks why nobody takes ownership. That isn't a communication problem; it's a game-design problem.

**On-screen takeaway:** > **How could a smart player maximize this payoff without giving me what I really want?**

### Lesson 11.3 — The levers: how a game can be changed (~14 min)

**Teaching points**
- **Rules:** a clear sales-to-customer-success handoff removes one source of strategic ambiguity.
- **Payoffs:** change the compensation, the payment schedule, or add a refundable deposit. Not every behavior should be bought, but payoffs matter.
- **Information:** who knows what, and when. Dashboards, transparent criteria, independent verification.
- **Timing:** sequential instead of simultaneous moves, immediate instead of annual feedback, staged payments. Sometimes the problem is the sequence.
- **Options:** trial periods, phased implementation, partial agreements. New options can alter the equilibrium.
- **Who decides** and **players:** assigned decision rights, an appraiser, a mediator. Who gets to decide is part of the game.
- **Observability, defaults, feedback:** what happens when nobody chooses is itself a rule; shorter feedback makes a game easier to learn.
- **Removing an incentive:** sometimes simplify, reduce measurement, and restore discretion.
- **Exit:** when you can't redesign a destructive game, you can stop playing, but exit has payoffs too.
- **Good rules can reduce the need for strategy** by making the desired behavior the easy behavior.

**Example to use:** A couple stops renegotiating chores every Saturday and creates a system: you handle these, I handle those, we rotate this one. A consultant facing scope creep introduces a defined scope, a change-request process, and a fee for extra work, protecting the relationship from resentment. And the person who says "I need more discipline" turns off notifications instead of fighting a willpower contest every five minutes.

**On-screen takeaway:** > **Sometimes the highest form of strategy is removing the need for strategy.**

### Lesson 11.4 — Ethical design, humility, and the system that talks back (~12 min)

**Teaching points**
- Designers are players too: set rule, players respond, learn, adapt, system evolves. Ask "then what?"
- Design for the behavior people are likely to have. Don't require saints; don't design for monsters.
- Don't confuse control with design: design, observe, test, update, adapt. When results surprise you, ask "What did I misunderstand about the game?" Start with the smallest change that tests your hypothesis.
- Friction is part of the game. The fact that you can make something strategically difficult does not mean you should. A dark pattern is still game design, serving the wrong ethical objective.
- Ethical design asks: Does the player understand the game? Do they retain meaningful choice? Are important facts hidden? Does it treat people as decision-makers? Sometimes rules should protect the weaker player, as disclosure and inspection rights do in the used-car problem.

**Example to use:** Boston school choice. Boston's old assignment system let families benefit from strategically ranking schools instead of listing their true preferences. Sophisticated parents could game it; parents who simply reported what they wanted could be disadvantaged. In 2005 Boston replaced it with a deferred-acceptance mechanism designed to remove much of that incentive. You could tell parents "don't game the system," or you could ask "why does the system make gaming strategically useful?" The second question is mechanism design.

**On-screen takeaway:** > **Create a game in which good outcomes emerge without violating the people playing it.**

## Key terms

| Term | Plain-language meaning |
|---|---|
| Mechanism design | Starting from the outcome you want and asking what rules would make that outcome more likely, instead of taking the rules as given. |
| Incentive compatibility | A design in which following the desired strategy lines up with each participant's own incentives, so people don't have to fight their self-interest to do the right thing. |
| Strategy-proof mechanism | A system designed so that truthfully reporting your preferences is a good strategy, reducing the need to outsmart the rules. |
| Metric vs. goal | The measurement (test scores, call time, clicks) is not necessarily the outcome you care about (learning, solved problems, good content). Once payoffs attach to a metric, people may play the metric. |
| Principal-agent problem | A principal wants one thing; the agent acting on their behalf has somewhat different incentives and often knows more about their own actions. |
| Multitask incentives | When a job has several valuable tasks, strongly rewarding the most measurable one can pull effort away from the others. |
| Default | What happens when nobody chooses. It is itself a rule, and it shapes outcomes. |
| Friction | How easy or hard each choice is made to be. Friction is part of the design and carries ethical weight. |
| Exit | Stopping play when the game is destructive and you can't redesign it. A strategic choice with its own payoffs and costs. |

## Research spotlight

**1. Property-tax inspectors in Punjab, Pakistan (Khan, Khwaja, and Olken)**

*What was done:* In a field experiment, 525 property-tax inspectors took part in a system where performance rankings determined the order in which inspectors could choose their future job postings. Instead of telling bureaucrats to work harder, the design connected performance to something many employees actually valued: where they would be assigned to work.

*What was found:* The mechanism increased annual tax-revenue growth by roughly 30 to 41 percent. The research dossier notes that inspectors the researchers' model predicted would face stronger incentives under the new system improved more.

*What it suggests for real conversations:* Before giving another speech, ask what people genuinely value and whether the system connects the behavior you want to it.

*What it does NOT prove:* Not that any incentive redesign will improve outcomes, or that this design transfers unchanged to your team or family. Nor that responses will take the form the designer intended.

**2. Multitask incentives: Holmström and Milgrom's theory, illustrated by the Kenyan teacher-incentive study (Glewwe, Ilias, and Kremer)**

*What was done:* Holmström and Milgrom's multitask principal-agent analysis formalized a problem: strong incentives on one measurable activity can distort effort away from other valuable but poorly measured activities. Glewwe, Ilias, and Kremer studied a program in Kenyan primary schools that rewarded teachers based on students' test performance.

*What was found:* Scores on the incentivized exams improved. But researchers found little evidence that teacher attendance, homework assignment, or classroom pedagogy improved. Teachers increased test-preparation sessions, gains were concentrated on the exams linked to the incentive rather than unrelated tests, and the improvements did not persist as broader long-run learning gains.

*What it suggests for real conversations:* Whenever you attach a payoff to a number (calls made, bugs fixed, chores done), ask what behavior actually moves it, and what harder-to-measure work might be quietly abandoned.

*What it does NOT prove:* Not that incentives "don't work." The chapter's dossier notes teacher-incentive experiments in rural India and Tanzania with different results; in Tanzania a multiple-threshold design produced greater learning gains at lower cost than a rank-based one. The design of the incentive changes the game. Nor is there always one correct rule: goals like fairness, simplicity, and strategy-proofness can trade off.

## The tool: The Game-Change Audit

Use this when the same bad outcome keeps returning, after better arguments, better wording, more motivation, and more pressure haven't fixed it.

**Step 1 — Name the recurring problem**

The outcome that keeps returning: ______________________________

What I (or we) have already tried, inside the current game: ______________________________

My current character explanation ("they're lazy / selfish / gaming it"): ______________________________

**Step 2 — What does the game actually reward?**

What behavior is the current game rewarding? (Not what the policy claims to reward. What actually pays?) ______________________________

What behavior is it punishing? Could the desirable action currently be costly? ______________________________

What metric are people optimizing? Is it actually the outcome we care about? ______________________________

What does my stated value say, and what does the reward say? ______________________________

**Step 3 — Inspect the levers**

| Lever | How it works now | One possible change |
|---|---|---|
| Rules: what players can and can't do | ______________________________ | ______________________________ |
| Payoffs: what pays, what costs | ______________________________ | ______________________________ |
| Information: who knows what, when | ______________________________ | ______________________________ |
| Who decides | ______________________________ | ______________________________ |
| Timing: who moves when | ______________________________ | ______________________________ |
| Options: what choices exist | ______________________________ | ______________________________ |
| Players: could a third party help? | ______________________________ | ______________________________ |
| Observability and feedback | ______________________________ | ______________________________ |
| Default: what happens if nobody acts | ______________________________ | ______________________________ |

What is currently easy? ______________________________

What is currently hard? (Friction is part of the design.) ______________________________

Is there an incentive or rule I should remove rather than add? ______________________________

**Step 4 — Stress-test the redesign**

How could a smart player game this system, including my new version of it? ______________________________

What unintended behavior might the new rule create? (Think one response further: then what?) ______________________________

What harder-to-measure work might lose effort if I reward the measurable part? ______________________________

**Step 5 — Ethics check**

Does the player understand the game? ______________________________

Do they retain meaningful choice? ______________________________

Are important facts hidden, or does the system exploit confusion? ______________________________

Does it treat people as decision-makers rather than objects to be manipulated? ______________________________

**Step 6 — Smallest testable change**

What specific mechanism appears to be producing the unwanted behavior? ______________________________

The smallest change capable of testing that hypothesis: ______________________________

What I'll observe, and when: ______________________________

If exit is on the table: what would I lose, and what future would I give up? ______________________________

**The central question:** Can I redesign the game so that the behavior I want becomes a more natural response to the structure itself? ______________________________

## Practice exercises

### Exercise A — Analyze the scenario

Dana manages a twelve-person software support team. A leaderboard ranks engineers by "tickets closed per week," and the top three each quarter get a bonus and first choice of vacation weeks. Dana keeps saying customer satisfaction and knowledge-sharing matter most, and has run two mentoring workshops. Yet senior engineers rarely help juniors, complex tickets sit untouched until someone is assigned them, and customers increasingly reopen tickets marked "resolved" within days. Reopened tickets are counted as new tickets. Escalations to engineering require Dana's personal sign-off, and she is often in meetings until late afternoon. Last month, two engineers were caught splitting single customer issues into several tickets. Dana's boss has suggested firing them and "hiring people with better values."

1. What behavior is Dana's current game rewarding, and what is it punishing?
2. What metric is the team optimizing, and how does it differ from the goal?
3. Which design levers could Dana change? Name at least three.
4. How could a smart player game your proposed redesign?
5. Is "hire people with better values" the right diagnosis? What would you test first?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

1. **Rewarded and punished.** The game rewards closing many tickets quickly and not helping others, since mentoring or untangling a hard ticket lowers your count while coworkers are your rivals. It punishes complex work, mentoring, and thorough fixes. The workshops are speeches; the leaderboard is the game.

2. **Metric versus goal.** The metric is tickets closed; the goal is solved problems and a stronger team. Because reopened tickets count as new, a shallow fix earns credit twice, and ticket-splitting is people playing the metric. It is also a multitask problem: mentoring and durable fixes are hard to measure, so effort drains away from them.

3. **Levers.** *Payoffs:* a team-level reward, or one weighted toward problems that stay resolved. *Rules:* count a reopened ticket against the original resolution. *Observability:* publish reopen rates. *Who decides:* let senior engineers escalate within defined limits so tickets don't wait on Dana's calendar. *Removing an incentive:* drop the public leaderboard and restore discretion.

4. **Gaming the redesign.** Reward low reopen rates and engineers might discourage customers from reopening. Reward a team target and some may free ride. Reward satisfaction scores and people might survey only happy customers. That is stress-testing, not accusation.

5. **Diagnosis.** Firing people fixes the player before inspecting the game. Accountability can still matter, but a new hire faces the same payoffs. Dana should name the mechanism (individual ranking on a count that double-credits shallow fixes), make the smallest testable change (stop counting reopens as new tickets for one quarter), explain it openly to the team, then observe, update, and adapt.

</details>

### Exercise B — Your own game

Pick a problem in your own life that keeps coming back despite your best efforts: a recurring argument at home, a work process that keeps producing the same failure, a client pattern, or a habit you keep "trying harder" to change.

1. List every "better move" you have already tried: the conversations, the wording, the willpower.
2. Work the problem through all six steps of the Game-Change Audit.
3. Circle the single lever most responsible for the behavior.
4. Design the smallest change that would test your hypothesis. If you are both designer and player, design for the behavior you are likely to have, not the behavior you wish you had.
5. Run the ethics questions. If others are affected, can you design the change with them rather than impose it?
6. If you lack authority to redesign anything, ask: why am I trying to become better at this game? List what exit would cost before deciding.

### Exercise C — Partner drill / role-play

**Format:** pairs or small groups, 15 minutes. Works with an AI practice partner too.

**Setup:** A small family business, a bakery, has a recurring problem. Every Friday the two co-owners, siblings, argue about who gets to approve the weekend order, and the order goes in late about half the time. Every order decision requires both of them to agree.

**Roles:**
- **Sibling A (front of house).** Privately: you think your sibling over-orders and you discard unsold goods. You lack the sales numbers.
- **Sibling B (kitchen and ordering).** Privately: you have the sales data, believe running out on Saturday costs more than waste, and feel micromanaged.
- **Designer (optional third player).** Your job is not to decide who is right about quantities but to help them redesign the game.

**Round 1 (5 min):** The siblings argue Friday's order under the current rules. The Designer notes the rules, information gaps, timing, and who decides.

**Round 2 (7 min):** The Designer leads the group through the audit's lever table. Propose at least two redesigns (for example, B decides within a set range, a shared sales-and-waste sheet, or a standing Wednesday review), then stress-test: how could either sibling game the new rule?

**Debrief (3 min):**
1. In Round 1, which disagreement was really about payoffs, and which was about missing information?
2. Which lever changed the most in your redesign? Who decides, information, or timing?
3. How might the new arrangement be gamed, and what would you watch for in the first month?
4. Did the redesign treat both siblings as informed decision-makers, or did it quietly favor one?

## Common mistakes

- **Giving another speech when the structure rewards the opposite behavior.** Before another workshop, ask what the system actually pays for.
- **Blaming character before inspecting the game.** Ordinary people often respond predictably to a badly built system. Ask what the game makes attractive, without assuming structure explains everything.
- **Confusing the metric with the goal.** Ask what behavior actually improves the number, and what hard-to-measure work may lose effort.
- **Assuming "set rule, get outcome."** Players respond, learn, and adapt. Treat your design as a hypothesis.
- **Designing for saints or for monsters.** Design for ordinary human beings, not only saints and not only villains.
- **Always adding rules and rewards.** Sometimes the accumulated rules are the problem; remove the distorting incentive.
- **Redesigning everything at once.** Systems are interconnected. Make the smallest testable change, then observe and update.
- **Treating design as control or manipulation.** Friction, defaults, and hidden terms are powerful; respect truth, autonomy, dignity, and informed choice.
- **Ignoring exit, or treating it as automatically right.** Not every game deserves another move, but leaving has costs too.

## Field assignment

**This week: run one small, ethical game redesign.**

Choose one recurring problem you have the authority to influence: a household routine, a team process, a client arrangement, or your own work environment. Complete the Game-Change Audit, identify the mechanism you believe is producing the unwanted behavior, and implement the smallest change that tests it. If others are affected, explain the new game clearly, or better, design it with them.

Each day, record in a few lines:
- What behavior did you observe under the new structure?
- Did anyone respond in a way you didn't predict, including ways of gaming the new rule?
- What became easier, and what became harder?

**Debrief questions for next week:**
1. What did the system "talk back" with, and what did it reveal that you had misunderstood about the game?
2. Before this week, how much of the problem had you attributed to someone's character? How has that changed?
3. What is your next smallest adjustment, or have you concluded that the better move is to leave this game?
