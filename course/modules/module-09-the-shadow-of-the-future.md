# Module 9 — The Shadow of the Future

> **The question:** What changes because there is a next time?

| | |
|---|---|
| **Book reading** | Chapter 9, "The Shadow of the Future" |
| **Time** | ~55 min of video lessons · ~60 min of workbook · one week of field practice |
| **Tool you'll build** | The Repeated-Game Audit |

## Why this module matters

Imagine buying something from a street vendor in a city you will never visit again. Now imagine buying the same thing from the store around the corner from your house, a store you expect to visit every week for the next twenty years. Same transaction, same product, same price. But not necessarily the same game.

That is the shadow of the future. Once there is a tomorrow, today's move doesn't only determine today's payoff. It changes what the other person expects from you, how they treat you next time, whether they trust you, and whether they cooperate with you again. Trust matters. Reputation matters. Retaliation matters. Repair matters. The future has entered the payoff.

This module trains a shift in horizon. Earlier in the course you learned to ask, "Then what?" Now you'll ask, "What happens next time?" A choice can be excellent in a one-shot game and terrible in a repeated one. Most of the important parts of life (your marriage, your children, your closest friends, your team, your business partners) are repeated games. You are always teaching the people in them what to expect from you, whether you intend to or not.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this module you will be able to:

1. Distinguish a one-time transaction from a repeated relationship, and estimate how long the shadow of the future is in a given interaction.
2. Explain why repeated interaction makes reciprocity and conditional cooperation strategically possible, without claiming it guarantees cooperation.
3. Identify what a specific response is teaching another person to expect next time, including unintended lessons.
4. Design responses that discourage exploitation while leaving a path back to cooperation after honest mistakes (boundaries plus repair).
5. Recognize when "thinking long term" has become a reason to tolerate endless losses, and when changing the rules or exiting is the long-term strategy.

## Lessons

### Lesson 9.1 — Tomorrow changes today (~12 min)

**Teaching points**
- In the one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma, defection is individually attractive, so players can end up at defect/defect even though cooperate/cooperate would leave both better off.
- Change one thing: the players will meet again tomorrow, and the day after. The immediate payoff hasn't changed. The continuation of the relationship has, and that can completely change the strategy.
- Today's move can change the value of tomorrow's game. A gain today comes bundled with its future consequences.
- Cooperation is not only a personality trait. It can also be a strategic response to the structure of the relationship, and that doesn't make it fake.
- In a repeated game, today becomes history. Memory creates consequences.

**Example to use:** Two business partners have \$10,000 on the table. One discovers a loophole that would let them take an extra \$1,000 at the other's expense. If it's their last interaction, the choice looks like \$1,000 versus \$0. If they expect millions in business over the next ten years, that \$1,000 comes bundled with less trust, tighter contracts, fewer opportunities, retaliation, or the end of the partnership.

**On-screen takeaway:** > **Today's move can change the value of tomorrow's game.**

### Lesson 9.2 — Reciprocity, reputation, and trust (~12 min)

**Teaching points**
- Axelrod's iterated Prisoner's Dilemma tournaments: a simple strategy, Tit for Tat, won. It cooperated first, then copied the other player's previous move. It let cooperation continue while still responding to exploitation.
- The lesson is not "Tit for Tat is the best strategy for life." Its success depended on the environment. The broader lesson: once people expect a future together, reciprocity becomes strategically possible.
- Reputation is a prediction. It helps others answer, "What is this person likely to do next?" It can be wrong or unfair, but it shapes expectations.
- Trust lowers the cost of interaction. Demonstrated reliability lets contracts, deposits, verification, and monitoring fall away for both sides.
- The most valuable reputation is often simply: "This person is safe to play repeated games with." Squeezing every round teaches the opposite: every interaction requires protection.

**Example to use:** The neighborhood restaurant versus the highway restaurant. Mistreat a local customer and they may never return, tell neighbors, and take their family with them. The highway restaurant won't necessarily behave badly, but structurally the local one has a longer direct shadow of the future. Online ratings and seller histories extend that shadow to strangers: the platform has created memory.

**On-screen takeaway:** > **Reputation is accumulated information about future behavior.**

### Lesson 9.3 — What are you teaching them? (~11 min)

**Teaching points**
- Every important repeated interaction contains a learning process: what happens when I tell you the truth, disagree, say no, or make a mistake?
- Your words and your behavior can send different messages. When they conflict, behavior usually wins.
- Winning the round can lose the game. Sometimes strategic thinking requires resisting a short-term win.
- Judge some strategies across the relationship, not the incident: leaving \$500 on the table, enforcing an uncomfortable boundary, or admitting a mistake can cost today and pay for years.

**Example to use:** A manager tells employees, "Bring me bad news early." An employee reports a serious problem, and the manager humiliates and blames them. Next month, nobody reports anything. The words said "report problems." The behavior said "reporting problems is dangerous." Pair it with the spouse who wins an argument by using something their partner once confided, and teaches them that vulnerability is dangerous.

**On-screen takeaway:** > **What am I teaching this person to expect from me next time?**

### Lesson 9.4 — Boundaries, noise, and repair (~12 min)

**Teaching points**
- The long game is not "always be nice." Unconditional cooperation with someone who repeatedly exploits you can make exploitation profitable. Tit for Tat was cooperative but not passive.
- Boundaries can protect cooperation: "I want to keep cooperating with you, but this behavior changes what I will do next." Conditional cooperation is often more durable than unconditional cooperation.
- But punishment creates its own problem. Real relationships contain noise: we mishear, forget, and misread motives. An accidental betrayal punished as if intentional can become a feud.
- Repair (apology, clarification, restitution, forgiveness, renewed commitment) is not just a soft skill. In a noisy repeated game, it stops errors from becoming permanent retaliation loops.
- Forgiveness is not forgetting. It means: do not allow every deviation to make future cooperation impossible.
- History changes meaning. The same action, a cancelled dinner, means "something must have come up" from someone reliable for ten years and "here we go again" from someone who cancelled the last six times.

**Example to use:** Two families who have hated each other for years. Each side describes a response to a response to something years earlier. The retaliation has become the game.

**On-screen takeaway:** > **Cooperation does not require defenselessness, and protection does not require a feud.**

### Lesson 9.5 — The limits of the long game (~8 min)

**Teaching points**
- The end of the game changes behavior. When someone knows Friday is their final day, some future consequences disappear. Values may still hold, but certain incentives have changed.
- Cooperation does not automatically emerge. The future creates possibilities; it doesn't guarantee outcomes.
- Cooperation is not the same thing as goodness. Cartels and corrupt organizations cooperate too. Game theory tells us how patterns become sustainable; ethics tells us whether they're worth sustaining: truth, autonomy, dignity, informed choice.
- Reputation can trap you ("I never back down," "I'm always the nice one"). Ask: what reputation do I want to be bound by?
- Don't use "the long term" to justify endless losses. If someone repeatedly exploits cooperation, change your strategy, change the rules, or leave the game. The shadow of the future matters only if the future game is worth playing.

**Example to use:** A business relationship that keeps producing broken promises, escalating monitoring, and conflict. Instead of optimizing each new round, ask, "Why am I still playing this repeated game?" The existence of a future does not obligate you to preserve the relationship.

**On-screen takeaway:** > **Sometimes exit is the long-term strategy.**

## Key terms

| Term | Plain-language meaning |
|---|---|
| Shadow of the future | The influence that expected future interaction has on today's choices. The longer and more valuable the future, the longer the shadow. |
| One-shot game | An interaction both sides expect to happen only once, so today's move carries no future consequences between them. |
| Repeated game | The same strategic interaction played again over time, so today's behavior can shape tomorrow's treatment. |
| Tit for Tat | A strategy that cooperates first, then copies whatever the other player did last round. Simple and successful in Axelrod's tournaments, but not universally best. |
| Reciprocity | Rewarding cooperation and responding to exploitation over time; made strategically possible by a shared future. |
| Conditional cooperation | Cooperating as long as the other side does, with a credible response if they don't. Often more durable than unconditional cooperation. |
| Reputation | Accumulated information about how someone is likely to behave next; a form of strategic predictability. |
| Noise | Misunderstandings, errors, and imperfect observation that make it hard to tell mistakes from exploitation. |
| Repair | A route back to cooperation after a mistake: apology, clarification, restitution, forgiveness, renewed commitment. |
| End-game effect | The change in incentives when players believe there will be no next round. |
| Strategic uncertainty | Uncertainty about what the other player is going to do; cooperation tends to flourish when it is robust to it. |

## Research spotlight

**1. Dal Bó's test of the shadow of the future.** Pedro Dal Bó had participants play repeated Prisoner's Dilemma games in the laboratory while he varied the probability that another round would occur. A longer expected future significantly reduced opportunistic behavior, broadly in the direction repeated-game theory predicts.

*What it suggests for real conversations:* when future interaction becomes more likely, the future consequences of present behavior become more important, and that can make cooperation more attractive.

*What it does not prove:* it does not show that repeated interaction automatically produces cooperation. Later work by Dal Bó and Guillaume Fréchette found that cooperation can prevail as subjects gain experience, but the conditions were more demanding than cooperation simply being theoretically possible. Their survey of the experimental literature found high cooperation tends to emerge when cooperative strategies are robust to strategic uncertainty. These are laboratory games; they don't predict any particular person.

**2. Noise and the value of leniency.** Masaki Aoyagi, V. Bhaskar, and Guillaume Fréchette compared repeated Prisoner's Dilemma games under perfect monitoring, where players could see exactly what happened, and under imperfect (noisy) monitoring. Subjects sustained cooperation in all conditions, but under noisy monitoring they used strategies that were more complex and more lenient, which helped limit the damage from observational errors.

*What it suggests:* when misunderstandings are possible, a completely unforgiving strategy is fragile. Some tolerance and a path to repair can be strategically valuable.

*What it does not prove:* it is not evidence for "always forgive." Leniency helps in noisy settings, but exploitation is still possible, and a too-forgiving strategy can make exploitation profitable. Similarly, Axelrod's tournaments do not show that Tit for Tat is the best strategy everywhere. Its success depended on the mix of strategies, the payoffs, and how errors were handled.

## The tool: The Repeated-Game Audit

Use this when an interaction is likely to continue. Don't evaluate it as though it ends today.

**The interaction:** ______________________________

**The move I'm tempted to make:** ______________________________

**Step 1 — Is there a next round?**
Is there likely to be a next round? (yes / no / unsure) ______________________________
How valuable is that future interaction to me? To them? ______________________________
Is an endpoint visible (last day, final contract, move away)? ______________________________

**Step 2 — Transaction or relationship?**
Is this a transaction that ends when the exchange ends, or a relationship that creates another round? ______________________________

**Step 3 — Teaching and learning**
What is today's move teaching the other person to expect from me? ______________________________
What am I learning about them? ______________________________
What does their history tell me (reliable for years, or "here we go again")? ______________________________

**Step 4 — Reputation**
What reputation are we each building? ______________________________
Is that reputation useful predictability, or exploitable predictability? ______________________________
What reputation do I want to be bound by? ______________________________

**Step 5 — Incentives**
What behavior am I rewarding? ______________________________
What behavior am I making costly? ______________________________

**Step 6 — Repair**
If a mistake occurs, is there a path back to cooperation? ______________________________
How would I tell a mistake from exploitation? ______________________________

**Step 7 — The hundred-round test**
What happens if we keep playing this way for the next hundred rounds? ______________________________

**My move, revised:** ______________________________

## Practice exercises

### Exercise A — Analyze the scenario

Dana runs a small marketing agency. For four years her biggest client has been a regional furniture retailer, and her main contact there is Marcus. Last month Marcus signed off on an ad with the wrong sale dates, and the client lost a weekend of sales. Dana's contract technically lets her bill for the rework, and her account lead, Priya, wants to bill the full \$4,000 and send Marcus an email quoting his approval line by line "so it never happens again." Meanwhile Dana also has a freelance designer, Theo, who has missed three of his last four deadlines, each time with an apology and a promise, and Dana has absorbed the overtime "to keep the relationship good." Theo just asked for a raise. Separately, Dana learned that Marcus is leaving the retailer in two months, and his replacement is unknown.

1. Which of these relationships has the longer shadow of the future, and does Marcus's departure shorten it?
2. What would Priya's approach win in this round, and what might it teach the client to expect next time?
3. What has Dana been teaching Theo to expect, and what does the chapter suggest she do?
4. How does noise apply to the Marcus situation, and how should Dana distinguish mistake from exploitation in each case?
5. Run the hundred-round test on Dana's current approach with Theo.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

1. **The shadow.** The relationship with the retailer has the long shadow. Marcus's departure shortens his personal horizon, but not the account's. How Dana handles this becomes history Marcus's replacement inherits. The game has an endpoint for one player, not for the relationship.

2. **Winning the round.** Priya's approach wins the round: \$4,000 and a documented record that the error wasn't the agency's. But it may teach the client that every mistake with this agency produces a bill and an itemized accusation, so every interaction requires protection. Dana could still protect herself (clarify how approvals work going forward, perhaps split the rework cost) without turning an honest error into a punishment.

3. **Theo.** Dana has been cooperating unconditionally with someone who repeatedly defects. She's been teaching Theo that missed deadlines cost him nothing, because she absorbs the cost. Thinking long term doesn't mean refusing to protect yourself. A conditional-cooperation boundary might sound like, "I want to keep working with you, but missed deadlines change what I can do next. Before we talk about rates, I need the next three projects on time." If the pattern continues, the long-run data are telling her something, and the long-term strategy may be to change the rules or leave the game.

4. **Noise.** Marcus's error looks like a one-time mistake inside four years of reliability: same action, different history. Treating it as exploitation risks starting a retaliation loop in which the client reads the bill as hostility and responds in kind. Theo's case is different. One missed deadline might be noise; three of four, each followed by a new promise, is a pattern. Apologies without changed behavior are not repair.

5. **Hundred rounds.** If Dana keeps absorbing Theo's misses for a hundred rounds: more overtime, growing resentment, rising rates for unreliable work, and a likely eventual collapse that ends the relationship anyway, on worse terms. A clear boundary now gives it a better chance, or ends it before more value is lost.

</details>

### Exercise B — Your own game

Choose a relationship you expect to continue (coworker, partner, child, client) and one recurring moment in it, such as how mistakes or disagreements are handled.

1. Write down the last three times this moment happened and how you responded.
2. Complete the Repeated-Game Audit for the next time it happens.
3. For each of your three past responses, finish this sentence: "From this, they probably learned that ______________________________."
4. Where do your words ("tell me the truth") and your history disagree?
5. Decide on one change: a behavior you'll start rewarding, a behavior you'll make more costly, or a repair route you'll offer. Write the exact sentence you might say.

### Exercise C — Partner drill / role-play

**Format:** pairs, 15 minutes. (An AI practice partner can play Player B.)

**Setup:** Two roommates, or two coworkers who share a project, have been splitting tasks for a year. Last week Player B didn't do a task they had agreed to do, and Player A had to cover it.

**Player A privately knows:** You're annoyed. This is the second time in a month. You aren't sure whether B forgot or is starting to take advantage of you. You want the arrangement to continue.

**Player B privately knows:** You genuinely misread a message and thought A had swapped tasks with you. The first miss a few weeks ago was real carelessness on your part.

**Round 1 (4 min):** Player A responds as if this is a one-shot game: maximize being right today. Player B responds naturally.

**Round 2 (4 min):** Reset. Player A uses the audit (mistake or exploitation? what will this teach? is there a path back?) and aims to set a clear expectation without starting a feud. Player B responds naturally.

**Debrief (5–7 min):**
- In Round 1, what did B learn to expect from A? Did B start retaliating, even subtly?
- In Round 2, did A find a way to address the earlier, real miss without punishing the honest mistake?
- What would the hundred-round version of each conversation look like?

## Common mistakes

- **Treating a relationship like a transaction.** Optimizing every round can make the game itself less valuable. Give the future a place in the decision.
- **Assuming "long term" means "always be nice."** Unconditional cooperation can make exploitation profitable. Use conditional cooperation and credible boundaries.
- **Treating every apparent defection as intentional.** In a noisy world, rigid retaliation turns mistakes into feuds. Separate mistakes from exploitation, and build in a path to repair.
- **Confusing forgiveness with forgetting.** It means not letting every deviation make future cooperation impossible.
- **Using "the long term" to justify endless losses.** Repeated exploitation is data. Change your strategy, change the rules, or leave the game.
- **Treating cooperation as automatically good.** Cartels and corrupt groups cooperate too. Game theory explains what patterns can persist; ethics decides whether they deserve to.

## Field assignment

This week, pick one ongoing relationship and pay attention to what you are teaching. Each day, note one moment where someone told you the truth, disagreed, made a mistake, or kept or broke their word. Record:

- What they did.
- How you responded.
- What your response likely taught them to expect next time.
- Whether it was a mistake or a pattern, and whether there was a path back to cooperation.

At least once this week, deliberately choose the move that makes tomorrow's game better over the one that gives you the most today. Note what happened.

**Debrief questions for next week:**
1. Where did your words and your behavior teach different lessons?
2. Did you find a relationship where you've been cooperating unconditionally with repeated exploitation, or punishing honest mistakes as if they were exploitation? What would conditional cooperation look like there?
3. Running the hundred-round test on your most important relationship this week, where does the current pattern lead?
