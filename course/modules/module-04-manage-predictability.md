> **The question:** What does my predictability enable the other player to do?

## Why this module matters

In Module 3 you learned to think in responses: before you make a move, ask what it is likely to make the other person do. This module turns that question around. What happens when the other person can predict *you*? If someone knows exactly how you're going to respond, they can begin planning around you. Your behavior becomes part of their strategy.

Think of the coworker who knows you always say yes to favors, or the counterpart who knows you always concede after their first counteroffer. You never announced a rule, but they learned one: *Ask, and he'll say yes. Push, and he'll move.* You've taught them the game.

But this is not a module about becoming erratic, deceptive, or hard to read. Predictability is also what makes trust, coordination, and credible consequences possible. The skill is knowing when each is strategically valuable. The shift you'll practice is adding one question: **"What are they learning about me from what I repeatedly do?"**

## Learning objectives

By the end of this module you will be able to:

1. Identify the patterns in your own behavior that other people have learned to predict, including rules you never intended to create.
2. Distinguish situations where predictability builds trust and coordination from situations where it creates exploitation and strategic vulnerability.
3. Explain the mixed-strategy idea in plain language, and why variety is not the same as unpredictability.
4. Distinguish withholding information you are not obligated to disclose from deliberately providing false information.
5. Choose deliberately among four moves — be predictable, protect your predictability, vary your behavior, or be deliberately consistent — based on what your predictability enables the other player to do.

## Lessons

### Lesson 4.1 — The Habit They Learn (8 min)

**Teaching points**
- If someone can reliably predict your action, they can choose a response that takes advantage of it. They no longer wonder "Should I push?" — your pattern tells them.
- Your patterns can create rules even when you never intended to create them. Letting something go once is a choice; letting it go every time is a policy.
- Frame the ethics up front: this chapter is not about lying, manipulating, or becoming erratic so nobody knows what you'll do.

**Example to use:** Someone repeatedly interrupts you in meetings. You let it go three times. Now they've learned something: *Interrupting you has no cost.* You never consciously decided to establish that rule, but your repeated behavior established one anyway.

**On-screen takeaway:** > **Your patterns can create rules even when you never intended to create them.**

### Lesson 4.2 — Rock-Paper-Scissors and the Value of Uncertainty (9 min)

**Teaching points**
- In formal game theory, a **mixed strategy** is one where a player chooses among actions according to probabilities rather than choosing the same action every time.
- If you always throw rock, your opponent learns to throw paper. They don't even need your exact next move; a tendency is enough to adjust.
- A good mixed strategy is not "do something random." It is often a carefully chosen probability distribution that prevents the opponent from exploiting your pattern. You don't need to calculate probabilities to grasp the idea: sometimes uncertainty itself has strategic value.
- Caution: the mental model is not "Randomize everything." Randomization helps only where another player gains by correctly anticipating you.

**Example to use:** Play it out live. You and a partner are playing rock-paper-scissors, and you favor rock 70 percent of the time. Your partner only needs to notice the tilt and lean toward paper. That is what strategic predictability means.

**On-screen takeaway:** > **If they know your pattern, your pattern becomes a weakness.**

### Lesson 4.3 — The Predictability Tax: When Your Pattern Becomes Information (10 min)

**Teaching points**
- Your actions can reveal information you didn't intend to reveal. That hidden cost is the **predictability tax**.
- Every action communicates. Always answering emails immediately signals availability; always threatening to quit but never doing it teaches people your threats aren't credible.
- A bluff that once worked stops working when others learn you bluff a lot. The words didn't change; the other players' beliefs did.
- Giving away your bottom line changes the information structure: you've told them how far they need to push. You can decline without lying: "I'm not prepared to disclose my bottom line."

**Example to use:** A negotiator always makes three concessions: $100, then $90, then $80. The other side learns the pattern. They don't need to know the true minimum; they just need to know *you'll move again*. So they push, you move, they push again. By the third round, your pattern has effectively told them: *There's more room.*

**On-screen takeaway:** > **Your behavior creates information — sometimes more than your words do.**

### Lesson 4.4 — Predictability Is Not the Enemy (9 min)

**Teaching points**
- Coordination requires predictability. At a stoplight, you want everyone to know red means stop. Conventions and norms work because everyone can predict everyone else.
- Predictability can create trust and coordination, or exploitation and strategic vulnerability. The challenge is knowing which environment you're in.
- Don't become unpredictable in the wrong way: tolerating an interruption one day and exploding the next isn't sophistication. It makes you difficult to trust and destroys reliable expectations.
- When rebuilding trust, become *more* predictable. If you say you'll call, call. Over time they learn: *his words predict his actions.*
- A consistently enforced consequence ("falsify customer records and you will be terminated") changes behavior before a violation occurs.

**Example to use:** Two managers. One says, "I really don't like firing people," and employees learn he never actually fires anyone — so some begin taking risks they otherwise wouldn't. The other is known for this: if you violate this rule, there will be consequences. That predictability improves cooperation. Same trait, opposite strategic effect.

**On-screen takeaway:** > **Predictability isn't inherently good or bad. Its value depends on what it allows other players to do.**

### Lesson 4.5 — Managing Predictability: Secrecy, Surprise, and the Feedback Loop (9 min)

**Teaching points**
- Distinguish **manipulative unpredictability** from **strategic unpredictability**. Varying launch timing so a competitor can't optimize against a fixed pattern is competing, not deceiving.
- Secrecy is not deception: withholding information you aren't obligated to disclose differs from deliberately providing false information to induce a decision.
- Don't romanticize surprise. Ask: *Who benefits from knowing what I'm going to do?* If "both of us," predictability helps. If "they can use it to take advantage of me," some uncertainty may be valuable.
- Add learning to the response chain: action → response → learning → adaptation. Your behavior today can change the game tomorrow.
- The Prisoner's Dilemma paradox: in a repeated game, if you know I'll always retaliate against defection, that predictability can support cooperation. The same predictability that makes you exploitable in one game can make you trustworthy in another.
- The four moves: **be predictable** (when trust, coordination, credibility, or cooperation are valuable), **protect your predictability** (when revealing your pattern gives another player leverage), **vary your behavior** (when someone could exploit a fixed pattern), **be deliberately consistent** (when you want someone to understand a consequence is real).

**Example to use:** A competitor who always responds to a price cut with another price cut can be trapped into a predictable escalation. Contrast that with a negotiator asked, "What's the lowest price you'll accept?" who answers, "I'm not prepared to disclose my bottom line." One is trapped by a fixed rule; the other protects information without saying anything false.

**On-screen takeaway:** > **Don't confuse unpredictability with strategic intelligence. The skill is managing predictability.**

## Key terms

| Term | Plain-language meaning |
|---|---|
| Strategic predictability | When someone knows enough about your pattern to adjust and gain an advantage, even without knowing your exact next move. |
| Mixed strategy | Choosing among actions by carefully chosen probabilities so an opponent can't exploit your pattern. Not "doing something random." |
| Predictability tax | The hidden cost of predictable behavior: your actions reveal information you didn't intend to reveal, such as your limits or how far someone needs to push. |
| Unintended rule | A rule other people learn from your repeated behavior even though you never consciously chose it ("Interrupting her has no cost"). |
| Strategic unpredictability | Varying your behavior so another player can't simply optimize against a fixed pattern — legitimate in competition and compatible with honesty. |
| Manipulative unpredictability | Unpredictability used to deceive or to destabilize another person, rather than to prevent legitimate exploitation. |
| Reputation | Accumulated evidence about your future behavior. Every repeated pattern teaches the other player something about you. |
| Feedback loop | Action → response → learning → adaptation. Today's behavior changes tomorrow's game. |

## Research spotlight

**1. Penalty kicks: when being predictable gets punished.** Chiappori, Levitt, and Groseclose (2002) analyzed 459 penalty kicks from the top French and Italian professional soccer leagues. Penalty kicks are a clean strategic setting: opposing goals, known tendencies, high stakes. The researchers found that the testable predictions of mixed-strategy theory fit the data surprisingly well. A later analysis using a different statistical method reached broadly consistent results.

*What it suggests for real conversations:* When another person's gain depends on correctly anticipating you, your predictability changes the payoff of your action. A kick to the left is worth less if the goalkeeper expects it — and a concession is worth less if the other side knows it's coming.

*What it does NOT prove:* Professional soccer is close to a pure zero-sum contest. It does not show that every conversation works like a penalty kick, that "unpredictable people win," or that you should randomize your communication. Nor does it show that people perfectly randomize; related research on baseball pitches (Bhattacharya and Howard) found professionals only approximate the mixed-strategy benchmark, more closely as stakes rise.

**2. Trust games: when being predictable creates value.** Bracht and Feltovich ran a trust-game experiment in which players could see one previous action of the person they were interacting with. Simply making past behavior observable produced substantially higher cooperation and efficiency than the basic trust game, while cheap-talk messages alone had little effect. Past cooperative behavior predicted current cooperative behavior and influenced whether others were willing to trust.

*What it suggests for real conversations:* When cooperation depends on trust, being readable is an asset, and what you've *done* outweighs what you *say* — which is why the book says to rebuild trust by becoming more predictable.

*What it does NOT prove:* It is a simple laboratory game. It does not show that more transparency is always better or that you should disclose everything. Observable track records support cooperation where trust matters — a context-dependent result.

**Taken together:** behavior others can anticipate is a liability in the first setting and an asset in the second — which is why the chapter asks what your predictability enables, rather than offering a universal rule.

## The tool: The Predictability Audit

Use this on one recurring interaction where a pattern may be shaping the game.

**Step 1 — Name the interaction**

The person or group: ______________________________

The recurring situation (meeting, negotiation, household routine, request): ______________________________

**Step 2 — Find the pattern**

What do I repeatedly do in this situation? ______________________________

What have my past actions taught this person to expect from me? ______________________________

Have I taught this person how to respond to me? If so, what is the rule they've learned (finish the sentence "If I ___, she/he will ___")? ______________________________

Did I choose this rule, or did my repeated behavior establish it? ______________________________

**Step 3 — Calculate the predictability tax**

What does this pattern reveal that I didn't intend to reveal (my limits, availability, whether my threats or boundaries are real)? ______________________________

**Step 4 — Asset or liability?**

Who benefits from knowing what I'm going to do? (Circle one: both of us / mainly them / mainly me)

Is this pattern helping me? Helping them? Helping both of us? ______________________________

Could they exploit this pattern? How? ______________________________

Check any that apply. This pattern currently:
- [ ] creates trust, cooperation, or a credible commitment
- [ ] helps others coordinate with me
- [ ] shows people exactly how to pressure me
- [ ] reveals threats, concessions, or unenforced boundaries

**Step 5 — Choose your move**

Would greater consistency create trust — or greater flexibility create strategic room? ______________________________

Which of the four moves fits? (Circle one)
- **Be predictable** — trust, coordination, credibility, or cooperation are valuable here.
- **Protect my predictability** — revealing my pattern gives them leverage.
- **Vary my behavior** — they could otherwise exploit a fixed pattern.
- **Be deliberately consistent** — I want them to understand a consequence is real.

The specific behavior I'll change or reinforce: ______________________________

**Step 6 — Ethics check**

Am I protecting legitimate information or providing false information? ______________________________

Could my change destabilize someone or make me harder to trust where reliability matters? ______________________________

## Practice exercises

### Exercise A — Analyze the scenario

Dana is a senior designer at a marketing agency. For two years, whenever account manager Rob brings her an "urgent" client revision on Friday afternoon, she stays late and finishes it. Lately the Friday requests have multiplied, and several had been sitting in his inbox since Tuesday. Dana has complained in meetings that "people need to plan better" and once told Rob she "wouldn't keep doing this," then stayed late anyway. Dana also reviews junior designers' work every Thursday morning, and the juniors have built their weeks around it. Frustrated, she is considering becoming "harder to read" across the board so nobody takes her for granted.

1. What rule has Rob learned from Dana's behavior, and did she ever choose to create it?
2. What is Dana's predictability tax here? What information has her pattern revealed?
3. Which of Dana's patterns is an asset and which is a liability? Why?
4. Is "becoming harder to read across the board" a good plan? What would you recommend instead, using the four moves?
5. How can Dana change the pattern without deception?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

1. **The learned rule:** "If I bring Dana an urgent revision on Friday, she'll stay late and do it." Dana never chose this rule; her repeated behavior established it. Rob has adapted — the feedback loop of action, response, learning, adaptation has made Friday requests more frequent.

2. **The predictability tax:** Her pattern revealed that her evenings are flexible and her complaints carry no consequence. "I won't keep doing this," followed by doing it anyway, taught Rob her warnings aren't credible. Her behavior communicated more than her words.

3. **Asset vs. liability:** The Thursday review is an asset — the stoplight kind of predictability that helps the juniors coordinate. Both sides benefit from knowing what she'll do. The Friday pattern is a liability: it shows exactly how to pressure her and reveals a boundary that's stated but unenforced.

4. **Not across the board.** That confuses unpredictability with strategic intelligence and would destroy expectations her team depends on. Instead: **be predictable** about the Thursday review, and **be deliberately consistent** about a clear Friday boundary — for example, "Revisions that arrive after 2 p.m. Friday will be done Monday morning unless the client deadline truly requires otherwise." Then enforce it every time. Staying late only sometimes would be the wrong kind of unpredictability; it would keep Rob testing her.

5. **Without deception:** No fake evening plans needed. She states the rule honestly and lets consistent follow-through teach the new pattern. She needn't explain her whole schedule — withholding what she doesn't owe is not lying. Over time Rob learns: "Her words predict her actions."

</details>

### Exercise B — Your own game

Pick one live situation where someone has likely learned to predict you — a coworker, negotiation partner, client, or family member. Then:

1. Complete Steps 1–3 of the Predictability Audit. Write the rule they've learned in one sentence, starting with "If I ___, they will ___."
2. In Step 4, list one way this pattern helps both of you and one way it could be exploited. No exploitation risk? This may be a pattern to protect.
3. In Step 5, choose one of the four moves. Write the exact words or behavior you'll use the next three times this comes up.
4. Run the Step 6 ethics check. If your plan involves saying anything false, rewrite it so you are withholding rather than misrepresenting.

### Exercise C — Partner drill / role-play

**Format:** Pairs, 15 minutes (or with an AI practice partner). Two rounds.

**Round 1 — The concession pattern (6 min)**

*Setup:* A freelance photographer (Seller) is quoting a corporate event. A procurement manager (Buyer) wants the lowest price.

*Seller's private card:* Your opening quote is $4,000. Your true minimum is $3,000. Follow a fixed rule: every time the Buyer rejects your number, drop $300. If asked your minimum, tell the truth.

*Buyer's private card:* You have budget up to $4,000 but want to pay as little as possible. Figure out the Seller's pattern and use it. Ask once, "What's the lowest you'll accept?"

Play for four minutes. Then the Buyer states the rule they think the Seller was following.

**Round 2 — Managing predictability (6 min)**

Same roles, same private numbers, but the Seller now has no fixed rule. The Seller must (a) decline to disclose the bottom line honestly, (b) tie any movement to a reason or a change in scope rather than to rejection alone, and (c) say nothing false. The Buyer again tries to find a pattern.

**Debrief (3 min)**
- In Round 1, how many rejections did it take the Buyer to learn the pattern? What did the Seller's behavior reveal that their words didn't?
- In Round 2, how did "I'm not prepared to disclose my bottom line" change the Buyer's decision-making?
- Did the Seller ever say anything untrue? Where was the line between protecting information and deceiving?

## Common mistakes

- **Treating "keep them guessing" as the lesson.** Game theory doesn't say that. Where trust matters, excessive unpredictability is destructive. Ask what your predictability enables the other player to do first.
- **Becoming unpredictable in the wrong way.** Tolerating something one day and exploding the next doesn't make you strategic; it makes you difficult to trust. Where you need a consequence to be real, be deliberately consistent.
- **Confusing variety with unpredictability.** A rigid sequence of different moves (rock, then paper, then scissors) is still a pattern. If the other player can learn it, it is still exploitable.
- **Stating a boundary but not enforcing it.** A boundary that's predictable but unenforced teaches the opposite of what you said. Your behavior creates more information than your words.
- **Answering every question about your limits.** Giving away your bottom line tells the other side how far they need to push. You can decline honestly; protecting strategically valuable information isn't deception.
- **Crossing from secrecy into deception.** Withholding what you're not obligated to disclose is legitimate. Deliberately providing false information to induce someone into a decision is not.
- **Romanticizing surprise.** Surprise is useful only when the uncertainty it creates is valuable. A spouse or customer gains nothing from wondering whether you'll keep your word.

## Field assignment

**This week: run a pattern log.** Choose one recurring interaction — a weekly meeting, a regular request from a colleague, a household routine, a client relationship. Each time it happens, write down three things: what you did, how the other person responded, and what they might have learned about you from that exchange.

By midweek, complete the Predictability Audit, choose one of the four moves, and carry it out on purpose for the rest of the week. Stay honest throughout — no false statements.

Record:
- The rule you think the other person had learned before the week started.
- Any sign that they noticed a change (testing, surprise, adjustment, relief).
- Whether the change made the interaction better for you, for them, or for both of you.

**Debrief questions for next week:**
1. What had your past actions taught this person to expect from you — and how accurate was your guess once you watched closely?
2. Did they adapt to your new pattern? What does that tell you about the feedback loop of action, response, learning, and adaptation?
3. Where in your life would greater consistency create trust, and where would greater flexibility create strategic room?
