# Module 2 — See the Payoffs

> **The question:** What payoff would make their behavior make sense?

## Why this module matters

In Module 1 you learned to see the game before choosing your move. But identifying the players isn't enough. You also have to understand what each player is trying to accomplish. And here is a mistake we make all the time: we assume other people want what we want. When they don't behave the way we expect, we conclude that they're irrational, difficult, selfish, stubborn, or just wrong. Sometimes they are. But often they're simply responding to a different set of incentives. They're playing the same game, but they're playing for a different payoff.

A payoff is simply what makes an outcome better or worse for a player. Money is one payoff. So are time, security, status, fairness, reputation, control, relationships, and identity. If you only look at the dollar amount, you can completely misunderstand the game.

This module builds one mental shift. Instead of asking, "Why would they do that?" you'll ask, "What payoff would make that behavior make sense?" That doesn't mean you'll agree with them or excuse them. It means you stop treating your own preferences as the universal standard for rational behavior. And it is understanding, not manipulation: the goal is never to find someone's hot button and push it, but to understand the incentive structure before choosing your move.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this module you will be able to:

1. Define a payoff in plain language and list the non-monetary currencies (status, fairness, autonomy, security, identity, relationships, and others) that can drive a decision.
2. Distinguish opposed, aligned, and mixed interests in a real interaction, and identify which dimensions of a disagreement are conflicting and which are shared.
3. Separate stated preferences from revealed preferences, and what happened from what each person thinks happened.
4. Identify what each player is trying to avoid, not just what they are trying to gain, including how losses and reference points shape perceived payoffs.
5. Complete a Payoff Map for an important interaction and use it to choose a move that respects the other person's autonomy and dignity.

## Lessons

### Lesson 2.1 — What is a payoff? (~10 min)

**Teaching points**
- In game theory, a player's payoff is what they value about an outcome. Economists use numbers to compare outcomes, but you don't need numbers to use the concept. The working definition: *What makes this outcome better or worse for this player?*
- Money is one currency among many: time, security, status, freedom, fairness, reputation, control, relationships, avoiding embarrassment, reducing risk, protecting one's identity.
- Sometimes the payoff is what the outcome represents. A person may fight over $500 because of what conceding it means: respect, principle, or not being taken advantage of.
- The most important question: *What are they actually trying to get out of this?* Not what you think they should want, not what you'd want in their shoes, and not even necessarily what they said they want.
- People don't make decisions based on what would make *you* happy. They make decisions based on what they value.

**Example to use:** The raise you didn't get. You want more money and assume your boss wants to save money. But your boss may also want to retain good people, stay within budget, avoid an expensive precedent, keep the team happy, and avoid explaining the raise upstairs. Your boss may even *want* to give you the raise but believe they can't afford it. The game shifts from "they don't want to pay me" to "We both want me to stay, but we have different constraints about how compensation is structured."

**On-screen takeaway:** > **A payoff is whatever makes an outcome better or worse for that player, and money is only one currency.**

### Lesson 2.2 — Opposed, aligned, and mixed interests (~10 min)

**Teaching points**
- Interests have different structures. Sometimes they're strongly opposed, sometimes largely aligned, and very often mixed.
- **Opposed interests:** what benefits me hurts you. Zero-sum games like chess are the strong form, and they're rarer in everyday relationships than we imagine.
- **Aligned interests:** we both benefit from the same outcome. The problem is often coordination, not conflict: "How do we get our actions aligned?"
- **Mixed interests:** where most of human life happens. We both want the deal, company, or relationship to succeed, but disagree about how.
- Replace "How do I beat them?" with "Where are our interests actually aligned, and where are they opposed?"
- The pie doesn't have to be fixed. Before asking "Who gets what?" ask "Can we create more value before deciding how to divide it?" Conflict over one dimension doesn't mean conflict over everything.
- But don't turn this into a "there's always a win-win" philosophy. Sometimes payoffs really are incompatible. Misdiagnose a conflict as cooperation and you'll make bad decisions; misdiagnose cooperation as pure conflict and you'll leave value on the table.

**Example to use:** Buying a house. You want to pay $500,000; the seller wants $550,000. You conflict over price but align on completing the transaction. And in a business deal where the seller cares most about getting paid quickly while you care about paying over time, restructuring the terms can increase the total value even though you still disagree about the final allocation.

**On-screen takeaway:** > **Conflict over one dimension doesn't mean conflict over everything.**

### Lesson 2.3 — Hidden payoffs and why "rational" doesn't mean "selfish" (~10 min)

**Teaching points**
- The visible payoff may not be the complete payoff. Someone who refuses to apologize may care about the relationship but believe apologizing means admitting they were entirely at fault: relationship plus dignity plus self-image. The lesson is *not* "everyone has a secret hidden motive."
- Empathy and game theory meet here. Don't imagine yourself in their position with *your* preferences. Reconstruct their game: What do they value? What are they afraid of losing? What alternatives and constraints do they have? What would feel like winning, or losing?
- The Prisoner's Dilemma shows that payoff structure can push decent people toward a behavior. Sometimes behavior is produced by the structure of the game rather than the character of the players.
- The Ultimatum Game shows people often reject offers they see as unfair, even when rejecting leaves them with nothing. If people consistently sacrifice money to punish perceived unfairness, fairness is part of the game.
- "That person isn't being rational" often means "That person isn't maximizing the thing I assumed they cared about."
- Boundary: understanding someone's incentives doesn't mean their behavior is justified. Game theory explains why a behavior may make sense; it doesn't tell us whether it's good, moral, or wise.

**Example to use:** The lucrative job. You turn down a high-paying job because it would mean missing your children's lives. Financially, it looks irrational. But if your priorities include family, it makes perfect sense. The observer misunderstood your payoff function.

**On-screen takeaway:** > **Sometimes the behavior we observe is being produced by the structure of the game rather than the character of the players.**

### Lesson 2.4 — Gains, losses, perception, and revealed preferences (~10 min)

**Teaching points**
- Ask: *What are they trying to avoid?* Embarrassment, uncertainty, conflict, lost status, financial risk, looking incompetent, being controlled. Same upside, different perceived downside: "Let's try it" versus "What if it fails?"
- Kahneman and Tversky's prospect theory: choices often depend on gains and losses relative to a reference point, and losses can weigh more than equivalent gains in many contexts (not always).
- Perception is part of the game. The same deal can read as "I'm getting $10,000" or "I'm losing $5,000 compared with what I expected." Distinguish what happened from what each person thinks happened.
- Stated versus revealed preferences: choices give you information beyond words. That doesn't mean people are lying; they often don't know their preferences until they face a real choice.
- The payoff isn't always immediate. People take short-term losses to protect reputation or relationships.
- The payoff can change. Switch from individual bonuses to a team bonus and the people don't change; the game does. Before trying to change behavior, understand what the environment rewards.

**Example to use:** A manager gives two employees more responsibility. Employee A thinks, "They trust me." Employee B thinks, "They're dumping more work on me." Same action, different perceived payoff, different likely response.

**On-screen takeaway:** > **The same objective outcome can produce different decisions depending on what someone perceives themselves as gaining or losing.**

### Lesson 2.5 — The Payoff Map and how it changes communication (~5 min)

**Teaching points**
- The Payoff Map adds a second layer to the game map from Module 1: what I want, what they want, where we align, where we conflict, what we're each trying to avoid, and what I might be missing.
- The most important question on the map: *Am I assuming their payoff is the same as mine?*
- Before asking "How do I convince them?" ask "What payoff are they protecting?" It might be money, time, status, control, security, identity, fairness, reputation, autonomy, or a relationship.
- Knowing what someone values tells you what they'll likely respond to, resist, or see as a threat. That is not license to "find their hot button and push it." That's tactics.
- The map may help you persuade, negotiate, or cooperate, or tell you agreement isn't possible, or that you're solving the wrong problem.

**Example to use:** The curfew. A parent says, "Be home by midnight." The teenager says, "That's ridiculous." Framed as parent versus teenager, it's a standoff. Seen as safety versus independence, both positions make sense.

**On-screen takeaway:** > **Understand the incentive structure before choosing your move.**

## Key terms

| Term | Plain-language meaning |
|---|---|
| Payoff | What makes an outcome better or worse for a particular player. |
| Payoff function | The full set of things a person values and how they weigh them. |
| Opposed interests | What benefits one player hurts the other; zero-sum is the strong form. |
| Aligned interests | Both benefit from the same outcome; the challenge is coordination. |
| Mixed interests | Shared goals on some dimensions, conflict on others. |
| Hidden payoff | A value such as status or dignity that drives a decision but isn't the visible stake. |
| Ultimatum Game | One person proposes a split; the other accepts or rejects, and rejection leaves both with nothing. |
| Reference point | The baseline, often an expectation, against which an outcome feels like a gain or a loss. |
| Stated vs. revealed preference | What a person says they want vs. what their choices show they value. |

## Research spotlight

**1. The Ultimatum Game: fairness is part of the payoff**

*What was done.* One person proposes how to divide a sum of money; the responder accepts (both get the proposed amounts) or rejects (both get nothing). Güth, Schmittberger, and Schwarze's 1982 experiment is the classic starting point. A later meta-analysis pooled 37 papers containing 75 experimental results, and Slonim and Roth (Econometrica, 1998) ran versions in Slovakia with stakes varied by a factor of 25.

*What was found.* A money-only model says accept anything positive, since $1 beats $0. People often don't. In the meta-analysis, proposers offered about 40% of the pie on average and responders rejected about 16% of offers; rejection was less common with larger offers and larger total stakes. Slonim and Roth found that raising stakes had only a small effect on inexperienced players: rejections fell somewhat, and experienced proposers gradually offered less.

*What it suggests.* The payoff isn't only "How much did I get?" It can also be "How was I treated?" Price only the material outcome and an insulted person's walkout will look irrational when it isn't.

*What it does NOT prove.* Not that people always choose fairness over money, or that money doesn't motivate people. The weight on fairness varies across people, cultures, situations, and stakes, and higher stakes reduce some of these effects without eliminating them.

**2. Falk and Kosfeld: autonomy can be part of the payoff**

*What was done.* Armin Falk and Michael Kosfeld (American Economic Review, 2006) ran a principal-agent game in which principals could impose a minimum performance requirement on agents.

*What was found.* Instead of performing better under control, most agents reduced performance in response to it. Many described the control as a signal of distrust and a limit on their autonomy.

*What it suggests.* Changing someone's material options can also change what the outcome means to them. A rule meant to secure performance can read as "I don't trust you."

*What it does NOT prove.* Not that oversight always backfires; the evidence is good but context-dependent. Similarly, Gneezy and Rustichini's daycare study (a late-pickup fine followed by more late pickups) is a vivid example that incentives can change social meaning, but a later replication attempt found deterrent effects from fines. Treat these as possibilities, not universal laws.

## Practice exercises

### Exercise A — Analyze the scenario

Dana runs a small marketing agency. Her best designer, Marcus, has been there four years. Dana offers him a promotion to creative director with a 15% raise, and she expects him to be thrilled. Instead, Marcus hesitates and asks for a week to think. In a hallway conversation he mentions that the new role "means a lot more meetings" and that he'd "stop doing the actual design work." A few days later Dana learns from a colleague that Marcus was hurt that the promotion was announced to the leadership team before it was offered to him. Dana is frustrated: "I'm offering him more money and a better title. Why is he being so difficult?" She is considering adding another 5% to close the deal. Marcus has said several times that he "doesn't care about titles," but last year he turned down a higher-paying offer from a larger firm.

1. What payoff is Dana assuming Marcus has, and what is the evidence that his payoff function is different?
2. List at least three currencies that may be in play for Marcus, including at least one he's trying to avoid.
3. Is the interest structure between Dana and Marcus opposed, aligned, or mixed? Where specifically?
4. What does his past behavior (the turned-down offer) reveal compared with what he has stated?
5. Why might adding another 5% fail to solve the problem, and what would a better next move look like?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

1. Dana assumes Marcus values what she'd value: more money and a bigger title. The evidence says otherwise: he hesitated, he described the role in terms of what he'd lose, and he was hurt by how the offer was handled. "Why is he being so difficult?" signals that she may be misreading his payoff function, not that he's irrational.

2. Likely currencies: **identity and craft** (he sees himself as a designer, and the role takes him away from "the actual design work"); **autonomy and time** (more meetings, less control over his day); **respect** (leadership heard before he did). He may be trying to avoid losing the work he's good at or struggling in a management role he never sought. Money matters, but it doesn't look like the binding payoff.

3. Mixed. Aligned: both want Marcus to stay and the agency's creative work to be excellent. Opposed: Dana needs someone to carry management; Marcus may not want it.

4. Turning down a higher-paying offer is a revealed preference that backs up his stated one: money isn't his top currency. It isn't proof, but it's meaningful information.

5. Another 5% doubles down on the currency that isn't driving his decision, and could even read as "she thinks I can be bought." A better move is to ask, not push: acknowledge the announcement misstep, then ask what a good outcome would look like for him and what would feel like a loss. That may reveal a value-creating option, such as a senior designer track with a raise and mentoring but no management load. Or it may show the interests are genuinely incompatible, which is better to know than to pretend a win-win exists.

</details>

### Exercise B — Your own game

Pick a live situation where someone's behavior frustrates or puzzles you: a coworker resisting a plan, a family member refusing a reasonable request, a client who won't commit.

1. Write down the sentence you've been thinking ("Why would they..." or "They're being..."), then rewrite it as "What payoff would make that behavior make sense?"
2. Complete the Payoff Map for this situation.
3. Answer: "What outcome would make this person feel that they got what they wanted?" and "What outcome would make them feel that they lost?"
4. Note one place where you've treated their stated preference as the whole story, and one choice of theirs that reveals something else.
5. Decide whether the interests are opposed, aligned, or mixed. If they're genuinely opposed, write that down plainly; knowing what kind of game you're in is useful too.
6. Draft one honest, open question that would test your map rather than confirm it.

### Exercise C — Partner drill / role-play

**Time:** 15 minutes. **Format:** pairs, or one person with an AI practice partner.

**Setup:** Two co-owners of a small bakery are deciding what to do with this year's $60,000 profit.

**Jordan (private):** You want to reinvest most of it in a second location. Growth matters a lot, and your biggest fear is the business plateauing and looking small-time to people who doubted you. You're comfortable with risk.

**Riley (private):** You want to distribute most of it. You're quietly helping a parent with medical bills, so financial security matters a lot and debt makes you anxious. You'd like the business to grow, just not in a way that risks your income this year.

**Round 1 (5 min):** Argue positions only. Try to convince each other.

**Round 2 (7 min):** Start over. Your only job now is to fill in the other person's Payoff Map by asking: What are you trying to gain? What are you trying to avoid? What would feel like a loss? Share only what you're comfortable sharing. Then look for a deal that creates value before dividing it.

**Debrief questions:**
- In Round 1, what did you assume the other person was optimizing for? How close were you?
- What payoffs surfaced in Round 2 that never came up in Round 1?
- Did you find an arrangement (for example, a smaller expansion plus a guaranteed distribution) that gave each of you more of what you cared about most? If not, was the conflict real?
- Did anyone feel pushed or "worked"? What separates understanding a payoff from exploiting it?

## Common mistakes

- **Assuming they want what you want** — The mistake the chapter is built around. Ask "Am I assuming their payoff is the same as mine?" before interpreting their behavior.
- **Calling someone "irrational" too fast** — It usually means they aren't maximizing what you assumed they cared about. Ask what payoff would make the behavior make sense.
- **Treating money as the only currency** — Status, fairness, autonomy, identity, and relationships can matter as much or more.
- **Turning hidden payoffs into conspiracy theories** — The lesson isn't that everyone has a secret motive; it's that the visible payoff may not be the complete payoff.
- **Assuming every interaction is zero-sum, or that there's always a win-win** — Most interactions are mixed. Identify conflict accurately rather than inventing it or wishing it away.
- **Taking stated preferences as the whole story** — Watch choices as well as words, without concluding people are lying.
- **Confusing understanding with excusing, or with exploiting** — Seeing why a behavior makes sense doesn't make it good, and knowing what someone values is not a hot button to push.

## Field assignment

This week, catch yourself in one "Why would they do that?" moment: a moment of resistance, disagreement, or unexpected behavior from someone at work or at home. Before you respond or judge, pause and complete a quick Payoff Map, even if it's only a few lines on your phone. Then, if it's appropriate, ask one honest, open question to test your map, such as "What would a good outcome look like for you here?" or "What are you most worried about?"

Record:
- The behavior you saw and your first interpretation of it.
- The payoffs you guessed (gain and avoid) before the conversation.
- What you learned from their answer or their next choice.
- Whether the interests turned out to be opposed, aligned, or mixed.

**Debrief questions for next week:**
1. Where was your first interpretation based on assuming their payoff was the same as yours?
2. What payoff did you miss, and which category was it (status, fairness, autonomy, security, identity, relationship, something else)?
3. Did understanding their payoffs change your next move, or did it confirm that your interests were genuinely opposed? What did you do with that knowledge?
