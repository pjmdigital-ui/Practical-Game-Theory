# Module 12 — Strategic Judgment

> **The question:** What did I learn from what just happened?

| | |
|---|---|
| **Book reading** | Conclusion, "Strategic Judgment" |
| **Time** | ~60 min of video lessons · ~75 min of workbook · one week of field practice, plus the Capstone project (two to three weeks) |
| **Tool you'll build** | The Strategic Judgment Worksheet (the Eleven Questions) and the Model → Act → Observe → Update → Adapt Practice Log |

## Why this module matters

You now have eleven lenses. The risk at this point is not that you know too little. It is that you try to use everything, everywhere, all at once. The book is blunt about this: the eleven models are not eleven steps. You are not supposed to walk into a conversation and think, "Step one: identify the game. Step two: identify the payoffs." An experienced driver doesn't recite "check mirror, calculate distance, adjust steering angle." They have developed perception. The goal of this course was never recitation. It was recognition.

Strategic judgment is the skill that sits above the models. It means choosing the two or three lenses that matter right now, knowing when a situation doesn't need analysis at all ("Sure" sometimes just means "Sure"), keeping "Will this work?" separate from "Should I do it?", and treating your read of any situation as provisional. Your first model will often be wrong. The person you thought cared about money cares about status. The person you thought was angry is scared. The threat you thought was a bluff was a commitment.

So this module adds one rule over all eleven models: **your model of the game is provisional, so update it from play.** The loop that holds everything together is Model → Act → Observe → Update → Adapt. That is what strategic thinking is. You won't predict perfectly or be right every time. What you can do is learn faster from what happens. The module ends with the Capstone, where you put the whole framework to work on one real interaction that matters to you.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this module you will be able to:

1. **Select** the two or three lenses that matter most in a given interaction, and justify why the others can wait.
2. **Distinguish** situations that warrant strategic analysis from ordinary interactions where more analysis would only add suspicion and confusion.
3. **Separate** facts from your story about the facts, state your confidence, and hold several competing hypotheses at once.
4. **Apply** the Model → Act → Observe → Update → Adapt loop, treating surprise and prediction errors as information.
5. **Evaluate** any planned move against both "Will this work?" and "Should I do it?", using truth, autonomy, dignity, and informed choice.

## Lessons

### Lesson 12.1 — Recognition, not recitation (12 min)

**Teaching points**
- The eleven models are lenses, not a procedure. At first you'll ask the questions consciously. Over time you'll start noticing the structure almost automatically.
- Recognition sounds like short internal prompts: "What payoff are they protecting?" "Would they actually want to carry that out?" "This is a pattern, not an incident." "What makes that credible?" "They're not opposed. They don't know what the other is going to do." "The incentive system rewards the exact opposite behavior."
- The world will not label the game for you. You'll get symptoms, like "We need an answer today," a child asking again, employees who stop sharing leads, or the same argument with your partner. Your job is to recognize the structure underneath.

**Example to use:** The book's list of disguised games. Nobody walks into your office and announces "an incomplete-information bargaining game containing asymmetric outside options and a credibility problem." They say "We need an answer today." Your partner won't say "We have entered a stable negative equilibrium reinforced by reciprocal expectations." You'll just have the same argument again.

**On-screen takeaway:** > **Reality gives you the symptoms. You have to recognize the structure.**

### Lesson 12.2 — Choosing the right lens, and knowing when to put it down (14 min)

**Teaching points**
- Walk through the book's "final offer by tomorrow" example. Every one of the eleven lenses produces a useful question. Who has authority? What happens to them if I say no? Have I taught them that artificial pressure makes me move? What makes "final offer" believable? Do I need to accept their yes-or-no structure at all?
- You probably don't need all eleven. Judgment means recognizing which two or three matter most right now.
- More analysis is not always better. Use the framework when the stakes matter, something doesn't make sense, a pattern keeps repeating, incentives are unclear, trust is uncertain, multiple people affect the outcome, or your normal way of thinking isn't working.
- Strategic thinking should reduce confusion. If it makes ordinary life needlessly suspicious and complicated, you're using the tool badly.
- Strategic thinking is not suspicious thinking. Suspicion asks "What are they hiding?" and assumes hostile intent. Strategic thinking asks "What possibilities should I consider?" and tests hypotheses. The framework should make you less naive. It should not make you cynical.

**Example to use:** The friend who texts "Sure." Then the overanalysis: "What strategic signal does the absence of an exclamation point communicate about their latent payoff structure?" Don't do that. Sometimes "Sure" means "Sure."

**On-screen takeaway:** > **Which two or three lenses matter most right now?**

### Lesson 12.3 — Your model is provisional: the learning loop (14 min)

**Teaching points**
- Everything depends on your interpretation of the game, and your interpretation can be wrong. Don't fall in love with your diagnosis. Test it.
- The loop: **Model** (form your best understanding), **Act** (make a move), **Observe** (what actually happens?), **Update** (where did reality differ from your prediction?), **Adapt** (change your strategy, or change your model). Then repeat.
- Your mistakes are information. If you explain your reasoning clearly and they understand perfectly but still refuse, maybe it was never an information problem. Maybe your interests are genuinely opposed.
- Surprise is a gift. They didn't cooperate, so ask what you misunderstood. They stayed, so ask what payoff you underestimated. They followed through, so ask what made the commitment stronger than you realized. The new rule made things worse, so ask what response the design created that you failed to anticipate.
- Don't label someone irrational too quickly. Ask, "What would have to be true for this behavior to make sense?" They might have information you don't, a different payoff, a longer game, a different reading of your behavior, or history you're discounting. And yes, they might simply be making a mistake.

**Example to use:** The refused request. You predict, "If I refuse, they'll become angry." You refuse. They say, "No problem. I completely understand." Good. You were wrong, and that's valuable. Maybe you overestimated their dependency, misread their payoff, or believed the relationship was more fragile than it was.

**On-screen takeaway:** > **Model → act → observe → update → adapt.**

### Lesson 12.4 — Facts, stories, and multiple hypotheses (10 min)

**Teaching points**
- Separate the fact from the interpretation. Fact: they didn't return your call. Interpretation: they're disrespecting you. That's possible, but not the same thing. Maybe they forgot, are overwhelmed, never got the message, are avoiding the conversation, or are delaying on purpose.
- The discipline has three parts: "Here's what I know. Here's what I think it means. Here's how confident I am." This keeps you responding to reality instead of to an assumption.
- Strong strategists hold several hypotheses at once, then ask questions or watch for behavior that helps tell them apart.
- Certainty feels good. Accuracy is better.

**Example to use:** The customer who suddenly becomes resistant. It could be a price objection, a lack of trust, a need for someone else's approval, a play to improve their negotiating position, or a polite cover for having already chosen a competitor. You don't need to choose one immediately.

**On-screen takeaway:** > **Here's what I know. Here's what I think it means. Here's how confident I am.**

### Lesson 12.5 — Should I do it? Ethics, vulnerability, walking away (10 min)

**Teaching points**
- Truth, autonomy, dignity, and informed choice are not decorative. They constrain how this framework should be used.
- A strategy can "work" and still be wrong. You may be able to exploit someone's ignorance, press on an insecurity, or hide information so they choose what you prefer. Doing so means they did not make an informed choice. Keep two questions apart, "Will this work?" and "Should I do it?", and never collapse them into one.
- Cooperation isn't automatically good. People can cooperate to build a hospital or to commit a fraud. The ethical question is "What are they cooperating toward?"
- People are not pieces on a board. Calling someone a "player" reminds you that they are a decision-maker too, with agency and a different vantage point. That should make you more respectful of their agency, not less.
- Sometimes the correct move is vulnerability. In a repeated relationship where both sides protect themselves, a measured risk (a disclosure, a concession, an apology) can change beliefs and shift the equilibrium. The risk is exactly why the action carries information.
- Sometimes the correct move is walking away. If you have communicated clearly, tested assumptions, enforced boundaries, allowed for repair, and the game is still destructive, then stop playing. An outside option is part of the game. Know which games you refuse to play.

**Example to use:** "You never listen to me." The tactical reflex is to pick a technique, like active listening or mirroring. See the game first. Is this a repeated pattern? What payoff are they seeking: understanding, agreement, respect, or attention? Have your past responses taught them that raising a concern leads to defensiveness? Are you in an equilibrium where they criticize because they expect you not to listen, and you defend because you expect them to criticize? Is this a coordination failure about what "being heard" means? You may still use a technique, but now it serves an understanding of the situation.

**On-screen takeaway:** > **Will this work? Should I do it? Never collapse them into one.**

## Key terms

| Term | Plain-language meaning |
|---|---|
| Strategic judgment | Choosing which lenses matter in this situation, acting on your best model, and learning from what happens. |
| Recognition | Noticing the structure of an interaction almost automatically, as opposed to reciting steps. |
| Provisional model | Your current best interpretation of the game, held as a hypothesis to test, never as a conclusion to defend. |
| Model → Act → Observe → Update → Adapt | The learning loop: form a model, make a move, watch what happens, find where reality differed from your prediction, then change your strategy or your model. |
| Prediction error / surprise | The gap between what you expected and what happened. It's the most useful signal that your model has a hole in it. |
| Multiple hypotheses | Holding several plausible explanations at once and gathering evidence that tells them apart. |
| Transfer | Recognizing a familiar structure (commitment, coordination, repeated game, mechanism design) in a situation you've never seen before. |
| Calibration | Making intuition trustworthy by checking it against outcomes, so confidence rises only as accuracy does. |

## Research spotlight

The Conclusion introduces no new experiments. Its evidence base comes from the research audit behind the whole framework. Two findings from that audit explain why this module exists.

**1. Learning in games.** The full-framework audit compared the eleven models with the topics covered in university game-theory curricula. Those curricula include learning in games (evolutionary and non-equilibrium learning) as well as equilibrium analysis. The dossier notes that researchers such as Fudenberg and Levine have argued that learning models improve game theory's descriptive power, and that experimental evidence shows behavior can change substantially through experience rather than starting at equilibrium.
- *What it suggests:* Real players don't arrive already knowing the solution. They adjust from feedback, and so should you. That is why the audit made "Model → act → observe → update → adapt" a meta-rule for the whole book instead of a twelfth model.
- *What it does NOT prove:* It doesn't show that any particular person will learn quickly, or learn the right lesson. Experience without feedback can entrench a wrong story. Learning models also describe patterns across many players and rounds, so they don't predict what one individual will do in your next conversation.

**2. The audit's completeness test.** The audit asked whether any major area of game theory (bargaining, coalitions, dominance, social dilemmas, auctions, voting, networks) needed its own model. It applied one test: *does the concept give an ordinary person a genuinely different and broadly useful way to see strategic human interaction?* The answer was to keep eleven models and fold the other ideas in as supporting concepts. Examples include outside options and coalitions under See the Game, dominance under Think in Responses, and the coordination versus free-rider distinction under Diagnose the Coordination Problem.
- *What it suggests:* The framework is meant as "the smallest useful latticework of game-theoretic lenses for human interaction," not as all of game theory. Choosing lenses well matters more than owning more of them.
- *What it does NOT prove:* Eleven is a design judgment, not an empirical discovery. The audit doesn't claim the models are exhaustive, and it doesn't claim that a situation fits neatly into one of them.

## Practice exercises

### Exercise A — Analyze the scenario

Dana is the operations director at a regional logistics company. For the third quarter in a row, the sales team has promised clients delivery windows her warehouse can't meet, and her team absorbs the overtime and the angry calls. On Monday, the VP of Sales, Marcus, emails her and copies the CEO: "We just landed the Harlan account. They need three-day turnaround starting next month. This is non-negotiable. The CEO is aware." Dana's first impulse is to reply-all with a detailed list of every missed promise from the last nine months. She knows Marcus's team is paid commission on signed contracts, not on retained or profitable accounts. She doesn't know whether the CEO actually approved three-day turnaround or was simply told about the deal. She'll work with Marcus for years, and the Harlan account would be good for the company if it could be served reliably. Two of her shift leads have privately told her they're close to quitting.

1. Which two or three lenses matter most here, and which can Dana set aside for now?
2. Separate the facts from Dana's likely story. What competing hypotheses should she hold about Marcus and the CEO?
3. What is wrong with Dana's first impulse, judged by both "Will this work?" and "Should I do it?"
4. Design a move and state a prediction. Then describe what Dana should do in the Observe and Update steps if her prediction turns out wrong.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

**1. The lenses that matter.** Three stand out.
- **See the Payoffs / Change the Game.** Sales is paid on signed contracts, not on deliverable ones. Over-promising is the rational response to that incentive, so this is a pattern the system produces, not one bad act by Marcus. A better email won't fix it. The durable fix sits at the level of the game, for example a sign-off rule for delivery terms or commission tied partly to retention.
- **Map Information & Beliefs / Separate Words From Credibility.** "Non-negotiable" and "The CEO is aware" are claims. What makes them credible? "Aware" is not the same as "approved three-day turnaround knowing warehouse capacity." Dana doesn't know what the CEO knows, and the CEO may not know about the overtime and the shift leads who are about to quit.
- **The Shadow of the Future.** Dana and Marcus will play this game for years, and her response sets a precedent. If she quietly absorbs this one, she teaches the organization that operations will always bend (which also touches Manage Predictability).

She can set aside, for now, Diagnose the Coordination Problem (the underlying interests aren't really aligned while the incentive stays as it is) and a detailed Think in Responses tree for Marcus alone. Marcus may not be the whole game. The CEO is the decisive player.

**2. Facts vs. story.** Facts: three quarters of missed promises, the commission structure, the email, the CC, the shift leads' warnings. Story: "Marcus is deliberately throwing my team under the bus." Competing hypotheses: (H1) Marcus knows the windows are unrealistic and is using the CEO's name as pressure. (H2) Marcus genuinely doesn't understand warehouse constraints. (H3) The CEO did approve three-day turnaround but without capacity data. (H4) The CEO approved it and plans to add resources Dana hasn't heard about. A question to the CEO will distinguish H1/H2 from H3/H4.

**3. The reply-all list.** *Will it work?* Probably not. It turns a design problem into a public fight, invites Marcus to defend himself, and makes Dana look like an obstacle in front of the CEO. Then what? Marcus escalates and the pattern hardens. *Should she do it?* Her facts may be true, but publicly shaming a colleague is a dignity problem, and the "gotcha" framing aims to win the exchange rather than to give the CEO the information needed for an informed choice.

**4. A move.** Dana replies to both, briefly and without blame: "Glad we landed Harlan, and I want us to keep them. To commit to three-day turnaround reliably, here's what it would take [staffing, shift, cost], and here's what we can guarantee today [four days, with three-day by a set date]. Can the three of us take 20 minutes this week to decide which we commit to?" She also asks the CEO separately for a broader conversation about how delivery terms get approved. She doesn't need to name the commission plan yet, but she brings data on the three quarters of missed windows and overtime cost.

*Prediction:* the CEO, given capacity information, picks a staged commitment, and Marcus accepts because keeping Harlan protects his future commissions.

*If she's wrong.* Say the CEO replies, "Just make three days work." Dana should update rather than label anyone irrational. Maybe the CEO has information she doesn't, like a strategic reason Harlan matters more than its margin. Maybe her capacity numbers weren't credible to someone outside operations. Her adaptations could be to ask what's behind the urgency, to make the cost concrete and verifiable, or to tie her agreement to a specific commitment of resources. If over several rounds the game stays destructive (promises without resources, staff leaving), strategic judgment may tell her that her own outside options deserve a serious look.

### Exercise B — Your own game

Pick one live situation from your own life that meets at least two of the book's criteria: the stakes matter, something doesn't make sense, a pattern keeps repeating, incentives are unclear, trust is uncertain, multiple people affect the outcome, or your normal approach isn't working.

1. Write the facts in one paragraph with no adjectives or motives. Then write your story in a second paragraph. Put a confidence percentage on the story.
2. Scan all eleven questions in the Strategic Judgment Worksheet for about 60 seconds each. Then circle no more than three. For each lens you set aside, write one sentence explaining why.
3. Write at least three competing hypotheses. For each, name one question you could ask or one behavior you could watch for that would separate it from the others.
4. Draft your move and your prediction. Run the four ethics checks. If any one fails, redesign the move before going further.
5. Ask yourself whether there is a version of this where the best move is vulnerability, or one where it is walking away. Write one sentence on each.
6. Enter the situation as the first row of your Practice Log. You'll return to it in the Field assignment and may choose it for your Capstone.

### Exercise C — Partner drill / role-play

**"Two Lenses, Then Update"** (15 minutes, pairs or trios, or with an AI practice partner)

**Setup.** A small nonprofit's development director (Player A) is meeting a longtime major donor (Player B). The donor has given $50,000 a year for six years. This year the donor says, "I'm thinking of pausing my gift. I'll decide by Friday."

**Player A privately knows:** The organization recently changed its program focus. The board chair told A that the donor "always comes around." A has no information about the donor's finances.

**Player B privately knows (choose one card at random and don't reveal it):**
- *Card 1:* B is upset that nobody told them about the program change before it was announced publicly. B feels taken for granted and wants to be consulted.
- *Card 2:* B's business had a bad year and B is embarrassed to say so. B would like to give a smaller amount without losing face.
- *Card 3:* B's adult daughter has started her own nonprofit and B feels torn. B is open to a joint project.

**Round 1 (5 min).** Before speaking, Player A names out loud the two lenses they'll lead with and one hypothesis they're testing. A then conducts the conversation. A's goal is to tell the hypotheses apart, not to secure the gift.

**Round 2 (3 min).** Pause. A states what they observed, what surprised them, and how they're updating. B says nothing yet.

**Round 3 (4 min).** A adapts and continues. A must stay within truth, autonomy, dignity, and informed choice. That means no inventing urgency, no guilt, and no misrepresenting the program change.

**Debrief (3 min).**
- Which card did A think B held, and when? What evidence moved the estimate?
- Did the board chair's "always comes around" belief help or hurt? (It's a story, not a fact.)
- Did A respect B's ability to say no? Would B feel comfortable if they saw A's full reasoning?
- Which lens turned out to matter most, and was it one A chose at the start?

## Common mistakes

- **Running all eleven models every time.** The models are lenses, not a checklist. Full analysis of everything is slow and noisy. Instead, pick the two or three that matter most right now.
- **Analyzing interactions that don't need it.** Turning "Sure" into a signaling puzzle makes ordinary life needlessly suspicious and complicated. Save the framework for high stakes, recurring patterns, unclear incentives, and uncertain trust.
- **Falling in love with your diagnosis.** Your first model will often be wrong. Treat it as provisional and look for evidence that would disprove it.
- **Labeling people irrational.** That word often replaces thinking. Ask what would have to be true for the behavior to make sense, while allowing that sometimes people simply make mistakes.
- **Confusing strategic with suspicious.** Suspicion assumes hostile intent and reads ambiguity negatively. Strategic thinking recognizes ambiguity and tests hypotheses. Become less naive without becoming cynical.
- **Collapsing "Will this work?" into "Should I do it?"** A strategy can work and still violate truth, autonomy, dignity, or informed choice. Ask both questions every time.
- **Trusting uncalibrated intuition.** Experience without feedback can produce confident error. Use the Practice Log so your intuition earns its trust.

## Field assignment

**This week: run the loop five times.**

Choose five interactions this week that clear the bar for analysis. At least one should involve someone you'll deal with again, and at least one should involve more than two people. For each one:

1. Before acting, take the pause between stimulus and response. Write your story, your confidence, and the one or two lenses you're leading with.
2. Make a specific prediction: "If I do X, they'll do Y."
3. Act, then log in the Practice Log what actually happened.
4. Score your prediction as *right*, *partly right*, or *wrong*, and fill in the Update and Adapt columns.

Also record one ordinary interaction where you noticed yourself starting to over-analyze and chose not to.

**Debrief questions for next week**
1. How many of your five predictions were right? In the ones that were wrong, was the error mostly about payoffs, information, credibility, or the structure of the game?
2. Which surprise taught you the most, and how did you change your model because of it?
3. Did any move tempt you toward something that would "work" but fail the truth, autonomy, dignity, or informed-choice check? What did you do instead?

## Capstone project

### Purpose

This is the course-completion project. Over two to three weeks, you'll take one real, high-stakes interaction from your own life through the full strategic-judgment cycle. You'll see the game, choose the lenses that matter, design an ethical move, act, and learn from what happens. The Capstone does not test whether you get the outcome you wanted. People surprise us, and no framework gives you control over them. It tests whether you saw the game clearly, acted with integrity, and learned faster from what happened.

### Choosing your interaction

Choose something real, current, and consequential where you'll actually make a move within the project window. Good candidates include a compensation or contract negotiation, a recurring conflict with a partner, family member, or colleague, a team that keeps failing the same way, a boundary you need to set, a decision about whether to stay in or leave a situation, or a proposal you need others to adopt.

It must meet at least three of the book's criteria: high stakes, something doesn't make sense, a recurring pattern, unclear incentives, uncertain trust, multiple people affecting the outcome, or your normal approach not working.

Avoid situations involving physical danger or ongoing abuse. In those cases, safety and appropriate professional support come before any analysis.

### Deliverables

1. **Situation brief** (250–400 words). Facts, kept strictly separate from your story, with a confidence rating, plus your stakes and what "winning" would actually mean to you.
2. **Completed Strategic Judgment Worksheet.** All eleven questions scanned, with the 2–4 lenses you chose analyzed in depth and a written rationale for the lenses you set aside.
3. **Hypothesis table.** At least three competing explanations of the other players' behavior, with a plan to tell them apart.
4. **Move design** (300–500 words). Your move, the alternatives you rejected and why, your explicit prediction, and the full ethics check. Include whether a game-changing move (new options, new rules, new players, or refusing to play) is available.
5. **Practice Log.** At least three Model → Act → Observe → Update → Adapt entries covering the interaction and its follow-ups.
6. **Debrief** (600–900 words). Observe, Update, Adapt. What happened, where reality differed from your prediction and why, what you'd do differently, and what you'll do next. End with one paragraph on which lens you'll now notice faster in other situations (transfer).

### Step-by-step process

1. **Pick and frame (days 1–2).** Choose the interaction. Write the situation brief. Name what you'd consider a good outcome, including outcomes other than "winning," such as preserving the relationship, telling the truth at a cost, or walking away.
2. **Scan wide (day 3).** Answer all eleven questions briefly, without editing. This is the only time you deliberately run the whole set.
3. **Choose the lens (day 3).** Circle the two to four lenses that explain the most. For the others, write one line each on why they matter less here. Over-analysis counts against you, not for you.
4. **Go deep (days 4–5).** Fully analyze the chosen lenses. Map players (including people not in the room), payoffs, likely responses, the stabilizing pattern, information gaps, credibility, commitments, and future rounds, as relevant.
5. **Hold hypotheses (day 5).** Build the hypothesis table. Where you can, design a low-risk question or observation that would discriminate among them before your main move.
6. **Design the move (day 6).** Draft your move and two alternatives. Ask "Will this work?" and "Should I do it?" separately. Consider vulnerability, consider walking away, and consider changing the game. Write a specific, checkable prediction.
7. **Act (days 7–14).** Make the move. Log what happened as soon as you can, with facts first and interpretation second.
8. **Observe, update, adapt (days 7–21).** Compare outcome to prediction. Use the surprise prompts. Revise your model, make at least one follow-up move if appropriate, and log it.
9. **Write the debrief (final days).** Be honest about what you got wrong. A clean prediction that came true teaches less than a surprise you learned from.

### Scoring rubric

| Criterion | Developing | Proficient | Strong |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Seeing the game** | Describes the conversation but not the structure. Players, options, or outside options are missing. Facts and story are blended. | Identifies the main players, options, and payoffs. Separates facts from story. | Maps the full structure, including players not in the room, outside options, coalitions, and timing. Separates facts from story with explicit confidence levels. |
| **2. Lens selection** | Applies every model mechanically, or relies on just one without justification. | Chooses 2–4 relevant lenses and explains the choice. | Chooses lenses with a sharp rationale, explains why the others matter less, and shows the restraint of "more analysis isn't always better." |
| **3. Hypotheses and prediction** | One explanation, held with certainty. Prediction vague or missing. | Several hypotheses and a specific prediction. | Several well-differentiated hypotheses, a deliberate way to tell them apart, and a specific, checkable prediction made before acting. |
| **4. Move design** | Move is a script or tactic disconnected from the analysis. | Move follows from the analysis and considers likely responses. | Move follows from the analysis, weighs alternatives (including vulnerability, walking away, or changing the game), and accounts for future rounds and precedent. |
| **5. Ethics: truth, autonomy, dignity, informed choice** | Ethics not considered, or the move relies on deception, pressure, or withheld information. | Move passes all four checks, and "Will this work?" and "Should I do it?" are addressed separately. | Move passes all four checks with specific reasoning, considers what any cooperation is directed toward, and treats the other person as a decision-maker whose agency is respected even when it costs a payoff. |
| **6. Observe / Update / Adapt** | Reports the outcome only, or explains away surprises. Calls others "irrational." | Compares outcome to prediction, identifies at least one model error, and states an adaptation. | Treats surprise as information, asks what would have to be true for others' behavior to make sense, revises the model specifically, follows up, and names a transferable lesson. |

A passing Capstone is Proficient or better on all six criteria. Ethics (criterion 5) must be at least Proficient regardless of other scores. A strategy that works but fails that check is incomplete.
