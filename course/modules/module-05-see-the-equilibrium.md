# Module 5 — See the Equilibrium

> **The question:** What equilibrium have we created?

| | |

## Why this module matters

Imagine two people who have been arguing for months. Neither likes it, yet neither changes. You stop calling because "they never call me." They don't call because "I always have to be the one who reaches out." Nobody sat down and decided, "Let's create a miserable relationship." Yet here you are, in a stable pattern.

You've learned to see the game, see the payoffs, and think in responses. This module adds **equilibrium.** Sometimes people aren't doing what they want; they're doing what makes sense given what everyone else is doing. And the most important thing to understand: **stable doesn't mean good.** A bad situation can persist without anyone needing to be irrational.

The mental shift is from moves to patterns. Instead of asking, "Why did they say that?" you start asking, "Why do we keep ending up here?" Instead of asking, "Who's to blame?" you ask, "What would have to change for the pattern to move?" Sometimes the problem isn't that you haven't found the right words. Sometimes you've both become very good at playing the same bad game.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this module you will be able to:

1. Define a Nash equilibrium in plain language and explain why "stable" is not the same as "good," "fair," or "optimal."
2. Walk through the Prisoner's Dilemma and show how individually rational responses can produce an outcome both players dislike.
3. Map a recurring interaction as a loop of mutual responses rather than a series of isolated moves.
4. Explain why "just change" can backfire, and identify what would have to change for a different pattern to become attractive.
5. Distinguish between using equilibrium as a mental model and using it as a prediction, including the problem of multiple equilibria.

## Lessons

### Lesson 5.1 — Stable Doesn't Mean Good (10 min)

**Teaching points**
- A Nash equilibrium is a combination of strategies in which each player's strategy is a best response to the others'. Simply: given what everyone else is doing, no one has a reason to change on their own.
- Equilibrium does not mean everyone is happy, that everyone got the best possible outcome, or even that anyone thinks the situation is fair. It means the existing pattern is stable because changing unilaterally doesn't look attractive.
- Everyday language hears "equilibrium" as balance or harmony. In game theory, an equilibrium can be terrible for everyone.
- The Prisoner's Dilemma shows it: if the other person cooperates, you'd do better defecting; if they defect, you'd better defect too. Both reason the same way, both defect, and both end up worse off than mutual cooperation.

**Example to use:** Walk the Prisoner's Dilemma in three questions. "Which outcome would both players prefer?" (Both cooperate.) "What should each player do if the other's choice is fixed?" (Defect.) "So where do they end up?" (Both defect.) Land the realization: everyone doing what makes sense locally can produce an outcome everyone dislikes globally.

**On-screen takeaway:** > **A bad situation can persist without anyone needing to be irrational.**

### Lesson 5.2 — Look for the Pattern, Not Just the Move (10 min)

**Teaching points**
- Chapter 3 asked, "What will they do after I move?" This chapter asks something deeper: "What pattern are our responses creating?"
- One interaction looks like "I push, you resist." Repeated, it becomes "I push, you resist, I push harder, you resist harder." Now you're looking at a system, and systems develop stable patterns.
- Equilibrium traps are everywhere: rival companies cutting prices because each fears being undercut; coworkers working unsustainable hours because each fears being taken advantage of.
- In relationships, complaint responds to withdrawal and withdrawal responds to complaint. The loop is self-reinforcing, which doesn't make it healthy.
- Most communication advice asks, "What should I say differently?" Game theory adds: "What equilibrium have we created?"

**Example to use:** The "Can you help me with this?" loop from the chapter. You ask; they're too busy; "You're always too busy"; "Because you always ask at the last minute"; "Because you never tell me when you're available"; "Because you don't plan ahead." Skip who's right and name the pattern: you ask late, they refuse, you criticize, they defend, you feel unsupported, you ask late again. The interaction itself is producing the outcome.

**On-screen takeaway:** > **Don't ask "Why did they say that?" Ask "Why do we keep ending up here?"**

### Lesson 5.3 — Unwritten, Self-Reinforcing, and Sometimes Invisible (8 min)

**Teaching points**
- An equilibrium doesn't require an agreement. Many of the strongest social patterns are unwritten: who speaks first, who pays, who apologizes, who gives in, who gets the last word.
- Once a pattern develops, expectations adjust to it. People respond not only to incentives but to expectations about what others will do.
- Some equilibria are invisible: everyone hates the two-hour meeting, but nobody wants to be the one who says, "I'm leaving after 30 minutes."
- Repetition turns a pattern into a habit. After enough rounds, "We need to talk" triggers "Here comes another criticism" before anything is said. Nobody chooses the pattern anymore; they've learned it. The game starts playing you.

**Example to use:** The new employee in a company where everyone stays late. At first: "Why is everyone still here?" After a few weeks: "I guess this is normal." After a few months: "If I leave at five, people will think I'm not committed." Now their own behavior reinforces the very norm they first questioned. The equilibrium has become cultural.

**On-screen takeaway:** > **The game starts playing you.**

### Lesson 5.4 — Why "Just Change" Can Be Bad Advice (9 min)

**Teaching points**
- Equilibrium is about unilateral change. If I change while you don't, I'm worse off, and vice versa, so neither of us moves alone, whether the pattern is beneficial, harmful, unfair, or merely acceptable.
- "Just stop participating in the argument" sounds reasonable. But if the partner reads silence as abandonment, they may escalate. A unilateral change can initially make things worse.
- A company that raises prices alone during a price war may simply lose customers. Good intentions alone rarely break an equilibrium; sometimes the players need to change their expectations about what the others will do.
- So you have to change something that affects the strategic structure: your response, the sequence, the incentives, the information, a commitment, a third party, expectations, or the available choices.
- Equilibrium is the bridge between the individual models: it explains why the pieces you've learned lock together into persistent patterns.

**Example to use:** The company where everyone waits for senior management before deciding. Management asks, "Why doesn't anyone take initiative?" Employees think, "Because whenever we do, management overrides us." Each side's behavior reinforces the other. "Take more initiative" doesn't touch what holds the pattern in place.

**On-screen takeaway:** > **Ask "What would have to change for the pattern to move?" instead of "Who's to blame?"**

### Lesson 5.5 — Many Equilibria, and Why It's a Lens, Not a Crystal Ball (8 min)

**Teaching points**
- Some games have several equilibria. If two people each do best by matching the other, both "A/A" and "B/B" can be stable. The problem is getting to the same one: a coordination problem.
- Knowing the equilibria doesn't tell you which one people will land on (the equilibrium-selection problem). Salient cues help, but research after Schelling found even small payoff asymmetries can disrupt coordination around an otherwise obvious choice.
- Equilibrium isn't prediction. People make mistakes, have limited information, use rules of thumb, and learn over time. They can sometimes learn their way into an equilibrium (model, act, observe, update), but convergence isn't universal.
- A credibility rule: say "Nash equilibrium" for formal games you've actually specified; for everyday patterns, say "equilibrium lens." Not everything that persists is an equilibrium. Habit, coercion, inertia, or rules can also keep things going. Test it: would changing alone actually make the player worse off?

**Example to use:** Schelling's meeting-in-New-York thought experiment. You've agreed to meet someone tomorrow but set no place or time. People frequently converged on noon at Grand Central Station, though nothing made it mathematically unique. It worked as a focal point: people can coordinate through context and shared expectations without talking.

**On-screen takeaway:** > **Equilibrium is a mental model, not a crystal ball.**

## Key terms

| Term | Plain-language meaning |
|---|---|
| Nash equilibrium | A set of strategies where each player's choice is a best response to the others', so no one has a reason to change on their own. |
| Unilateral change | One player changing while everyone else keeps doing what they were doing. Equilibrium is defined by the lack of reward for this. |
| Prisoner's Dilemma | The classic game where each player's best response is to defect, so both defect, even though both would prefer mutual cooperation. |
| Equilibrium trap | A stable pattern that the people inside it dislike but that no one sees a good way to leave alone. |
| Self-reinforcing pattern | A loop in which each person's response provokes the response that sustains it, such as complaining and withdrawing. |
| Coordination problem | The challenge of getting everyone to the same equilibrium when several are possible. |
| Focal point | Something that stands out and helps people coordinate their expectations without explicit communication, such as noon at Grand Central. |
| Equilibrium-selection problem | Knowing the possible stable outcomes without knowing which one people will land on. |
| Equilibrium lens | Using equilibrium thinking to ask why an everyday pattern persists, without claiming it is a formally proven Nash equilibrium. |

## Research spotlight

**1. Cooperation in one-shot Prisoner's Dilemmas (Charness, Rigotti, and Rustichini).**

- *What was done:* The researchers ran clean, one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma experiments with 348 participants and varied how much players earned from mutual cooperation.
- *What was found:* Cooperation ranged from 23% to 60% depending on the mutual-cooperation payoff. Participants' beliefs about whether others would cooperate also shifted as the payoff structure changed.
- *What it suggests for real conversations:* Structure matters: changing the payoffs changed behavior, so you shift a pattern by changing what makes it stable. But the simple money-only prediction ("everyone defects") didn't hold. Beliefs, norms, social preferences, and errors matter too.
- *What it does NOT prove:* It doesn't show that people always cooperate, that any particular relationship is a Prisoner's Dilemma, or that Nash equilibrium is either a reliable predictor or useless. It is a useful structure to think with and an incomplete description of real people.

**2. Cooperation in repeated Prisoner's Dilemmas (Dal Bó and Fréchette, American Economic Review; and their later survey in the Journal of Economic Literature).**

- *What was done:* Participants played indefinitely repeated Prisoner's Dilemma games with no known end. Theory says mutual cooperation can become sustainable in repeated play if players value the future enough.
- *What was found:* Cooperation can emerge, but under conditions more demanding than the bare theoretical requirement. The survey concludes that being supportable in equilibrium is not enough; cooperation must also be robust to strategic uncertainty (the risk the other person won't go along).
- *What it suggests for real conversations:* Knowing a better equilibrium exists doesn't mean people will find it. Two partners who both want openness may stay guarded because whoever opens up first absorbs the risk. The practical question: how do we move to a better pattern without one person carrying all the risk?
- *What it does NOT prove:* These are lab games with defined payoffs, not marriages or teams. They don't tell you when your own relationship will shift, and they don't say cooperation is always right. Some competitive patterns exist for good reasons.

A note on honesty: that equilibria are mutual best responses, and can be bad for everyone, is foundational. Applying the idea to everyday relationships is the book's own synthesis, a lens rather than a proof. A pattern can "resemble an equilibrium trap"; your marriage isn't "literally in a Nash equilibrium" unless you've modeled it.

## Practice exercises

### Exercise A — Analyze the scenario

Priya and Marcus co-lead a product team. Every Friday, Priya sends Marcus a long status email flagging risks she sees in his part of the project. Marcus has started replying with a short "Noted, thanks" and handling the issues on his own without updating her. Because she hears nothing, Priya assumes the risks aren't being handled, so the next Friday's email is longer and copies their director. Marcus reads the cc as Priya trying to make him look bad, so he shares even less and starts sending his own updates straight to the director. Both say they want a real partnership, and both say the other started it. Last month Marcus tried skipping his separate update to the director "to be the bigger person," and the director asked him in a meeting why he'd gone quiet, which embarrassed him.

1. Describe the pattern as a loop of mutual responses.
2. Why does each person's current behavior make sense given the other's?
3. Why did Marcus's unilateral change backfire, and what does that show about this pattern?
4. Is this a formal Nash equilibrium? How should you describe it honestly?
5. What would have to change for a different pattern to become attractive to both?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

1. **The loop:** Priya flags risks, Marcus goes quiet, Priya escalates (longer emails, cc to the director), Marcus feels attacked and shares less, Priya sees even less and escalates further. It's the chapter's "I push, you resist, I push harder" in a work setting.

2. **Why each response makes sense:** Given Marcus's silence, escalating is Priya's way to make sure risks get handled and she isn't blamed. Given Priya's escalation, guarding information and protecting his reputation is Marcus's. Neither is irrational. Both want a partnership; the pattern persists anyway. Stable doesn't mean good.

3. **Why "just change" backfired:** Marcus changed while everyone else kept their expectations, so his silence read as disengagement and he paid for moving alone. That's the heart of equilibrium: if I change while you don't, I'm worse off.

4. **Honest description:** With no formal strategies and payoffs, it isn't a proven Nash equilibrium. It "resembles an equilibrium trap," and the equilibrium check supports that: changing alone did leave Marcus worse off.

5. **What would have to change:** *Sequence and information:* replace the one-way email with a 15-minute joint risk review, so Marcus's handling is visible and Priya needn't escalate. *Commitment:* neither copies the director on the other's area without talking first. *Third party:* they brief the director together, so a quieter channel isn't read as disengagement. Each now expects the other's cooperation, so neither carries the whole risk of going first. "Marcus should communicate more" doesn't do this; the change has to touch the structure.

</details>

### Exercise B — Your own game

Choose a recurring interaction that frustrates you (the same argument, a standoff with a coworker, a stalled negotiation), ideally one where you're tempted to say, "It's their fault."

1. Fill in the Equilibrium Audit. Write the pattern as a loop at least five steps long.
2. For question 4, name the exact cost you fear if you change alone, then the cost you think they fear. Mark your guess about theirs "untested."
3. Run the equilibrium check: is it really the cost of changing alone holding this in place?
4. Name one alternative stable pattern. Pick one lever and design a small, honest experiment, ideally one that lowers the other person's risk of changing too.
5. Complete: "Regardless of who started it, what keeps it going now is ______." Keep this page for the field assignment.

### Exercise C — Partner drill / role-play

**Time:** 15 minutes. **Players:** two people plus an optional observer (or one person with an AI practice partner).

**Setup:** Two roommates. The kitchen is always messy. Each player can "Clean" or "Wait."

**Player A privately knows/wants:** You want a clean kitchen, but you cleaned three times last month and your roommate never did. "If I clean and they don't, I'm the doormat."

**Player B privately knows/wants:** You want a clean kitchen too, but whenever you've cleaned, your roommate redid it "the right way," which felt like criticism. "If I clean, I'll get corrected. Better to wait."

**Round 1 (4 min):** Play the usual conversation in character, each making the move that seems sensible from their private view. The observer writes down the loop.

**Round 2 (4 min):** Player A changes unilaterally (cleans without comment) while Player B keeps the same beliefs. Notice what happens.

**Round 3 (5 min):** Step out of character for 60 seconds and share "what I fear if I change alone." Then replay, looking for a structural change: a schedule (sequence), a shared standard (information), a simultaneous commitment, or a different split of chores (available choices).

**Debrief questions:**
1. In Round 1, did either player behave irrationally given what they believed? What kept the pattern stable?
2. What happened to the unilateral change in Round 2? Did it shift the other player's expectations, or get misread?
3. Which lever in Round 3 made a different pattern attractive to both? Why did it work better than good intentions?

## Common mistakes

- **Treating "stable" as "good" or "chosen."** A repeating pattern may simply mean nobody sees a better unilateral move. Ask what keeps it stable, not who secretly prefers it.
- **Hunting for the villain.** Ask, "What interaction is producing this pattern?" This doesn't erase responsibility; it adds the question of what keeps the pattern going now.
- **Fixing the next sentence instead of the pattern.** If the loop is self-reinforcing, even a better line gets pulled back into it. Look at the system, not just the move.
- **"Just change."** A one-sided change can make things worse at first if the other person reads it through the old pattern. Change something structural, especially expectations about what the other will do.
- **Calling everything a Nash equilibrium.** Persistence can come from habit, coercion, inertia, or rules. Test whether changing alone would actually hurt the person, and call it an equilibrium lens.
- **Treating equilibrium as prediction.** It doesn't tell you which stable outcome people will reach or exactly what they'll do. Model, act, observe, update.

## Field assignment

**This week: catch one equilibrium in the wild and run one experiment.**

1. **Observe (days 1 to 3):** Take your Exercise B situation or a new one. Each time it happens, jot down the loop: their move, your response, their response. Don't fix anything yet. Note when you reacted to what you *expected* them to do rather than what they did.
2. **Audit (day 4):** Update the Equilibrium Audit from your notes, including the equilibrium check.
3. **Experiment (days 5 to 7):** Pick one lever and try one small, honest change, preferably one that doesn't leave the other person carrying all the risk of going first. For example, name the pattern without blame: "I think we're in a loop where I ask late and you say no, and we both end up frustrated. Could we try a weekly check-in?" Don't use this to corner or pressure anyone. The aim is a better pattern both of you would choose freely.
4. **Record:** What happened, and what did you learn about what keeps the pattern stable?

**Debrief questions for next week:**
1. When you mapped the loop, what did you see that you hadn't seen when you looked only at individual incidents?
2. What made changing alone risky, for you and, as best you could tell, for them? Were your guesses about their fears confirmed or wrong?
3. Did your experiment change the other person's expectations? If not, what would you change about the structure next time?

## The question to carry

> **What equilibrium have we created? Why is it stable? What does each of us get from maintaining it? What makes changing it risky? Are there other equilibria available? And what would have to change for us to move toward one of them?**
