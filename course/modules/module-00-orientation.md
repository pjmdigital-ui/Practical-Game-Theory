## Why this module matters

Most communication training starts with words: what to say, how to phrase it, which questions to ask, how to handle the objection. Those things matter. But by the time you're choosing words, you've already made a series of quieter decisions about what is happening, what the other person wants, what they know, and how they're likely to respond. If those judgments are wrong, excellent communication simply helps you execute the wrong strategy more effectively.

This course teaches the layer underneath the words. We call it **Internomics**: the study of what happens between people. Internomics applies the principles of game theory to communication and human interaction—not to teach you what to say, but to teach you how to understand the game you're already playing. Game theory is the formal study of strategic interaction—situations where the outcome of one person's choice depends partly on the choices of others. You won't do any math. Instead, you'll learn eleven mental models that change the questions you ask before you speak.

The goal is not to make you a better manipulator. The goal is to help you see the game behind the conversation, so you have far more choices about how you play.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this module you will be able to:

1. Explain, in one sentence, what "game" means in this course and why it does not mean manipulation or competition.
2. Name the eleven mental models and the question each one asks.
3. Describe the Model → Act → Observe → Update → Adapt loop that runs through the whole course.
4. State the course's ethical foundation—truth, autonomy, dignity, informed choice—and apply it as a test for any strategy.
5. Begin an Interaction Journal you will use in every module.

## Lessons

### Lesson 0.1 — Why most communication advice starts too late (8 min)

**Teaching points**
- Almost all communication training focuses on strategies and tactics: words, questions, objection handling, rapport.
- Underneath every tactic are judgments about the situation: who is involved, what each person wants, what choices they have, what they know and believe, and what they expect you to do.
- The same approach can work with one person and fail completely with another. Tactics don't explain why. The structure of the interaction does.
- The shift: from "What should I say?" to "What game am I actually in?"

**Example to use:** The author's own path—an only child learning to read a room through humor, a young man navigating the written and unwritten rules of federal prison, a salesperson and copywriter whose livelihood depended on understanding how people respond. In each setting, what mattered most wasn't the words, but understanding the situation surrounding the words.

**On-screen takeaway:** > **The words are only the visible layer.**

### Lesson 0.2 — What "game" means (and doesn't) (7 min)

**Teaching points**
- A game, in the game-theoretic sense, is any situation in which the outcome depends at least partly on the decisions of more than one person.
- A game can be cooperative, competitive, mixed, or a coordination problem. Families, friendships, teams, and partnerships are games in this sense.
- "Player" is a technical word, not a cynical one. People are always more than players.
- Game theory is a lens for thinking, not a guarantee that humans behave like perfectly rational machines. Throughout the course, we'll note where real behavior departs from the clean models.

**Example to use:** Asking your boss for a raise. Before you speak: What does your boss want? What happens if they say no? What alternatives does each of you have? Who else influences the decision? Suddenly, it isn't simply a conversation.

**On-screen takeaway:** > **Other people are decision-makers, and their decisions affect your outcomes.**

### Lesson 0.3 — The eleven mental models (10 min)

**Teaching points**
- Part I — See the structure beneath the conversation: situation → motivation → response → anticipation → pattern.
- Part II — See what isn't obvious: information, credibility, commitment.
- Part III — Think beyond the moment: the future, coordination, and the design of the game itself.
- You will not carry eleven checklists into a conversation. The aim is strategic judgment: noticing the right question at the right time.

| # | Mental model | The question it asks |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | See the Game | What game am I actually in? |
| 2 | See the Payoffs | What does each player actually value? |
| 3 | Think in Responses | If I make this move, then what? |
| 4 | Manage Predictability | What am I teaching other people to predict about me? |
| 5 | See the Equilibrium | What pattern are our responses creating together? |
| 6 | Map Information & Beliefs | What do I actually know, and what am I assuming? |
| 7 | Separate Words From Credibility | What makes this message believable? |
| 8 | Commitment Changes the Game | What actually binds a promise, threat, or boundary? |
| 9 | The Shadow of the Future | What changes because there will be a next time? |
| 10 | Diagnose the Coordination Problem | Are we in conflict, or failing to coordinate? |
| 11 | Change the Game | Do I need a better move, or a better game? |

**On-screen takeaway:** > **Don't just learn what to say. Learn to see the game.**

### Lesson 0.4 — Ethics and the learning loop (10 min)

**Teaching points**
- Strategic intelligence without humanity becomes manipulation. Our standard: **truth, autonomy, dignity, informed choice.**
- Understanding why someone behaves a certain way is not the same as approving of it.
- Every model you build of another person is a hypothesis. The loop: **Model → Act → Observe → Update → Adapt.**
- Surprise is information. When someone doesn't respond the way you predicted, your model of the game was incomplete.

**Example to use:** You assume a colleague's resistance is about status, so you offer public credit. They're still resistant. Rather than labeling them "irrational," you update: maybe the real cost to them is workload or risk. The surprise improved your model.

**On-screen takeaway:** > **Your model of the game is provisional.**

## Key terms

| Term | Plain-language meaning |
|---|---|
| Internomics | The study of what happens between people: game theory applied to communication and human interaction. |
| Game | Any situation in which outcomes depend on the choices of more than one person. |
| Player | Anyone whose choices affect the outcome, including people who aren't in the room. |
| Strategic interaction | An interaction in which each person's best choice depends on what others do. |
| Mental model | A lens that tells you what to look at; not a law that predicts exactly what people will do. |
| Strategic judgment | Knowing which model matters, when it matters, how much weight to give it, and when to change your mind. |

## Research spotlight

**What game theory is, and is not.** Game theory gives us a formal language for strategic interaction: players have choices and preferences, and the outcome depends on how those choices interact. Thomas Schelling, who received the 2005 Nobel Prize in economics, emphasized that most real-world interactions contain both conflicting *and* common interests rather than being purely competitive.

**What this does NOT prove:** Game theory was never created as a theory of better communication, and human beings are messier than the perfectly rational players in many mathematical models. In this course, research illustrates and disciplines our thinking; it does not promise that any technique will work on any particular person.

## Practice exercises

### Exercise A — Analyze the scenario

Dana manages a small marketing team. For three weeks, she has asked a senior designer, Marcus, to submit his drafts two days before client deadlines so she can review them. She has explained why, rephrased the request, and finally sent a firm email. Marcus keeps submitting the night before. Dana's colleague suggests she "work on her delivery." Dana suspects the problem isn't her words at all. Marcus is paid a bonus based on client satisfaction scores, not internal deadlines. The client contact loves Marcus and often requests last-minute changes directly from him.

1. Who are the players, including any who aren't in Dana's conversations?
2. Why might better wording fail here?
3. What question should Dana be asking instead of "How do I say this more clearly?"

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

1. Dana, Marcus, and the client contact, who is a hidden but powerful player. The bonus system (and whoever designed it) also shapes the game.
2. Marcus's incentives reward client satisfaction, and the client rewards his last-minute responsiveness. Clear words don't change what Marcus gains by submitting late; finishing early may even cost him, because the client will request changes anyway.
3. "What game is Marcus actually in, and what makes his current behavior sensible from where he stands?" That question opens options such as involving the client in the timeline, adjusting what the bonus rewards, or agreeing on a change-request process. It's a preview of Modules 1, 2 and 11.

</details>

### Exercise B — Your own game

Think of one recurring interaction that frustrates you: at work, at home, or with a customer. Write down the last thing you said in it. Then list everything you *assumed* before you said it: about what they wanted, what they knew, and how they'd respond. Circle any assumption you have never actually tested.

### Exercise C — Partner drill / role-play

**Setup (10 minutes, pairs).** Partner 1 describes a recent conversation that went badly in two minutes, focusing only on *what was said*. Partner 2 may then ask only structural questions for five minutes: Who else was affected? What did each person want? What were their alternatives? What happened next? Switch roles.

**Debrief:** What did the structural questions reveal that the words didn't? Did either of you change your interpretation of the other person?

## Common mistakes

- **Treating "game" as a cynical word** — In this course, a game includes cooperation, friendship and family life. Seeing the structure is about understanding, not winning at others' expense.
- **Expecting scripts** — This course will not give you seven phrases for every situation. It gives you better questions, which transfer to situations no script anticipates.
- **Overanalyzing everything** — Use as much analysis as the stakes and uncertainty justify. Most interactions don't need a strategic audit.
- **Mistaking your model for the truth** — Every read of another person is a hypothesis to test, not a fact.
