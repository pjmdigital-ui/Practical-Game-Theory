# **The evidence standard for every chapter**

I would make this non-negotiable during writing.

Every chapter should contain **five different evidentiary layers**, and we should label them correctly in our research notes:

**Foundational theory:** What the formal game-theory literature actually establishes.

**Experimental evidence:** What people did in controlled games or laboratory studies.

**Field evidence:** Wherever possible, what happened when the relevant mechanism was tested in real-world behavior.

**Illustrative case:** A memorable historical, business, sports, relationship, or organizational example. A case illustrates; it does not “prove” the theory.

**Boundary conditions:** Where actual human behavior diverges from the clean model.

That last one may become one of the book's strengths.

For example, backward induction is elegant theory, but Johnson, Camerer and colleagues found limited look-ahead in experimental bargaining. [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0022053101928509?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

The Centipede Game provides another warning: standard complete-information equilibrium predicts very early stopping, yet experimental subjects commonly continued much further, prompting explanations involving incomplete information and beliefs about player types. [CaltechAUTHORS](https://authors.library.caltech.edu/records/n6131-v8q36?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

That means we can tell readers:

> **Game theory is a lens for thinking, not a guarantee that humans behave like perfectly rational machines.**

That's important.

## **Chapter 1 research dossier — See the Game**

The foundational lineage is straightforward. Von Neumann and Morgenstern’s *Theory of Games and Economic Behavior* formalized the study of strategic interaction as a distinct mathematical field, and modern treatments retain the same core idea: outcomes depend on the combination of multiple players’ choices rather than on one person’s decision in isolation. [Princeton University Press](https://assets.press.princeton.edu/about_pup/PUP100/book/2cNeumann.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

For the reader, we translate that into five things to notice:

| Element | Formal idea | Everyday translation |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| **Players** | Who makes strategically relevant choices? | Who can actually affect the outcome? |

### **The first strong experiment: the same game can produce different behavior depending on how people construe it**

A particularly memorable study for this chapter is Liberman, Samuels, and Ross’s 2004 Prisoner’s Dilemma research.

Participants played essentially the same strategic game, but researchers labeled it either the **“Wall Street Game”** or the **“Community Game.”** The labels had a substantial effect on cooperation versus defection, greater than observers expected, while prior reputation judgments did a poor job distinguishing who would cooperate. The experiments included American college students and Israeli pilots/instructors. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15359020/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

We need to be precise about what this proves.

It does **not** prove:

> “If you consciously map a game, you'll make optimal decisions.”

It does show:

> **People's construal of the strategic situation can materially affect how they behave inside it.**

That gives us a powerful human example for the chapter.

Two people can face nearly identical formal incentives and behave differently partly because they believe they're participating in different kinds of interaction.

That connects perfectly to the chapter thesis:

> **How you identify the game matters.**

But we maintain our earlier distinction: there is still an underlying strategic structure. People's interpretations of it can be accurate or inaccurate.

### **A second useful experiment: context can affect strategic reasoning**

Cooper, Kagel, Lo, and Gu studied strategic interactions based on incentive problems in Chinese firms. They compared students with experienced managers, varied financial incentives, and presented games either abstractly or explicitly in their real managerial context.

They found that increasing incentives increased strategic play initially, and presenting the game in familiar managerial context increased strategic play among experienced managers much more than among students. [American Economic Association](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Faer.89.4.781&utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Again, the careful takeaway isn't:

> “Context always makes people better strategists.”

It's:

> **The way a strategic situation is represented and the experience a person brings to that structure can affect strategic behavior.**

That's relevant to your own original intuition.

You learned to recognize environments and players from experience before you ever had formal language for what you were doing.

Game theory gives us a structure for making that kind of situational awareness explicit and teachable.

### **A newer experiment strengthens the idea**

Charness and Sontuoso published a 2023 experiment on **frame-dependent rationalizability**. Participants played coordination games involving visual objects, and the researchers manipulated which attributes of the choices players were aware of. Changing awareness of the available frames changed strategic behavior. [Benny](https://benny.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Fmic.20210082&utm_source=chatgpt.com)

That gives us a modern research anchor for a subtle point:

> **People do not necessarily perceive every strategically relevant feature of the situation available to them.**

That question could become one of the important secondary questions of Chapter 1\.

Game changes again.

Then:

A senior executive is observing the meeting.

Game changes again.

Same two people.

Same basic subject.

Different strategic structure.

That could be the chapter's main teaching device.

## **Chapter 2 Research Dossier — See the Payoffs**

The central claim for this chapter should be:

> **To understand why people choose what they choose, you have to understand how they value the possible outcomes—not assume that money, logic, or your own preferences are the only things that matter.**

That is solidly grounded in game theory. In formal game theory, payoffs represent players’ preferences over outcomes; they are not inherently “money.” A utility function is a way of representing how an agent ranks outcomes. [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/game-theory/)

That gives us an important foundation:

> **A payoff is whatever makes one outcome more or less desirable to the player.**

For humans, that can include money, status, fairness, autonomy, security, time, belonging, reputation, relationships, risk, or other things the person values.

### **The first major evidence anchor: the Ultimatum Game**

The classic Ultimatum Game is almost perfect for this chapter.

One person receives a sum of money and proposes how to divide it with another person. The second player can accept, in which case both get the proposed amounts, or reject, in which case both get nothing.

Under the narrowest money-only model, the responder should accept any positive amount.

But people often don't.

A meta-analysis of **37 papers containing 75 experimental results** found that proposers offered about **40% of the available pie on average**, while responders rejected about **16% of offers overall**. Rejection was less common when offers were larger and when the total stakes were larger. [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/kap/expeco/v7y2004i2p171-188.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

That does not prove:

> “People always choose fairness over money.”

It demonstrates something more careful:

> **Observed human behavior in these games is not adequately described by assuming that only the responder’s own monetary amount matters.**

The outcome's perceived fairness, the other player's behavior, and other social considerations appear to matter.

That makes the Ultimatum Game an ideal opening experiment for **See the Payoffs**.

Their results showed that subjects were sometimes willing to sacrifice their own money to increase overall welfare, particularly for people receiving low payoffs. > **People may care not only about what they receive, but about what other people receive and how those people behaved.**

That's a much richer payoff structure.

# **Autonomy can be part of the payoff too**

This is where the Falk and Kosfeld experiment becomes extremely useful.

In an experimental principal-agent game, principals could impose a minimum performance requirement on agents.

A simple material-incentive intuition might predict that more control should improve performance.

Instead, Falk and Kosfeld found that **most agents reduced performance in response to the controlling decision**. When asked about their reaction, many described the control as a signal of distrust and a limitation of autonomy. The overall effect of control on the principal’s payoff was therefore not monotonic. [AEAweb](https://swlb2.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Faer.96.5.1611&utm_source=chatgpt.com)

This provides strong evidence for an idea we have discussed repeatedly:

> **Autonomy itself can matter to people.**

Again, we shouldn't overgeneralize the specific experimental result to every workplace or relationship.

But it establishes that:

> **Changing someone's material choices can also change nonmaterial elements of the outcome they value.**

That belongs directly in this chapter.

# **The High-Stakes Test**

A legitimate objection to Ultimatum Game evidence is:

> “People reject unfair offers because the laboratory stakes are tiny. If real money were involved, they'd act differently.”

There is some truth to the intuition—but not enough to rescue a purely money-only account.

Slonim and Roth varied financial incentives by a factor of **25** in high-stakes ultimatum experiments in Slovakia. For inexperienced players, increasing the stakes had only a small effect on behavior; rejection became less frequent as stakes increased, and experienced proposers gradually made lower offers at higher stakes. [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/ecm/emetrp/v66y1998i3p569-596.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

That gives us a nuanced conclusion:

> **The size of the material payoff matters. Social concerns don't simply disappear, but their relative importance can change as the stakes change.**

That's a better lesson than either extreme:

> “People don't care about money.”

or:

> “People only care about money.”

Neither survives the evidence very well.

A person may simultaneously value:

**Money**

**Status**

**Autonomy**

**Security**

**Fairness**

**Relationship**

**Reputation**

**Convenience**

**Identity**

And those dimensions can conflict.

But reject it because it also produces:

> less time \+ more stress \+ less autonomy.

They don't need to be confused.

# **This is also where we should correct a common misuse of incentives**

People often think:

> “If I want more of a behavior, increase the reward. If I want less, impose a cost.”

Sometimes.

But human payoff structures can be affected by what the incentive **means**.

The famous daycare study by Gneezy and Rustichini illustrates this. Several daycare centers introduced a financial penalty for parents arriving late to pick up their children. Instead of decreasing, late pickups **increased significantly**, and they did not immediately return to the previous level after the fine was removed. The authors argued that introducing a price may have changed the social meaning of the situation. [RCNi Company Limited](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/toc/jls/2000/29/1?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

But I would be very careful here.

A later replication attempt using a different survey-based design did **not** reproduce the famous persistent effect in the same way, and found deterrent effects from fines. So I would use the daycare study as a vivid example of the possibility that incentives can change perceived meaning—not as a universal law that “fines backfire.” [GSH Financial](https://gshfinancial.com/insights/bf-incentives-crowding-out-meta-analysis-war?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

That boundary condition belongs in the book.

Ethics tells us:

## **Chapter 3 Research Dossier — Think in Responses**

The central claim for this chapter should be:

> **In strategic interaction, the value of your move depends partly on how other people are likely to respond to it.**

That is not just communication advice; it sits near the center of game theory. A **best response** is the action that does best given what the other players are doing, and sequential games add timing: one player acts, another observes and responds, and earlier players may reason backward from those possible future responses. MIT teaches best responses as foundational and backward induction as the process of reasoning from later decisions back toward the present one. [MIT OpenCourseWare](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/14-12-economic-applications-of-game-theory-fall-2025/resources/mit14_12f25_lec05_1080p_mp4/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

For the reader, we translate that into one deceptively simple question:

> **Then what?**

Not merely:

> “What should I say?”

but:

> **“If I say or do this, what are they likely to do next—and what situation does that create?”**

---

# **The basic game-theory foundation**

There are really three layers we should teach here.

### **1\. Best responses**

Your best move often depends on the other player's move.

In ordinary life, that means an action cannot always be evaluated in isolation.

A manager's hard-line statement might look strong in isolation.

But if it predictably causes employees to hide bad news, its strategic value changes.

A concession may look generous.

But if it teaches the other player that resistance always produces another concession, its long-run consequences may differ.

This is the essence of strategic interdependence.

---

### **2\. Sequential reasoning**

Some games unfold in order.

You move.

I observe.

I respond.

You respond to my response.

That is different from a simultaneous game in which we choose without seeing each other's action.

MIT distinguishes sequential games for exactly this reason: the order of moves and what later players observe can change strategic behavior. [MIT OpenCourseWare](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/14-15j-networks-spring-2018/pages/lecture-and-recitation-notes/recitation-7-notes/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

This gives us one of the chapter's foundational ideas:

> **Timing is not separate from strategy.**

Who moves first, what they reveal, and what another person gets to observe can affect everything that follows.

---

### **3\. Backward induction**

If you can anticipate later choices, sometimes you can reason backward.

MIT defines backward induction as determining optimal choices by starting at the endpoint and working back toward the beginning. [MIT OpenCourseWare](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/14-12-economic-applications-of-game-theory-fall-2025/resources/mit14_12f25_lec08_1080p_mp4/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

For an everyday reader:

> **Start with where you want the interaction to end and ask what would have to happen immediately before that—then what would have to happen before that.**

That can radically improve communication planning.

---

# **The strongest experiment for this chapter: people often do NOT look far enough ahead**

Johnson, Camerer, Sen, and Rymon studied a **three-round sequential bargaining game**.

Under the subgame-perfect solution, the initial offer should have been **\$1.25**. An equal split would have been **\$2.50**.

Actual subjects offered an average of **\$2.11**.

More importantly, the researchers tracked which information subjects inspected before deciding. The patterns showed **limited look-ahead rather than full backward induction**. When subjects were explicitly trained in backward induction, they learned the procedure quickly, and their offers to computerized opponents moved very close to the theoretical prediction—about **\$1.22**. [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0022053101928509?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

This is an excellent study for us because it supports two points simultaneously.

First:

> **Humans do not automatically reason through long chains of responses as deeply as formal game theory sometimes assumes.**

Second:

> **Strategic look-ahead can be taught.**

That's directly relevant to the premise of the book.

We're not saying:

> “People naturally behave like expert game theorists.”

We're saying:

> **Game theory gives people a way of thinking they may not otherwise use spontaneously.**

That's exactly the project.

---

# **Another major research stream: how many steps ahead do people actually think?**

This is where **level-k reasoning** and **cognitive hierarchy** become extremely useful.

A simplified version works like this:

### **Level 0**

Acts without strategically modeling the opponent.

### **Level 1**

Asks:

> “What will they probably do?”

and best-responds to that.

### **Level 2**

Asks:

> “What do they think I will do?”

and responds to that.

And so forth.

Stahl and Wilson's experimental work found substantial heterogeneity in strategic depth. In one experiment, their estimates classified roughly **24% as level-1**, **49% as level-2**, and **27% as Nash-type**, with essentially none classified as level-0 under that particular model. [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/0167268194901031?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Camerer, Ho, and Chong later developed the **cognitive hierarchy model** across many games. Their empirical fit suggested an average strategic-thinking depth of roughly **1.5 steps** in the datasets they studied. [OUP Academic](https://academic.oup.com/qje/article-abstract/119/3/861/1938841?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Again, we should not turn those exact numbers into universal claims about humanity.

The broader evidence-based point is:

> **People vary in how deeply they reason about other people's reasoning, and perfect infinite strategic sophistication is a poor descriptive assumption in many experiments.**

That's enormously useful for communication.

---

# **This creates a subtle but important lesson**

Game theory does **not** teach:

> “Always think ten moves ahead.”

That's actually a bad simplification.

Because if you assume the other person is thinking five levels ahead when they're barely thinking one, you can outsmart yourself.

Imagine:

> “He knows that I know that he expects me to…”

Meanwhile, he just wants to leave the meeting and eat lunch.

So the practical mental model should be:

> **Think one or two responses ahead—but calibrate your model to the actual person and situation.**

That's much more defensible.

---

# **The Beauty Contest gives us a memorable illustration**

Rosemarie Nagel's famous guessing-game experiments asked participants to choose a number while trying to anticipate a fraction of the average choice of the group.

The game is powerful because success requires thinking about:

> what other people will choose,

and potentially:

> what they think everyone else will choose.

Her 1995 *American Economic Review* study became a classic demonstration of iterative strategic reasoning and the fact that actual subjects typically do not leap immediately to the full equilibrium prediction. [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/aea/aecrev/v85y1995i5p1313-26.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

This could be an excellent small exercise in the chapter.

Ask the reader to play before explaining the logic.

Then show them:

> **Your choice depends on your model of everyone else's reasoning.**

That is exactly what we want them to internalize.

---

# **The Centipede Game gives us the necessary warning**

There is another classic sequential game where perfect backward induction produces an extreme prediction.

Two players alternately choose whether to stop the game and take a larger current payoff or allow the pot to grow.

Under the standard complete-information backward-induction logic used in the experiment, play should stop immediately.

McKelvey and Palfrey's experiments found that subjects generally **did not** stop immediately. Play frequently continued for multiple rounds. They showed that behavior could be better understood if players entertained uncertainty about the other's preferences—such as a small possibility that the opponent might be altruistic. [CaltechAUTHORS](https://authors.library.caltech.edu/records/n6131-v8q36?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

This is a fantastic boundary condition for our chapter.

It teaches:

> **Anticipating responses requires a model of the other player—and that model can be wrong.**

Backward reasoning is only as good as the assumptions feeding it.

So our book should not say:

> “Work backward and you can predict exactly what someone will do.”

It should say:

> **Think through plausible responses, then remain humble about your assumptions.**

That is much closer to actual behavioral game theory.

# **The key everyday distinction: intention vs. response**

This could become one of the strongest portions of the chapter.

People often defend communication by describing their intention:

> “I was only trying to motivate him.”

> “I was just being honest.”

> “I was trying to show that I was serious.”

> “I wanted her to understand how upset I was.”

Those statements may all be true.

But the strategic question is different:

> **What response was reasonably foreseeable?**

Suppose a manager publicly humiliates an employee intending to:

> increase effort.

Possible responses include:

* greater effort,  
* defensiveness,  
* withdrawal,  
* hiding mistakes,  
* resentment,  
* seeking another job.

Intent matters morally.

But strategy also cares about:

> **the behavior your move changes.**

That is a distinct way of evaluating communication.

# **The “winning the turn” problem**

This deserves a major section.

People frequently optimize for the immediate conversational victory.

They want to:

* deliver the comeback,  
* prove the contradiction,  
* force an admission,  
* make the other person back down.

The immediate response may feel satisfying.

But game theory pushes us to ask:

> **Then what?**

Suppose you embarrass someone in front of their peers and they stop arguing.

You may think:

> “I won.”

But perhaps what you've actually produced is:

> silence now \+ resistance later.

The move cannot be evaluated solely from the next ten seconds.

This chapter should teach people to consider the **state of the game after the response**.

# **A useful three-stage lens**

I don't want this to become a formula, but this is simple enough to carry mentally:

### **Your move**

What are you considering saying or doing?

### **Their plausible responses**

Not one response—several.

### **The next game state**

What situation exists if they choose each response?

That last part is crucial.

If I threaten to quit and you call my bluff, the next game is very different from the one I imagined when I issued the threat.

# **Example: the predictable concession**

Suppose you're negotiating.

You make an offer.

They reject it.

You immediately improve the offer.

They reject again.

You improve it again.

From your perspective:

> “I'm being reasonable.”

From their perspective, a strategy may be emerging:

> **Rejecting causes concessions.**

Your move isn't only transferring value.

It may change the other player's future behavior.

This example later connects beautifully to **Manage Predictability** and **Shadow of the Future**.

But here the lesson is simply:

> **Your response becomes part of their next decision problem.**

# **Example: escalating a conflict**

Person A becomes aggressive.

Person B responds aggressively.

A interprets B's response as proof that more firmness is needed.

B responds to that escalation.

Now neither person needs to be consciously manipulating the other.

Their response functions can create an escalation process.

MIT's treatment of strategic complements offers a formal analogue: in some games, more aggressive behavior by one player can make greater activity by others their best response; in strategic substitutes, the opposite occurs. [MIT OpenCourseWare](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/14-15j-networks-spring-2018/pages/lecture-and-recitation-notes/recitation-11-notes/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

We should not claim every argument is literally a strategic-complements game.

But the lens is useful:

> **Does my move amplify the other person's behavior or dampen it?**

That's a sophisticated question.

---

# **Example: leadership and bad news**

Manager says:

> “Tell me immediately when something goes wrong.”

Employee reports a serious mistake.

Manager explodes.

What has the manager just done?

The immediate response addressed the mistake.

But it also changed the expected payoff of future disclosure.

Next time the employee faces bad news, their decision is made in a different strategic environment.

That is the kind of story that makes the model real:

> **Today's response becomes tomorrow's incentive.**

This bridges directly into repeated games later.

# **One of the deepest ideas: reason from the desired ending backward**

Suppose your objective is:

> “I want my business partner to tell me when they disagree.”

Don't begin with:

> “What phrase will make them open up?”

Start at the desired endpoint.

For them to speak honestly, what must they expect?

Perhaps:

> that disagreement will be heard rather than punished.

How do they form that expectation?

From:

> your previous and current responses to disagreement.

Now your problem changes.

The path becomes:

**desired behavior**  
← **required expectation**  
← **your observable response pattern**

That's backward reasoning translated into human interaction.

# **But don't confuse influence with control**

This distinction needs to be explicit because of the ethical foundation.

Anticipating responses does **not** mean:

> “Figure out how to make people do exactly what you want.”

Other people remain autonomous decision makers.

Your action can alter:

* incentives,  
* beliefs,  
* emotions,  
* expectations,  
* options.

But they still choose their response.

So the model teaches:

> **Influence the environment intelligently; don't pretend you control the player.**

That's both strategically realistic and ethically important.

# **Chapter 4 — Manage Predictability**

## **Chapter 4 Research Dossier — Manage Predictability**

The central claim for this chapter should be:

> **Predictability is a strategic variable. It can create trust and coordination, but it can also make you exploitable when another player benefits from anticipating your next move.**

That is a genuine game-theory idea, not just advice to “keep people guessing.” Mixed strategies are a standard part of game theory; MIT treats them as a foundational equilibrium concept, and its current materials explicitly note the practical intuition that if an opponent learns you always choose the same action, they can update and exploit that pattern. [MIT OpenCourseWare](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/17-810-game-theory-spring-2021/pages/readings/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

The subtlety is that the lesson is **not** “be random.” The better mental model is:

> **Be predictable where predictability creates value; avoid unnecessary predictability where it creates exploitability.**

That distinction is what makes this chapter worth keeping.

### **The cleanest theoretical starting point: Matching Pennies**

Matching Pennies is probably the best classic game for the chapter.

Two players choose between two actions. One wants the choices to match; the other wants them not to match.

If Player A always chooses the same action, Player B can exploit it.

If Player A follows an obvious alternating pattern, Player B can exploit that too.

In equilibrium, neither player wants to make their next move easy to predict.

That gives us the key insight:

> **Variation alone is not enough. A predictable pattern of variation is still predictable.**

MIT’s treatment of mixed strategies explicitly uses this kind of logic: if you acquire a reputation for always choosing one move, opponents can adjust against you. It also makes an important nuance we should preserve: what appears random to the opponent need not always involve literal coin-flipping; behavior can be conditioned on private information the opponent cannot observe. [MIT OpenCourseWare](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/14-12-economic-applications-of-game-theory-fall-2025/1_BO-FLYSFUjDHCCUhzPgQwFoJIAZkxbh_transcript.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

That is useful because the everyday lesson is not:

> “Inject randomness into your personality.”

It is:

> **Do not make your strategically relevant behavior mechanically exploitable.**

# **Strong real-world evidence: professional soccer**

The classic empirical case is penalty kicks.

Chiappori, Levitt, and Groseclose analyzed **459 penalty kicks** from the top French and Italian soccer leagues. Penalty kicks provide an unusually clean strategic environment because the kicker and goalkeeper have opposing objectives, professional players know one another's tendencies, and substantial stakes are involved. The researchers found that the testable implications of mixed-strategy theory fit the data surprisingly well. [Price Theory](https://pricetheory.uchicago.edu/levitt/Papers/ChiapporiGrosecloseLevitt2002.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

This gives us a memorable illustration:

If a kicker becomes too predictable about direction, the goalkeeper can adjust.

The exact value of:

> kick left

depends partly on how likely the goalkeeper thinks:

> kick left

is coming.

So the player's strategy needs to account for the opponent's ability to learn their pattern.

A later analysis using a different statistical methodology found results broadly consistent with the original penalty-kick findings. [Sage Journals](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/1527002506289648?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

We should be precise, though: professional soccer is unusually close to a clean zero-sum strategic environment. We should not claim that every conversation works like a penalty kick.

The experiment demonstrates the strategic value of avoiding exploitable predictability in an adversarial setting.

---

# **Wimbledon provides another field case**

Walker and Wooders studied serving behavior at Wimbledon as a real-world test of minimax and mixed-strategy play. Their work became another classic empirical test of whether expert competitors vary strategic actions in ways broadly consistent with game-theoretic predictions. [AEAweb](https://swlb2.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Faer.91.5.1521&utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Again, the point is not:

> “Business conversations are tennis matches.”

The point is:

> **In situations where another player's gain depends on correctly anticipating you, predictability itself can change the payoff of your action.**

That insight transfers.

---

# **A modern large-scale case: Major League Baseball**

Bhattacharya and Howard analyzed **millions of Major League Baseball pitches** using a matching-pennies-style model with state-dependent payoffs.

They found that professional players did not behave exactly like fully informed Nash optimizers at every moment. Instead, their responsiveness to payoff differences increased when the costs of attention were lower, and behavior moved closer to the full-information mixed-strategy benchmark as the stakes increased. [American Economic Association](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Fmic.20200310&utm_source=chatgpt.com)

This is particularly valuable for us because it adds realism.

The book should not say:

> “Experts perfectly randomize.”

The evidence suggests something more interesting:

> **People may approximate strategically unpredictable behavior more closely when the stakes justify the cognitive effort.**

That fits the broader theme of the book beautifully.

Game theory gives us the ideal strategic problem.

Behavioral evidence tells us how imperfect humans actually handle it.

---

# **Laboratory evidence: people react to exploitable history**

Mookherjee and Sopher studied repeated experimental Matching Pennies games.

They varied whether players could observe the opponent's past moves and payoffs. When players had fuller information about their opponents, their choices became considerably closer to independent, identically distributed play—the kind of less-patterned behavior one would expect when players are trying not to become easily exploitable. [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0899825684710372?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

The sample was small—two groups containing ten pairs each—so we should not present this as sweeping evidence about all human interaction.

But it supports the underlying mechanism:

> **When people can learn an opponent's patterns, pattern management becomes strategically relevant.**

---

# **Now the chapter gets more interesting: human interaction isn't always adversarial**

If we stopped there, the reader could walk away thinking:

> “Being unpredictable is strategically smart.”

That would be wrong.

Consider a trust relationship.

You often want another person to predict:

> “She keeps her promises.”

> “He behaves cooperatively when I cooperate.”

> “If I respect this boundary, the relationship remains stable.”

That kind of predictability can create value.

In a trust-game experiment, Bracht and Feltovich allowed players to observe one previous action of the person they were interacting with. Merely making previous behavior observable produced substantially higher cooperation and efficiency than the basic trust-game condition, while cheap-talk messages alone had little effect. The authors also found that previous cooperative behavior predicted current cooperative behavior and influenced whether others were willing to trust. [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S004727270900067X?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

That's crucial.

The same general property—**behavior that can be anticipated**—can be either:

> an asset

or

> a liability

depending on the game.

---

# **That gives us the central paradox of the chapter**

### **In cooperative settings**

Predictability can communicate:

> reliability.

### **In adversarial settings**

Predictability can communicate:

> exploitable pattern.

### **In mixed-motive settings**

You may need both.

This gives us one of the strongest phrases we've developed:

> **Predictable principles. Adaptable strategy.**

That could become the chapter's organizing idea.

You want people to know:

> you tell the truth.

> you keep commitments.

> you enforce genuine boundaries.

> you don't exploit good-faith cooperation.

But that does not mean they need to know:

> your exact reservation point,

> your next competitive move,

> the precise sequence of concessions you'll make,

> or the exact response they can trigger from you every time.

That is a sophisticated strategic distinction.

---

# **Negotiation gives us an excellent application**

This is one area where we can use negotiation research **as evidence of an application**, without turning negotiation into the foundation.

Suppose a negotiator follows a predictable rule:

> rejection → concession.

Over time, another player can learn:

> **Rejecting is rewarded.**

James Wall ran a series of four experiments in which negotiators' concessions were systematically rewarded. Negotiators exposed to that reinforcement strategy made larger concessions and reached agreement faster; importantly, the larger-concession behavior persisted after the rewards stopped. [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/0022103177900282?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Later research similarly found that negotiators can learn concession behavior through reinforcement rather than simply through an automatic norm of reciprocity. [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/0030507381900295?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

The lesson is not:

> “Never concede.”

It is:

> **Repeatedly rewarding the same move can teach the other player to make that move again.**

That fits our mental model perfectly.

---

# **But predictable concession patterns can also communicate useful information**

This makes the chapter richer still.

A 2021 series of negotiation studies examined **decreasing concessions**—for example, progressively smaller movements toward the other side.

The researchers found that decreasing concession patterns signaled that the negotiator was approaching a limit. Counterparts then made less ambitious counteroffers and tended to reach less favorable agreements for themselves. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016%2Fj.obhdp.2021.05.003?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

So predictability is not merely something an opponent exploits.

A pattern can also **signal information**.

That connects this chapter forward to:

> **Information & Beliefs**

and

> **Credibility.**

A predictable pattern may deliberately or inadvertently reveal:

* your limits,  
* your incentives,  
* your likely next move,  
* your level of desperation,  
* your willingness to walk away.

That is strategically important.

---

# **An everyday example: the parent and the tantrum**

This is one of the clearest nonbusiness examples.

Imagine the pattern:

Child asks.

Parent says no.

Child asks again.

Parent says no.

Child escalates.

Parent eventually gives in.

After enough repetitions, the child doesn't need a theory of reinforcement.

They simply learn:

> **Escalation predicts capitulation.**

The parent's response has become exploitable.

This doesn't mean the child is calculating probabilities consciously.

Strategic patterns can be learned behaviorally.

And notice the distinction:

The parent ideally *should* be predictable about:

> “I love you.”

> “I won't abandon you.”

> “These rules matter.”

But perhaps they should not establish:

> “If you make the interaction unpleasant enough, I eventually reverse my decision.”

Again:

> **Predictable principles. Adaptable strategy.**

---

# **Boundaries reveal the opposite case**

Suppose you tell someone:

> “If you insult me, I'm ending the conversation.”

Then when insulted you:

* sometimes leave,  
* sometimes argue,  
* sometimes ignore it,  
* sometimes give the person what they want.

Now the consequence is unpredictable.

That may weaken the boundary.

In this situation, strategic predictability is useful.

The other player should be able to anticipate:

> **Crossing this boundary reliably produces this response.**

So this model is not teaching randomness.

It is teaching **intentional consistency**.

---

# **One very important distinction: random is not the same as arbitrary**

Mixed-strategy game theory is sometimes misunderstood as:

> “Do random things.”

That's not the lesson.

In formal mixed-strategy equilibrium, the probabilities themselves are strategic. They depend on the payoff structure of the game.

And as MIT's material notes, behavior that looks random to another player may actually depend on private cues or information the opponent cannot observe. [MIT OpenCourseWare](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/14-12-economic-applications-of-game-theory-fall-2025/1_BO-FLYSFUjDHCCUhzPgQwFoJIAZkxbh_transcript.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

So we should probably avoid telling the reader:

> “Randomize.”

The better advice is:

> **Do not allow another player to exploit a rigid response rule when their interests give them reason to do so.**

That's much more defensible.

---

# **The predictability test**

This chapter can teach one very useful question:

> **If they knew exactly what I was going to do, would that help them cooperate with me—or help them exploit me?**

That single question captures the whole model.

If the answer is:

> “It helps them trust me,”

predictability may be valuable.

If the answer is:

> “It tells them exactly how to beat me,”

adaptability may be valuable.

That is much better than either extreme:

> “Always be consistent.”

or:

> “Always keep people guessing.”

---

# **Reputation belongs partly here—but we should not steal Chapter 9's material**

There is overlap with **The Shadow of the Future**.

Reputation is essentially information about how you tend to behave over time.

An AER laboratory experiment found that making a long-run player's past choices observable was seldom harmful and sometimes beneficial, though the benefits were weaker than some theoretical predictions. [AEAweb](https://swlb2.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Faer.100.5.2187&utm_source=chatgpt.com)

We can use that here only to establish:

> **Predictability can have positive strategic value.**

Then save the deeper mechanics of reputation, cooperation, retaliation, and repeated interaction for Chapter 9\.

That keeps the chapters distinct.

---

# **A surprising implication for communication advice**

This chapter gives us a strong critique of rigid scripts.

Suppose a communication book teaches:

> “Whenever someone says X, always respond Y.”

If the other person learns that rule, then:

> **X becomes a way of reliably producing Y.**

Whether that's a problem depends on the game.

Sometimes consistent responses are exactly what we want.

But sometimes a fixed script creates exploitability.

That's another reason your book is different from typical communication training.

We're not teaching:

> **Input X → output Y.**

We're teaching:

> **Understand how the other player may adapt to your strategy.**

That is much more game-theoretic.

---

# **The ethical boundary**

Strategic unpredictability can obviously be misused.

Someone can create instability deliberately to make another person anxious or easier to control.

That's not what we're teaching.

The ethical distinction should be explicit:

> **Unpredictability used to prevent legitimate exploitation is different from unpredictability used to destabilize another person's sense of security.**

We want:

> adaptability,

not:

> psychological chaos.

In close relationships especially, predictable safety and honesty are valuable.

The model applies only to strategically relevant choices where another player's ability to predict you affects the game.

---

# **What the evidence does NOT justify**

We should explicitly reject several simplistic claims.

**Not:** “Unpredictable people win.”

No.

**Not:** “Randomize your communication.”

No.

**Not:** “Never let anyone know what you'll do.”

That would destroy trust and coordination.

**Not:** “Mixed strategies prove that you should hide your intentions.”

No. Mixed-strategy theory applies to particular strategic structures.

**Not:** “Human beings perfectly randomize.”

Professional-sports and laboratory evidence show approximations, learning, and deviations—not perfect universal Nash behavior. [American Economic Association](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Fmic.20200310&utm_source=chatgpt.com)

The defensible claim is:

> **When another player can benefit from correctly anticipating your behavior, rigid predictable patterns may be strategically costly. Conversely, when cooperation depends on trust and coordination, predictability can be valuable.**

That's strong enough.

# **The classic teaching exercise**

I'd start the chapter with something extremely simple.

Ask:

> You're playing Rock-Paper-Scissors against the same person for \$100 per round. You've chosen Rock five times in a row. What happens if they realize this?

Obvious.

Then:

> So you decide to use Rock → Paper → Scissors → Rock → Paper → Scissors.

Are you safe now?

No.

You've confused:

> variety

with

> unpredictability.

Then we can escalate to penalty kicks, baseball, negotiation, parenting, boundaries, and reputation.

The reader will understand the principle before hearing the term:

> **mixed strategy.**

### **After**

## **Chapter 5 Research Dossier — See the Equilibrium**

The central claim for this chapter should be:

> **A pattern can be stable without being good.**

That is one of the deepest ideas in game theory.

A Nash equilibrium is a situation in which each player is choosing a best response to what the others are doing, so no one has an incentive to change unilaterally. MIT teaches exactly that definition: an equilibrium is a set of mutually optimal responses. [MIT OpenCourseWare](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/14-12-economic-applications-of-game-theory-fall-2025/resources/mit14_12f25_lec05_1080p_mp4/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

The everyday translation is:

> **Sometimes everyone can dislike the situation, yet each person's current behavior still makes sense given everyone else's behavior.**

That is what makes bad patterns so hard to break.

# **The classic game: Prisoner's Dilemma**

This is probably the perfect game for this chapter.

In the standard Prisoner's Dilemma, each player has an incentive to defect regardless of what the other player does. So mutual defection is the unique Nash equilibrium.

But both players would be better off under mutual cooperation.

MIT's notes make the distinction explicitly: in the Prisoner's Dilemma, the Nash equilibrium is **not Pareto optimal**. [MIT OpenCourseWare](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/1-022-introduction-to-network-models-fall-2018/c77eb253a955cf6e3b4dbb3e7b733b02_MIT1_022F18_lec22_and_lec23.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

That gives us an incredibly powerful idea:

> **Individually rational responses can produce a collectively inferior outcome.**

This is exactly what we want the reader to see.

---

# **Why “stable” and “desirable” are different**

People often unconsciously assume:

> “If a pattern keeps happening, someone must want it.”

Not necessarily.

Imagine two coworkers.

A thinks:

> “I don't share information because B doesn't share information.”

B thinks:

> “I don't share information because A doesn't share information.”

Each person's behavior may be a reasonable response to the other's.

Yet both would prefer a workplace with more trust.

That is the everyday power of the equilibrium lens:

> **The pattern can persist because changing alone feels costly.**

The mistake is looking only for the person causing the problem.

The strategic question is:

> **What makes everybody's current behavior a sensible response to everybody else's?**

---

# **The formal insight is stronger than “bad habits”**

This is not just:

> “People get stuck in routines.”

Equilibrium reasoning says something more specific.

Suppose you change your behavior while everyone else keeps theirs unchanged.

If that makes you worse off, then you have a reason **not** to change unilaterally.

That can lock a group into an inferior outcome.

This is why:

> “Why doesn't someone just go first?”

can be a surprisingly hard question.

Going first may carry the cost.

---

# **A workplace example**

Imagine an organization where nobody tells senior management bad news.

Management complains:

> “Why won't people be honest?”

But employees have learned:

* people who raise problems get blamed,  
* people who stay quiet avoid attention.

Given that environment, silence may be a best response.

Management's reaction to silence may then be:

> greater monitoring and pressure.

Employees respond to that by becoming even more guarded.

Now we have:

> pressure → concealment → more pressure → more concealment.

Nobody needs to have designed this system.

The equilibrium emerges from mutual responses.

The point is not that this is literally a formally proven Nash equilibrium unless we specify the strategies and payoffs.

The point is:

> **Equilibrium thinking teaches you to look for the forces stabilizing the pattern.**

That distinction is important for the book.

---

# **We should be careful with everyday use of the word “Nash”**

I think this chapter needs a credibility rule:

> **Use “Nash equilibrium” for formal games. Use “equilibrium lens” for everyday patterns unless we've actually specified the game.**

Otherwise we risk sounding more rigorous than we are.

We can say:

> “This resembles an equilibrium trap.”

But not:

> “Your marriage is literally in a Nash equilibrium.”

unless we've modeled it.

That keeps the book intellectually honest.

---

# **Experimental evidence gives us an important boundary condition**

Here is where things get interesting.

If standard monetary payoffs are the only thing people care about in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma, the prediction is defection.

But humans often cooperate anyway.

Charness, Rigotti, and Rustichini ran clean one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma experiments with **348 participants**. Depending on the payoff from mutual cooperation, cooperation ranged from **23% to 60%**. Participants' beliefs about others' cooperation also changed with the payoff structure. [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0899825616300902?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

That tells us two important things.

First:

> **The equilibrium structure matters.**

Changing the payoffs materially changed behavior.

But second:

> **The simple money-only Nash prediction is not a complete descriptive model of actual people.**

Social preferences, beliefs, errors, norms, and other factors matter too.

That's exactly the kind of nuance we want throughout the book.

---

# **This chapter should teach a distinction between equilibrium as prediction and equilibrium as lens**

Formal Nash equilibrium gives us a precise solution concept.

But for human behavior, we shouldn't say:

> “People always converge to Nash equilibrium.”

They don't.

A large body of behavioral game theory exists precisely because real behavior often departs from simple equilibrium predictions.

MIT's current curriculum still treats Nash equilibrium as foundational, while modern experimental work studies where behavior does and does not fit it. [MIT OpenCourseWare](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/14-12-economic-applications-of-game-theory-fall-2025/resources/lecture-notes/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

So our use is:

> **Equilibrium helps you ask why a pattern persists.**

It does not guarantee that every observed pattern is a Nash equilibrium.

---

# **The strongest practical question in the chapter**

I think it is:

> ## **What makes this pattern stable?**

That question is deceptively powerful.

Suppose everyone complains about something.

Ask:

> Why doesn't anyone change?

Possible answers:

* changing first creates risk,  
* defecting is individually safer,  
* nobody trusts others to reciprocate,  
* incentives reward the bad behavior,  
* people expect retaliation,  
* information is incomplete,  
* reputation locks people into roles,  
* the current pattern is self-reinforcing.

Now we've moved from:

> “People are irrational.”

to:

> **“What strategic forces keep reproducing this?”**

That is exactly the kind of thinking the book is supposed to develop.

---

# **The transition problem**

A particularly important insight is:

> **A better equilibrium can exist and still be difficult to reach.**

Imagine two business partners who would both prefer greater openness.

But each thinks:

> “If I reveal more and the other doesn't, I become vulnerable.”

So both stay guarded.

Telling them:

> “You should trust each other more”

doesn't solve the strategic problem.

The real issue is:

> **How do we move from one stable pattern to another without requiring one person to absorb all the risk?**

That question prepares the reader for later chapters on:

* credibility,  
* commitment,  
* repeated games,  
* coordination,  
* game redesign.

So this chapter acts as a bridge.

---

# **Multiple equilibria matter, but we should save most of that for coordination**

A game can have more than one equilibrium.

That creates another problem:

> Which one will people end up in?

The experimental literature confirms that equilibrium selection itself can be difficult, and recent work continues to study how information and signals affect which equilibrium players coordinate on. [Cambridge University Press](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/experimental-economics/article/abs/equilibrium-selection-through-incomplete-information-in-coordination-games-an-experimental-study/D35C760B7B738F21B5F84E2927ED44A6?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

But I would only introduce that lightly here.

Chapter 10 — **Diagnose the Coordination Problem** — is where focal points, common knowledge, and equilibrium selection should get full treatment.

For Chapter 5, the main idea remains:

> **Stable does not mean desirable.**

---

# **Repetition complicates the equilibrium story**

Another important limitation:

A one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma has one equilibrium structure.

A repeated Prisoner's Dilemma can support many more outcomes.

MIT's repeated-game notes explicitly show that mutual cooperation can become sustainable when the game is repeated and players value the future enough. [MIT OpenCourseWare](https://www.ocw.mit.edu/courses/14-15j-networks-spring-2018/pages/lecture-and-recitation-notes/recitation-10-notes/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Dal Bó and Fréchette's experimental work found that cooperation can indeed emerge in indefinitely repeated Prisoner's Dilemma games, but that the conditions are **more demanding than the simplest theoretical possibility condition**. [AEA Publications](https://pubs.aeaweb.org/doi/10.1257/aer.101.1.411?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Their later survey concludes that cooperation is more likely when it can be supported in equilibrium, but merely being supportable is not enough; it needs to be robust to strategic uncertainty. [American Economic Association](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Fjel.20160980&utm_source=chatgpt.com)

That gives us a beautiful lesson:

> **Knowing that a better equilibrium exists does not mean people will find it.**

That is highly relevant to organizations, teams, and relationships.

# **A very useful human example: mutual defensiveness**

Suppose a couple develops this pattern.

Person A:

> “I raise my voice because you never listen.”

Person B:

> “I stop listening because you raise your voice.”

Both behaviors are responsive.

Both reinforce the other.

Neither person may like the final pattern.

The equilibrium lens asks:

> **What would happen if only one person changed while the other didn't?**

Perhaps:

* the calmer person feels ignored,  
* the more open person feels exposed,  
* the person who stops retaliating fears being exploited.

Now we begin to understand why bad patterns can be persistent.

Again, not a formal Nash claim.

An **equilibrium lens**.

---

# **A business example: discounting**

Suppose two competing businesses both believe:

> “If we raise prices while the competitor stays low, we'll lose customers.”

So both keep prices low.

Both would prefer higher margins.

Yet neither wants to move first.

That's much closer to a classic strategic equilibrium problem.

The interesting question isn't:

> “Why are these companies so foolish?”

It is:

> **Given the other's strategy, what incentive does either have to move alone?**

That's game-theoretic reasoning.

---

# **One of the chapter's most important insights: blame is often strategically incomplete**

People naturally ask:

> “Whose fault is this?”

Sometimes that is necessary.

Ethics and responsibility matter.

But strategic analysis asks an additional question:

> **“Regardless of who caused it, what now keeps it going?”**

Those are not the same question.

A person can be morally responsible for creating a problem.

But once a pattern exists, other strategic forces may maintain it.

Understanding those forces does not erase responsibility.

It helps solve the problem.

---

# **This is where systems thinking and game theory meet**

Systems thinking asks us to notice feedback loops.

Game theory adds:

> **The components in the loop are decision makers adapting strategically to one another.**

That makes equilibrium a particularly useful mental model for human systems.

It's not simply:

> A causes B causes C.

It's:

> I act partly because of what you do.

> You act partly because of what I do.

> Those responses stabilize a pattern.

That is distinctly game-theoretic.

---

# **Equilibrium can also be created by expectations**

Suppose everyone believes:

> “Nobody challenges the CEO.”

So nobody challenges the CEO.

Because nobody challenges the CEO, everyone observes:

> “See? Nobody challenges the CEO.”

That belief becomes self-reinforcing.

This starts overlapping with **Information & Beliefs**.

Later we'll show how beliefs themselves can help sustain equilibria.

For now, the reader simply learns:

> **Expectations can be part of what stabilizes behavior.**

# **The chapter needs one major warning**

We cannot imply that:

> “If something persists, it must be an equilibrium.”

Persistence can arise from:

* habit,  
* coercion,  
* inertia,  
* institutional rules,  
* lack of awareness,  
* temporary conditions.

Equilibrium is a specific strategic concept.

So the correct habit is:

> **Test whether changing unilaterally would actually improve the player's situation.**

If yes, then perhaps the pattern isn't really equilibrium-like.

That's another reason we should teach the model carefully rather than use the word loosely.

| Everyday interpersonal patterns can be usefully viewed through an equilibrium lens | **Our application/synthesis; should not be presented as formally proven Nash equilibria** |

### **Chapter 5 dossier status: very strong**

The chapter's core idea is one of the most powerful in the entire book:

> **A bad situation can survive not because anybody likes it, but because nobody has an incentive to be the only person who changes.**

That naturally brings us to **Chapter 6: Map Information & Beliefs**, where we add another reason people get stuck:

> They may not even be responding to the same understanding of the game.

## **Chapter 6 Research Dossier — Map Information & Beliefs**

The central claim for this chapter should be:

> **Strategic behavior depends not only on what is true, but on what each player knows, does not know, believes, and thinks the other players believe.**

This is one of the deepest ideas in modern game theory. Harsanyi’s work made it possible to analyze games with **incomplete information**, where players are uncertain about things like other players’ preferences, payoffs, or available strategies. MIT still teaches Bayesian games, signaling, bargaining with incomplete information, and common knowledge as major parts of the field. [Nobel Prize](https://www.nobelprize.org/laureate/711?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

For the reader, the chapter should install one basic distinction:

> **Reality is not the same thing as information, and information is not the same thing as belief.**

That is not relativism. Beliefs can be wrong.

# **The four layers**

I think the chapter should make these four layers explicit:

### **1\. Reality**

What is actually true?

### **2\. Information**

What does each player actually know?

### **3\. Belief**

What does each player think is true?

### **4\. Higher-order belief**

What does each player think the *other* player believes?

Game theory goes surprisingly deep on that last one. MIT’s graduate notes explicitly treat **higher-order beliefs**, and the Nobel background on incomplete-information games describes the hierarchy as expectations about others’ expectations, and so on. [MIT OpenCourseWare](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/14.126-game-theory-spring-2024/mit14_126_s24_yildiz-lecture-notes.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

For everyday interaction, we do not need the mathematical machinery.

We need the mental habit:

> **What do I know, what am I assuming, and what might they know that I don’t?**

---

# **The foundational case: Akerlof’s “Market for Lemons”**

Akerlof’s classic used-car model is almost perfect for this chapter.

The seller may know much more about the car’s quality than the buyer.

The buyer understands that the seller possesses private information and therefore becomes suspicious:

> “If this car is so good, why are they selling it?”

That asymmetry can affect prices and, in sufficiently severe cases, shrink or even collapse the market for higher-quality goods. Akerlof’s asymmetric-information work later formed part of the basis for the 2001 Economics Prize. [Nobel Prize](https://www.nobelprize.org/laureate/744?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

The everyday lesson is bigger than used cars:

> **When one player knows something important that another cannot directly observe, the game changes.**

That can happen with:

* job candidates,  
* employers,  
* vendors,  
* business partners,  
* customers,  
* dating,  
* insurance,  
* lending,  
* negotiations.

Someone often knows more about their own:

> quality, intentions, risk, alternatives, or willingness to act

than the other player does.

---

# **A very important distinction: hidden type vs. hidden action**

I think this is worth introducing in plain English, even though we do not need a new mental model.

There are two very different information problems.

### **Hidden type**

Before entering the relationship, you may not know:

> **What kind of player is this?**

Are they:

* reliable?  
* competent?  
* high risk?  
* honest?  
* likely to cooperate?

This is the broad logic behind **adverse selection**.

### **Hidden action**

After the relationship begins, you may not know:

> **What are they actually doing when I cannot observe them?**

Are they:

* working carefully?  
* cutting corners?  
* taking hidden risks?  
* honoring the agreement?

This is the broad logic behind **moral hazard**.

MIT explicitly separates adverse selection and moral hazard in its economics-of-information teaching. [MIT OpenCourseWare](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/14-03-microeconomic-theory-and-public-policy-fall-2025/pages/readings/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

For everyday life, this gives the reader a useful diagnostic:

> **Do I have uncertainty about who this person is—or uncertainty about what they are doing?**

Those are different problems.

---

# **Strong field evidence: information disclosure changes the game**

Tadelis and Zettelmeyer ran a large-scale field experiment in wholesale automobile auctions.

They randomly disclosed quality information about vehicles.

The result was particularly interesting: both good and bad news could increase competition and revenue because buyers differed in what kinds of cars suited them. Better information improved the matching between buyers and vehicles. [AEAweb](https://swlb2.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Faer.20110753&utm_source=chatgpt.com)

That is an excellent piece of evidence because it shows something stronger than:

> “More information is nice.”

It shows:

> **Changing the information structure can change behavior and market outcomes.**

The cars did not change.

The buyers did not change.

The information changed.

And therefore the game changed.

That is exactly the lens we want readers to acquire.

---

# **The everyday equivalent**

Imagine a manager thinks an employee is underperforming.

The manager knows:

> the deadline was missed.

The employee knows something the manager does not:

> another department delivered essential data three days late.

Same outcome.

Different information sets.

The manager might infer:

> incompetence.

The employee might infer:

> unfair blame.

Now the interaction that follows is driven partly by an information asymmetry.

That gives us a strong rule:

> **Before interpreting behavior, ask whether someone may be acting on information you do not have.**

---

# **But information alone is not enough — beliefs matter**

This chapter gets much richer here.

Suppose I know your objective circumstances perfectly.

I may still be uncertain about:

> what you are going to do.

I form a belief.

My decision depends partly on that belief.

You are doing the same thing about me.

That's why Bayesian game theory models not just hidden facts but **beliefs over unknown characteristics or actions**. [MIT OpenCourseWare](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/14-12-economic-applications-of-game-theory-fall-2025/resources/mit14_12f25_lec16_1080p_mp4/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

The everyday translation:

> **People respond to their model of you, not directly to your internal reality.**

That model may be accurate.

Or wildly wrong.

---

# **False beliefs can create real outcomes**

Imagine you believe:

> “My coworker is trying to undermine me.”

So you become guarded.

Your coworker notices.

They conclude:

> “She doesn't trust me.”

They become guarded too.

Now both of you observe behavior that seems to confirm the original suspicion.

The initial belief may have been wrong.

But it changed behavior enough to create a real pattern.

This connects directly to the previous equilibrium chapter:

> **Beliefs can help stabilize the patterns they predict.**

---

# **Experimental evidence on beliefs is wonderfully messy**

Costa-Gomes and Weizsäcker had subjects play **14 one-shot strategic games** and separately report what they expected their opponents to do.

If people simply chose the best response to their consciously stated beliefs, the two should line up fairly well.

They did not.

On average, subjects failed to best respond to their own stated beliefs in **almost half of the games**. Their stated beliefs also appeared to show deeper strategic reasoning than their actual choices. [Edinburgh Research](https://www.research.ed.ac.uk/en/publications/stated-beliefs-and-play-in-normal-form-games/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

That is an excellent boundary condition for our book.

We should not teach:

> “Just figure out what you believe and act rationally from there.”

Humans can hold one belief and behave in a way inconsistent with it.

The broader lesson:

> **Beliefs matter, but human beings do not always translate beliefs into perfectly consistent strategic action.**

That's exactly the kind of evidence-based humility we want.

---

# **This also gives us a warning about mind-reading**

Suppose someone says:

> “He thinks I'm incompetent.”

Game-theoretically, there are at least three levels:

**Fact:**  
What observable behavior occurred?

**Belief:**  
What do I think he believes about me?

**Higher-order belief:**  
What do I think he thinks *I* believe?

These can become extremely tangled.

And the more levels we infer without evidence, the more fragile our model becomes.

So one of the chapter's strongest practical lessons should be:

> **Treat interpretations as hypotheses, not facts.**

---

# **“He knows” is often an assumption**

This deserves its own section because people constantly say:

> “He knows why I'm upset.”

> “She knows that's important to me.”

> “They know what the deadline means.”

Maybe.

Maybe not.

The strategic question is:

> **What evidence do I have that this information is actually in their information set?**

That sounds technical.

But it could prevent enormous numbers of conflicts.

A person cannot respond strategically to information they never had.

---

# **The reverse error also matters**

People also assume:

> “If they disagree, they must not understand.”

That can be equally wrong.

They may understand perfectly.

They may simply have:

* different payoffs,  
* different beliefs about consequences,  
* different risk tolerance,  
* different priorities.

That's why the mental models must work together.

**Information problem:**  
They don't know what you know.

**Payoff problem:**  
They know it and value the outcome differently.

**Belief problem:**  
They disagree about what is likely to happen.

Those require different diagnoses.

---

# **Confidence should track evidence**

A simple but powerful habit from this model is:

> **How certain am I, and why?**

We don't need probability theory.

Just distinguish:

**I know.**

**I have good evidence.**

**I suspect.**

**I'm guessing.**

People routinely convert:

> “I suspect”

into:

> “I know.”

Then communicate with the confidence appropriate to certainty they do not actually possess.

Strategically, that's dangerous because a bad belief can drive an entire response chain.

---

# **Belief about another player's response can matter more than the response itself**

Suppose you are considering asking for a raise.

You believe:

> “If I ask, my boss will think I'm disloyal.”

Maybe that's false.

But if you believe it strongly enough, you may never ask.

The boss's actual response never occurs.

Your belief about the response determines your move.

That is game-theoretically important:

> **Expected responses influence behavior before those responses ever happen.**

So information and beliefs aren't just retrospective interpretations.

They shape the future game.

---

# **Higher-order beliefs can become surprisingly important**

Consider a team where almost everyone privately dislikes a policy.

But each person thinks:

> “Everyone else seems okay with it.”

So nobody speaks.

Now suppose someone says publicly:

> “It sounds like several of us have concerns.”

The underlying private opinions haven't changed.

But something else has:

> **what people know about what other people know.**

We should save the full treatment of **common knowledge** for Chapter 10 on coordination.

But this chapter can plant the seed:

> **Sometimes knowing a fact is not enough. What matters is what people believe others know about it.**

---

# **Information has strategic value**

This chapter should introduce one extremely useful question:

> **What information would change my decision?**

That is more important than:

> “What else can I find out?”

Not all information is equally valuable.

Imagine you're deciding whether to enter a partnership.

Knowing their favorite movie probably changes nothing.

Knowing:

> they are carrying \$2 million in undisclosed debt

might change everything.

The value of information comes from its potential to change:

* your action,  
* your estimate of their action,  
* your understanding of payoffs,  
* or your estimate of the outcome.

That's a very useful decision habit.

---

# **More information is not automatically better**

This is another good nuance.

People can drown in details and still miss the strategically relevant unknown.

Imagine someone spends hours researching a negotiating counterpart's biography but has never asked:

> “Who actually has authority to approve this deal?”

More data.

Less useful information.

So the model should teach:

> **Seek decision-relevant information, not information for its own sake.**

---

# **Private information is not the same as deception**

This is ethically important.

People are not required to disclose:

* every thought,  
* every fear,  
* every alternative,  
* every preference,  
* every piece of private information.

Game theory assumes private information all the time.

But our ethics impose a boundary:

> **Keeping something private is different from actively deceiving someone about material facts necessary for informed choice.**

We should probably develop that distinction carefully later, particularly in the credibility chapter.

---

# **An important experiment on belief measurement also keeps us humble**

Recent experimental work continues to show that beliefs can be measured imperfectly and that how researchers elicit beliefs can affect what they observe. For example, Rutström and Wilcox found that incentivized belief elicitation could alter early play in a repeated asymmetric matching-pennies game under some conditions. [Chapman University Digital Commons](https://digitalcommons.chapman.edu/esi_pubs/50/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

That may not belong in the main narrative, but it is a valuable methodological note:

> **Even asking people what they believe can sometimes affect the game.**

Which is another reminder that human belief is not a perfectly transparent internal object.

# **A classic teaching case: “The Market for Lemons”**

I think the chapter should probably begin or end its theory section with Akerlof.

You have two cars.

One is excellent.

One is terrible.

The seller knows which one they have.

The buyer does not.

Now ask:

> How much should the buyer be willing to pay?

The buyer must account not only for the physical car but for:

> **why this seller wants to sell this particular car.**

That transforms the transaction.

And once the reader understands that, we can say:

> **You live in versions of this game constantly.**

Who knows more?

Who is trying to infer what the other knows?

Who has private information?

What does their action reveal?

That then flows beautifully into Chapter 7 on credibility and signaling.

# **A personal relationship example**

Imagine your spouse says:

> “I'm fine.”

You observe:

* short answers,  
* distant behavior,  
* tension.

You infer:

> “They're angry with me.”

Possible realities:

1. They are angry with you.  
2. They're upset about work.  
3. They're exhausted.  
4. They're worried about someone else.  
5. You're reading too much into it.

The strategic error isn't forming a hypothesis.

The error is:

> **promoting a hypothesis to a fact before you have evidence.**

That's a simple way of making Bayesian humility understandable without teaching Bayes' theorem.

# **A leadership example**

Manager:

> “Why didn't you tell me earlier?”

Employee:

> “I thought you already knew.”

That's almost a pure Information & Beliefs example.

The manager believes:

> “If there were a problem, they would tell me.”

The employee believes:

> “Management already knows.”

Both behave consistently with their beliefs.

The outcome is bad.

Communication failed because the information model was wrong.

# **The ethical dimension**

This model can improve empathy without sliding into relativism.

It teaches:

> **Before judging someone’s behavior, ask what information and beliefs made that behavior seem reasonable to them.**

That does not mean:

> therefore their behavior was right.

It means:

> understand before evaluating.

Truth remains truth.

A false belief remains false.

But understanding the belief can explain the behavior.

# **What this chapter should NOT claim**

**Not:** “Everyone has their own truth.”

Absolutely not.

**Not:** “If someone believes something, their behavior is rational.”

Beliefs can be irrational, and actions can fail to best respond even to stated beliefs. [OUP Academic](https://academic.oup.com/restud/article-abstract/75/3/729/1553652?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

**Not:** “More information always improves outcomes.”

The effect depends on the game, although strong field evidence shows that changing information can materially change outcomes in important settings. [AEAweb](https://swlb2.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Faer.20110753&utm_source=chatgpt.com)

**Not:** “You can infer people's private information perfectly from behavior.”

That problem is exactly why signaling theory exists.

**Not:** “People know their own beliefs perfectly.”

Experimental evidence gives us reason to be cautious about that as well.

The defensible claim is:

> **Different information and beliefs can produce different strategic choices, so good strategic analysis must distinguish facts from knowledge, uncertainty, and inference.**

## **Chapter 7 Research Dossier — Separate Words From Credibility**

The central claim for this chapter should be:

> **A message does not become credible simply because it is clear, confident, or persuasive. Its credibility depends partly on the speaker’s information, incentives, evidence, behavior, and the structure of the game.**

This is where the book moves beyond ordinary communication advice.

A traditional communication book might ask:

> “How can I make my message more convincing?”

Game theory asks a more fundamental question:

> **“Why should the other player believe me?”**

And from the listener’s side:

> **“What reason do I have to believe this message?”**

That distinction takes us directly into **cheap talk, signaling, asymmetric information, and strategic information transmission**.

---

# **The foundational problem**

Chapter 6 established that different players often possess different information.

Now imagine:

**You know something I don’t.**

You tell me what you know.

But my decision affects your payoff.

That immediately creates a strategic problem:

> **Should I believe you?**

This is precisely the environment studied in sender-receiver games.

Crawford and Sobel’s classic 1982 model formalized a better-informed sender communicating with a receiver whose subsequent action affects both players. One of their central findings was that informative communication becomes more possible as sender and receiver preferences become more aligned. [Haverford College](https://www.haverford.edu/sites/default/files/CrawfordSobel1982.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

That gives us a profound everyday insight:

> **The credibility of words depends partly on the relationship between the speaker’s interests and yours.**

Not only on how sincere they sound.

---

# **Cheap talk does not mean useless talk**

This needs to be handled carefully because *cheap talk* sounds insulting.

In game theory, cheap talk refers to messages that are essentially:

* costless to send,  
* nonbinding,  
* and do not directly alter the material payoffs of the game.

Farrell and Rabin’s influential review makes an important point: both extreme views are wrong.

Cheap talk is **not automatically credible**.

But it is also **not automatically meaningless**. [American Economic Association](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Fjep.10.3.103&utm_source=chatgpt.com)

That distinction is perfect for our book.

Imagine I tell you:

> “The restaurant is two blocks north.”

If I want you to find the restaurant, our interests are aligned.

I have little incentive to mislead you.

My words cost nothing, but they can still carry excellent information.

Now imagine I'm selling you a house and say:

> “You won't find anything better at this price.”

Same person.

Still merely words.

But now my incentives are different.

The proper reaction isn't:

> “He's lying.”

It's:

> **“How much information does this statement actually give me given his incentives?”**

That's much more sophisticated.

---

# **The first major principle: incentive alignment affects how informative words can be**

Crawford-Sobel gives us a strong theoretical foundation for this.

When sender and receiver preferences become more aligned, more detailed information can be credibly communicated in equilibrium. As their interests diverge, informative communication becomes more limited. [Haverford College](https://www.haverford.edu/sites/default/files/CrawfordSobel1982.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

That does **not** mean:

> people with conflicting interests always lie.

It means:

> **When the sender benefits from moving your decision away from what your information would otherwise support, you have more reason to discount unsupported claims.**

That's a game-theoretic way of evaluating communication.

---

# **Excellent experimental evidence: cheap talk can work**

Cox and Stoddard ran experiments involving a public-good decision in which players received private signals about whether contributing was valuable.

Players could communicate before choosing.

There were social gains from communicating truthfully, but players also had an individual incentive to exaggerate.

What happened?

Communication was **largely truthful and increased efficiency**.

Then the researchers strengthened the incentive to exaggerate.

Truthfulness declined, and the gains from communication became smaller. [American Economic Association](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Fmic.20180275&utm_source=chatgpt.com)

This is almost tailor-made for our chapter.

It demonstrates:

> **Words can carry real information even when they are cheap—but credibility changes when the incentive to distort changes.**

That is much better than the cliché:

> “Actions speak louder than words.”

Sometimes words communicate extremely well.

The real question is:

> **What game surrounds the words?**

---

# **Another experiment gives us a useful complication**

Li and Peeters studied a sender communicating private information to two potential entrants.

Senders' messages actually conveyed useful truthful information, and receivers picked up on that information.

But the improved information did **not** translate into the expected efficiency gains because receivers did not optimally use what they learned. [PLOS](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0163783&utm_source=chatgpt.com)

That gives us an important boundary:

> **Credible communication does not guarantee good decisions.**

There are at least two separate problems:

1. Did useful information get communicated?  
2. Did the receiver use it intelligently?

That's an excellent example of how these mental models interact.

---

# **Messages can influence people even when they should theoretically be discounted**

Another experimental result is worth including as a caution.

In ultimatum bargaining experiments with imperfect information, costless lies and even theoretically incredible threats still affected offers and responses. Participants sometimes responded to messages that standard theory might classify as strategically noncredible. [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0167268102000926?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Similarly, an experimental entry-deterrence study found that costless threats affected behavior, particularly early in repeated play. [Sage Journals](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0022002711414372?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

This gives us an extremely important distinction:

> **A message can influence someone without being credible evidence.**

People respond psychologically to:

* confidence,  
* threats,  
* authority,  
* emotional language,  
* repetition.

But:

> **Influence is not the same as information.**

That could be one of the chapter's most important lessons.

Something making you *feel* convinced does not necessarily mean the message gave you better evidence about reality.

---

# **This brings us to signaling**

Michael Spence's Nobel-recognized work addressed a different problem.

Suppose I possess some characteristic you can't directly observe.

Maybe:

* competence,  
* commitment,  
* quality,  
* reliability.

How can I communicate that information?

One possibility is an observable action that different “types” of players find differently costly or attractive.

Spence's original application involved education as a signal in labor markets. His broader contribution was showing how better-informed actors may use observable actions to convey private information to less-informed actors. [Nobel Prize](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/2001/spence/facts/?trk=public_post_comment-text&utm_source=chatgpt.com)

This creates a very different way to think about credibility:

> **Sometimes what someone is willing or able to do tells you something their words alone cannot.**

---

# **Experimental signaling evidence**

Kübler, Müller, and Normann experimentally recreated versions of Spence's job-market signaling model.

More productive worker types invested in education more frequently, and employers offered higher wages to workers who invested. But separation was incomplete and behavior did not perfectly converge to the theoretical equilibrium. [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0899825608000067?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

That's exactly the kind of evidence standard we want.

Theory:

> Signals can separate types.

Experiment:

> The mechanism appeared, but imperfectly.

Translation:

> **Behavior can contain information—but don't assume signals perfectly reveal hidden qualities.**

---

# **A particularly good field experiment**

Breza, Kanz, and Klapper studied workers at a factory who could open a **commitment savings account**.

Opening the account could credibly signal an intention to remain with the company.

Workers were more likely to sign up when their decision was visible to their employer, and managers in a related experiment were more willing to invest in the human capital of workers who had such accounts. [American Economic Association](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Fpandp.20181011&utm_source=chatgpt.com)

This is incredibly useful for the book.

The workers could simply have said:

> “I plan to stay here.”

But the observable commitment carried additional information.

It affected how employers behaved.

That provides a real-world bridge between:

**words → signals → beliefs → responses.**

We'll save the deeper strategic logic of **commitment itself** for Chapter 8\.

Here, the lesson is simply:

> **Actions can alter the evidentiary value of a claim.**

---

# **But we need to kill a dangerous oversimplification**

It would be very easy to teach:

> **Costly signals are credible.**

That's too crude.

Cost does not magically create truth.

A liar can spend money.

A bad company can buy expensive advertising.

Someone can make a dramatic sacrifice and still be wrong or dishonest.

For a signal to reliably reveal hidden information, the structure has to make that signal meaningfully different for different types of player.

In other words:

> **Why is this signal easier, cheaper, more worthwhile, or more sustainable for someone whose claim is true?**

That's much closer to signaling theory.

---

# **And experiments warn us not to overclaim costly signaling**

Kai Quek conducted three experiments specifically testing costly signaling in deterrence.

High-resolve senders were indeed more willing to incur costs.

But receivers did not respond as strongly to those sunk costs as senders expected.

There was a **sender-receiver gap**: the person paying the cost thought the signal was more persuasive than the receiver did. [RCNi Company Limited](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/685751?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

That is a fantastic counterweight.

It tells us:

> **Paying a price for a message does not guarantee the other person will interpret it the way you expect.**

Which fits our philosophy perfectly:

> The other player remains an independent decision maker.

---

# **A simple everyday example**

Suppose someone tells you:

> “I'm completely committed to this business.”

What should you make of that?

Possibilities:

### **Words**

They say they're committed.

### **Behavior**

They consistently show up and do the work.

### **Investment**

They've put significant time or resources into it.

### **Opportunity cost**

They turned down another opportunity to continue.

### **History**

They've stuck with difficult projects before.

Each piece of information may affect your belief differently.

The strategic thinker does not ask:

> “Do they sound sincere?”

alone.

They ask:

> **“What evidence does this game provide about the claim?”**

---

# **Credibility has at least three dimensions**

I think we should teach this explicitly.

### **1\. Honesty**

Are they reporting what they genuinely believe?

### **2\. Knowledge**

Are they actually in a position to know?

### **3\. Incentives**

What reason do they have to communicate accurately—or inaccurately?

This solves a lot of confusion.

Your grandmother might tell you:

> “That investment is completely safe.”

She may be:

**100% honest**

and

**poorly informed.**

A salesperson may be:

**extremely knowledgeable**

while also having:

**a strong financial incentive to persuade you.**

Credibility is not binary.

---

# **Behavior can contradict verbal communication**

This becomes very practical.

A manager says:

> “I value honest disagreement.”

Then punishes employees who challenge them.

What carries more strategic information?

The speech?

Or the historical payoff structure?

A company says:

> “Quality comes first.”

But compensation rewards speed exclusively.

Again:

> **The environment may communicate something different from the slogan.**

That does not mean “actions always speak louder than words.”

It means:

> **When words and incentives conflict, investigate the conflict.**

---

# **This also changes how we think about persuasion**

Suppose someone understands your argument perfectly but still doesn't believe you.

Most communication training responds by improving:

* wording,  
* storytelling,  
* framing,  
* delivery.

But what if the actual problem is:

> **credibility?**

Then better rhetoric may accomplish very little.

You may need:

* evidence,  
* verification,  
* demonstration,  
* reputation,  
* aligned incentives,  
* behavior consistent with the claim.

That's one of the strongest ways this book differs from ordinary persuasion training:

> **Not every persuasion failure is a message problem.**

Sometimes the listener understood your message just fine.

They simply have no reason to believe it.

---

# **Reputation belongs partly here**

Suppose two contractors both say:

> “If something goes wrong, I'll come back and fix it.”

One is unknown.

The other has worked with your family for 20 years and has consistently honored that promise.

The words are identical.

The evidence isn't.

Past behavior changes the receiver's belief about future behavior.

We should introduce that lightly here, then give reputation its full strategic treatment in **The Shadow of the Future**.

---

# **The listener should also ask: “Would they say this regardless?”**

This may become one of the simplest mental questions in the chapter.

When someone makes an important claim:

> **Would they have an incentive to say this even if it weren't true?**

If yes, that does not prove they're lying.

It tells you:

> **the words themselves are less discriminating evidence.**

Job applicant:

> “I'm a hard worker.”

Almost every applicant benefits from saying it.

Long record of consistently completing difficult work?

More informative.

Business:

> “Our customers love us.”

Easy claim.

Independent retention data?

Different evidence.

# **Signals can also be deliberately verified**

A recent randomized field experiment on an online matching platform examined **optional verification** as a visible signal. The work was designed around the idea that verified information can reduce information asymmetry when most other profile information is self-reported. [PubsOnline](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/isre.2022.1194?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

That belongs nicely as a modern application:

> **When words are cheap, verification can alter credibility.**

Again, not universally.

But strategically.

# **We should distinguish signaling from commitment**

This is important because Chapter 8 comes next.

### **Signal**

An action conveys information about something hidden.

> “What does this behavior tell you about me?”

### **Commitment**

An action changes what I can or will rationally do later.

> “How does this action constrain my future choices?”

One action can sometimes do both.

The factory savings account is a good example: it can signal intention while also involving commitment.

But conceptually they're different.

Keeping them separate will make Chapters 7 and 8 much stronger.

# **The ethical boundary**

This chapter is particularly important ethically because strategic communication can easily slide into:

> “How do I make something *look* credible?”

That isn't what we're teaching.

The ethical use is:

> **Make truthful claims easier to evaluate.**

and:

> **Evaluate other people's claims intelligently.**

Our framework should never teach:

> manufacture false signals,  
> conceal material facts,  
> fake costly commitment,  
> exploit trust.

Instead:

> **Align evidence with truth.**

That may become a recurring ethical principle throughout the book.

Two people tell you:

Person A benefits greatly if you believe them regardless of whether the claim is true.

Person B's incentives are strongly aligned with yours.

Obviously not.

Still no.

# **What this chapter should NOT claim**

**Not:** “Talk is cheap, so ignore words.”

They don't.

**Not:** “Costly signals are always credible.”

**Not:** “Actions are always more truthful than words.”

**Not:** “You can infer hidden motives perfectly from signals.”

Signaling exists precisely because inference under asymmetric information is difficult.

The defensible claim is:

> **Evaluate messages using the strategic context around them—not the wording alone.**

## **Chapter 8 Research Dossier — Commitment Changes the Game**

The central claim for this chapter should be:

> **Sometimes having fewer options can make you strategically stronger.**

This is one of the most counterintuitive ideas in game theory, and it absolutely deserves its own chapter.

Thomas Schelling’s contribution here is foundational. The Nobel committee explicitly highlighted his insight that a player can sometimes strengthen its position by **openly limiting its own options**—what the Nobel presentation memorably described as “burning its ships.” Schelling’s analysis of credible commitments helped launch a large literature on strategic self-binding. [Nobel Prize](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/2005/ceremony-speech/?print=1&utm_source=chatgpt.com)

MIT’s applied game-theory courses still treat **commitment / strategic moves** as a major theme in their own right, specifically alongside credibility, threats, promises, hidden information, and limited rationality. [MIT OpenCourseWare](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/15-040-game-theory-for-managers-spring-2004/pages/syllabus/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

That gives us a strong foundation for the chapter.

### **The paradox**

Normally, we assume:

> **More options \= more power.**

Usually, flexibility is valuable.

If I can choose among five responses and you can choose among only two, that often seems advantageous.

But in strategic interaction, flexibility can sometimes create a credibility problem.

Suppose I tell you:

> “I absolutely will not accept less than \$100,000.”

If I remain completely free to accept \$90,000 tomorrow, you may rationally think:

> “He says that now, but if I hold out, he'll probably take less.”

My flexibility undermines my statement.

Now imagine some real constraint makes accepting less than \$100,000 impossible.

You no longer need to decide whether I am bluffing.

If the commitment is credible, **your decision problem changes**.

That is the mental model:

> **Changing your own future options can change another player's present best response.**

---

# **Commitment is not merely “being determined”**

This distinction matters.

People use the word commitment loosely:

> “I'm committed to losing weight.”

> “I'm committed to this business.”

> “I'm committed to making the relationship work.”

Those may be sincere intentions.

But strategic commitment means something stronger:

> **You take an action that changes your future incentives or available choices in a way another player can recognize.**

That's why the chapter should distinguish:

### **Intention**

> “I plan to do X.”

### **Promise**

> “I tell you that I will do X.”

### **Commitment**

> **“I alter the game so that doing something other than X becomes harder, costlier, or impossible.”**

That is the game-theoretic idea.

---

# **The classic illustration: burn the ships**

The “burn the ships” image is powerful because it captures the paradox immediately.

Imagine an invading army arrives on an island.

If soldiers know retreat remains available, they may retreat when battle becomes costly.

If retreat is physically removed, the opponent now knows:

> **They cannot simply withdraw.**

The attacking army has worsened its own options.

But in doing so, it may change the defender's expectations and behavior.

The Nobel committee specifically used this kind of logic to explain Schelling’s insight: overtly restricting one's own alternatives can sometimes strengthen bargaining position. [Nobel Prize](https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2018/06/popular-economicsciences2005.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

We should be careful not to romanticize reckless commitment. The point is not:

> “Always eliminate your escape routes.”

The point is:

> **Flexibility and strategic power are not always the same thing.**

---

# **Commitment solves a credibility problem**

This chapter follows perfectly from Chapter 7\.

Chapter 7 asked:

> **Why should they believe me?**

Chapter 8 says:

> **One answer is to make future behavior costly to reverse.**

Suppose a company says:

> “We guarantee this product for ten years.”

A warranty may increase credibility because honoring it becomes costly if quality is poor.

But Chapter 7 treats that mainly as a **signal**.

Commitment asks a different question:

> **How does the warranty change what the company is obligated or incentivized to do later?**

Same action.

Different lens.

That distinction is worth teaching.

---

# **Threats only matter if they are credible**

Suppose a parent says:

> “If you're late again, you're grounded for six months.”

The child may think:

> “No chance.”

The threat is severe, but severity is not the same as credibility.

Suppose instead the parent creates a clearly established consequence they consistently enforce.

Now the relevant question becomes:

> **Will this actually happen if the condition occurs?**

Game theory cares enormously about whether threats and promises would still make sense when the future decision point arrives.

MIT’s commitment material explicitly frames strategic moves around the credibility of threats and promises. [MIT OpenCourseWare](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/15-040-game-theory-for-managers-spring-2004/pages/lecture-notes/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

This is the key:

> **A threat that you will not rationally carry out when the moment comes may have little strategic value.**

---

# **Commitment can create bargaining power**

This is where the theory becomes especially counterintuitive.

Imagine two parties negotiating.

One can credibly demonstrate:

> “I cannot accept anything below X.”

The other party now has fewer useful strategies.

Continuing to pressure below X may accomplish nothing.

So the committed party's reduced flexibility can shift bargaining power.

Schelling saw this clearly: the ability to constrain oneself can influence the other side's best response. The Nobel background explicitly notes that an agent may strengthen its bargaining position by worsening its own options. [Nobel Prize](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/uncategorized/the-sveriges-riksbank-prize-in-economic-sciences-in-memory-of-alfred-nobel-1969-2007/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

---

# **Laboratory evidence: commitment can improve the committer's payoff**

Swope, Cadigan, and Schmitt studied bargaining environments where players could credibly commit to bargaining positions.

They found that commitment could increase the committer's payoff, particularly in one-to-many bargaining settings where holdout behavior was possible. But there was a cost: commitment could also increase bargaining failure and reduce total efficiency. [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2214804314000160?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

That is an excellent experiment for this chapter because it prevents us from teaching commitment as magic.

The evidence says something more nuanced:

> **Commitment can strengthen your strategic position while also making agreement harder.**

That's exactly what we would expect conceptually.

By removing flexibility, you may gain bargaining power.

But you also remove pathways to compromise.

---

# **A very recent experiment adds another layer**

A 2026 *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization* experiment studied precommitment before sequential ultimatum bargaining.

The results did not simply match clean theoretical predictions. Players did use commitment strategically, but commitments were often less aggressive than predicted. In some later rounds, allowing precommitment produced very high efficiency, and the authors found behavior consistent with reciprocity and concerns about equal opportunity alongside strategic incentives. [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0167268125004779?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

That is ideal evidence for our approach.

Theory says:

> commitment can alter bargaining power.

Experiment says:

> humans use commitment, but fairness, reciprocity, and behavioral considerations also shape outcomes.

Again:

> **game theory gives the lens; behavior is messier.**

---

# **Commitment can backfire**

This should be a major section.

If you commit too aggressively, you may create an impasse.

Suppose two negotiators each publicly commit:

> “I will never go below X.”

and

> “I will never go above Y.”

If:

> X \> Y,

they have engineered failure.

The commitments may be individually credible and collectively disastrous.

This is why the bargaining experiment above found commitment could increase failed agreements. [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2214804314000160?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

So one of the chapter's key lessons is:

> **Credibility is not enough. You can credibly commit yourself to a bad outcome.**

That's a very important strategic distinction.

---

# **Commitment changes the other player's options too**

Imagine a business partner tells you:

> “I've already signed a noncancelable supplier agreement.”

That may constrain them.

But it also constrains **you**, because certain joint options are now unavailable.

This is why commitments can change the entire game tree.

Before commitment:

> A, B, C, D are possible.

After commitment:

> perhaps only A and B remain.

The other player must now solve a different strategic problem.

This is more than persuasion.

You've changed the choice architecture of the game itself.

---

# **The first-mover connection**

Commitment also helps explain why moving first can sometimes create advantage.

Suppose a company invests heavily in production capacity before competitors decide whether to enter a market.

That investment may make aggressive future production more believable.

The competitor's decision changes because the first player's earlier move altered the future game.

The Nobel committee specifically cited strategic investment in capacity as an application of Schelling-style commitment logic. [Nobel Prize](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/2005/ceremony-speech/?print=1&utm_source=chatgpt.com)

But first-mover advantage is not universal.

Sometimes moving first reveals information or locks you into a poor position.

So the lesson is:

> **Moving first matters when the early move changes later incentives or options.**

---

# **Commitment and boundaries**

This gives us a useful everyday application.

Suppose someone says:

> “If you continue speaking to me that way, I'm ending the conversation.”

If they repeatedly fail to follow through, the statement becomes cheap talk.

But if they consistently disengage when the boundary is crossed, the other player eventually learns:

> **This constraint is real.**

The important idea is not that every boundary must be rigid.

It's:

> **A boundary affects strategic behavior only to the degree that others believe it will actually constrain your future behavior.**

That links commitment, credibility, and repeated interaction.

---

# **Commitment and self-control**

There is also a second branch of commitment worth mentioning carefully.

Behavioral economics studies **commitment devices** people use against their own future behavior.

For example:

* automatic savings,  
* deadlines,  
* restricted accounts,  
* public commitments.

A field experiment with university students found that offering a nonbinding “soft commitment” to stay on track for graduation increased exam registration, participation, and passing, especially among procrastinators; simple reminders did not produce the same effect. [American Economic Association](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Fapp.20170288&utm_source=chatgpt.com)

This is not exactly the same problem as Schelling's interpersonal strategic commitment.

One is:

> **I bind myself to influence you.**

The other is:

> **I bind myself to influence my future self.**

But the common mental model is valuable:

> **Reducing future freedom can sometimes improve outcomes.**

I would include this as a sidebar rather than make it central.

---

# **A field experiment connecting commitment and signaling**

The Breza, Kanz, and Klapper factory study is useful again here.

Workers were offered commitment savings accounts that could signal an intention to remain employed. Workers were more likely to sign up when employers could observe the decision, and managers were subsequently more willing to invest in workers with such accounts. [American Economic Association](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Fpandp.20181011&utm_source=chatgpt.com)

As we said in Chapter 7, this blends two mechanisms:

**Signal:**  
The account communicates likely intent.

**Commitment:**  
The financial structure makes the intent more consequential.

That's actually a useful teaching example because real actions often perform more than one strategic function.

---

# **Commitment should be observable if it is meant to influence another player**

This is obvious once stated, but strategically important.

Suppose I secretly eliminate one of my options.

If you don't know I have done so, your current behavior may not change.

For strategic commitment to affect another player's choice, they generally need to know—or credibly infer—that the commitment exists.

So:

> **A private constraint can change your future behavior.**

But:

> **An observable credible constraint can change their present behavior.**

That distinction is excellent.

---

# **Fake commitment is not commitment**

Suppose someone says:

> “This is my final offer.”

But they have said “final offer” four times already and continued moving each time.

That isn't credible strategic commitment.

It's rhetoric.

This chapter should train readers to ask:

> **What actually prevents them from changing later?**

Maybe:

* contract,  
* public announcement,  
* sunk investment,  
* legal restriction,  
* delegation,  
* reputational cost,  
* physical constraint.

If the answer is:

> “Nothing except that they said so,”

then we're back in Chapter 7's cheap-talk problem.

---

# **Delegation can create commitment**

This is another fascinating game-theory idea.

Suppose a CEO has authority to compromise.

That flexibility may weaken their bargaining position.

The CEO delegates negotiation to someone with strict instructions:

> “You cannot approve more than \$X.”

Now when the negotiator says:

> “I don't have authority,”

it may genuinely be true.

Delegation has converted:

> “I don't want to”

into:

> “I cannot.”

That changes the other side's decision problem.

Schelling's commitment ideas have been applied broadly to delegation and institutional design; the Nobel background specifically notes applications to delegating political decision power. [Nobel Prize](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/uncategorized/the-sveriges-riksbank-prize-in-economic-sciences-in-memory-of-alfred-nobel-1969-2007/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Again, ethically neutral in itself.

Its use depends on context.

---

# **Public commitments can change the cost of reversal**

Suppose a leader privately intends:

> “We will finish this project.”

Versus publicly announcing:

> “We will launch on October 1.”

The public commitment can increase the reputational cost of abandoning the date.

That may make follow-through more likely.

But it can also create a danger:

> continuing with a bad plan merely to avoid losing face.

So commitment can improve discipline **and** create rigidity.

That's an important boundary.

---

# **Sunk costs are not the same as rational commitment**

This distinction must be explicit.

A strategic commitment changes future incentives.

A sunk cost is a cost that has already been paid and should not automatically justify continuing.

Suppose you spend \$1 million on a project.

That may make outsiders believe you're serious.

Strategically, it may function as a signal or commitment.

But once the money is irrecoverable, rational decision-making should not say:

> “We've spent too much to stop.”

That's the sunk-cost fallacy.

We should distinguish:

> **Using an irreversible action deliberately to alter a strategic game**

from:

> **irrationally continuing because you already spent resources.**

Very important.

---

# **Commitment is dangerous when information may change**

If the future is uncertain, binding yourself today can become costly.

Suppose you publicly promise:

> “We will never change this policy.”

Then new information emerges showing the policy is harmful.

Now your prior commitment makes adaptation more difficult.

So the value of commitment depends partly on:

* how much uncertainty exists,  
* how likely new information is,  
* how costly inflexibility would be.

This naturally connects backward to **Information & Beliefs**.

A great commitment in a well-understood game can be a disaster in a rapidly changing one.

---

# **The strategic question isn't “How committed am I?”**

It's:

> **“What future options am I giving up, and how does that change the other player's incentives?”**

That is the precise mental-model version.

You can be emotionally passionate without strategically committed.

And you can be strategically committed without feeling passionate at all.

---

# **An important ethical section**

Commitment can become coercive if used badly.

For example:

> “I've deliberately put both of us in a position where you now have no reasonable choice.”

That can cross into manipulation or coercion depending on context.

Our ethical use of commitment should be constrained by:

* truth,  
* autonomy,  
* dignity,  
* informed choice.

So we teach:

> **Understand how commitment changes strategic options.**

Not:

> “Trap people.”

Sometimes legitimate commitment is simply:

* a deadline,  
* a contract,  
* a boundary,  
* a transparent policy,  
* a public promise.

The ethics depend on what is being constrained and how.

You are buying something.

Seller says:

You suspect they'll take \$9,000.

Now change the situation.

The seller has legally committed the asset to another buyer if your offer is below \$10,000.

Not the seller's desire.

# **A second teaching example: the bridge**

Suppose two armies face each other across a bridge.

One side destroys the bridge behind itself.

It has reduced its own escape options.

Why might that strengthen its bargaining position?

Because the opponent now understands:

> retreat is no longer available.

Then we reveal Schelling.

That's memorable.

**Not:** “Commitment always creates power.”

No.

No.

Absolutely not.

## **Chapter 9 Research Dossier — The Shadow of the Future**

The central claim should be:

> **When people expect to interact again, the future changes the value of what they do today.**

This is foundational repeated-game theory. Aumann’s work formalized how repeated interaction can sustain cooperation even when one-shot incentives point toward conflict, and the Nobel committee specifically highlighted repeated games as a way to understand long-run relationships, conflict, and cooperation. [Nobel Prize](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/2005/ceremony-speech/?print=1&utm_source=chatgpt.com)

For the reader, the key question becomes:

> **What changes because there is a next time?**

That is the mental model.

# **The formal foundation**

A repeated game takes a “stage game” and lets the same strategic interaction occur again over time. Players can condition present behavior on what happened previously. Future payoffs are often discounted, so how much people value tomorrow relative to today matters. MIT’s repeated-game notes formalize exactly this structure. [MIT OpenCourseWare](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/14-126-game-theory-spring-2016/3fc58b6d07a73055a44dcbc5aaacc738_MIT14_126S16_Repeated.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

In plain English:

> **Once there is a tomorrow, today's behavior can affect tomorrow's rewards, punishments, trust, and opportunities.**

That means the strategic value of a move can change dramatically even though the immediate interaction looks identical.

---

# **The classic example: Prisoner’s Dilemma repeated**

In a one-shot Prisoner’s Dilemma, defection is the standard Nash prediction.

Repeat the game indefinitely, or make continuation sufficiently likely, and new strategies become possible because current defection can affect future treatment.

MIT’s notes summarize the central result simply: cooperative outcomes can be sustained in repeated Prisoner’s Dilemma games when players are patient enough. [MIT OpenCourseWare](https://www.ocw.mit.edu/courses/14-15j-networks-spring-2018/pages/lecture-and-recitation-notes/recitation-10-notes/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

That does **not** mean:

> repeated interaction automatically creates cooperation.

It means:

> **Future consequences can change what strategies are sustainable.**

That distinction is going to matter throughout the chapter.

---

# **The strongest experimental anchor: Dal Bó 2005**

Pedro Dal Bó designed laboratory Prisoner’s Dilemma games in which the probability of another round was experimentally varied.

That is almost a direct test of the “shadow of the future.”

He found that a longer expected future significantly reduced opportunistic behavior, and that the effect tracked theoretical predictions reasonably closely. [American Economic Association](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2F000282805775014434&utm_source=chatgpt.com)

This is excellent evidence for the chapter because it isolates the variable we care about:

> **When the expected future became longer, behavior changed.**

Same basic players.

Same basic strategic problem.

Different expected continuation.

Different incentives.

That gives us a defensible translation:

> **People may behave differently when today's action can affect tomorrow's interaction.**

---

# **But later research gives us the necessary caution**

Dal Bó and Fréchette later studied how cooperation evolves with experience in indefinitely repeated Prisoner’s Dilemma games.

They found that cooperation can emerge, but the conditions were **more demanding** than simply asking whether cooperation was theoretically supportable. [AEA Publications](https://pubs.aeaweb.org/doi/abs/10.1257/aer.101.1.411?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Their 2018 review of the experimental literature reached a similar conclusion:

* indefinite repetition tends to increase cooperation,  
* cooperation is more likely when it can be supported in equilibrium,  
* but mere theoretical possibility is not enough,  
* high cooperation tends to require that cooperation be fairly robust to strategic uncertainty. [AEA Publications](https://pubs.aeaweb.org/doi/10.1257/jel.20160980?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

That boundary is important.

We should not teach:

> “If you'll see each other again, cooperate.”

We should teach:

> **Future interaction creates the possibility of reputation, reciprocity, and conditional cooperation—but whether cooperation actually emerges depends on incentives, beliefs, monitoring, and uncertainty.**

Much stronger.

---

# **Axelrod belongs here—but accurately**

Robert Axelrod’s famous iterated Prisoner’s Dilemma tournaments are highly relevant.

Researchers submitted computer strategies to play repeated Prisoner’s Dilemma against one another. A simple strategy—Tit for Tat—performed exceptionally well in Axelrod’s tournaments, helping demonstrate how reciprocal cooperation could emerge under repeated interaction. Axelrod and Hamilton later connected this logic to evolutionary cooperation. [Gerald R. Ford School of Public Policy](https://fordschool.umich.edu/news/2019/robert-axelrod-evolution-cooperation?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

But we need to avoid the popular oversimplification:

> **“Tit for Tat is the best strategy.”**

That is not a universal theorem.

Its performance depended on the ecology of strategies, the payoff structure, repetition, and the possibility of future interaction.

The broader mental-model lesson is better:

> **In repeated relationships, conditional strategies become possible: I can respond tomorrow to how you treated me today.**

That's the important part.

---

# **Reciprocity becomes strategically meaningful**

In a one-shot encounter, I cannot reward tomorrow’s cooperation because there is no tomorrow.

In a repeated relationship, I can condition future behavior on yours.

That creates possibilities such as:

> cooperate with cooperation,

> protect yourself against exploitation,

> restore cooperation after mistakes.

Repeated interaction therefore creates **strategic memory**.

That is one of the simplest ways to explain the theory.

> **What happened before can change what makes sense now.**

---

# **Reputation is compressed history**

This chapter is where reputation gets its full treatment.

A reputation is useful strategically because other people form expectations about future behavior from observed past behavior.

If you have repeatedly:

* kept promises,  
* reciprocated,  
* honored agreements,  
* enforced boundaries,

then the next person may update their expectations accordingly.

Repeated-game reputation theory formally studies how past behavior can influence beliefs and future play. [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/B9780444537669000045?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

But again, real behavior is more complicated than clean reputation models.

Experiments have found broad qualitative reputation effects while also finding substantial departures from exact equilibrium predictions. [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0167268101002165?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

So the correct lesson is:

> **History can affect future strategic expectations; it does not perfectly determine them.**

---

# **Reputation information alone is not magic**

This is a useful counterexample.

A 2022 experiment examined whether letting players observe partners’ past play would increase cooperation in an infinitely repeated Prisoner’s Dilemma under random matching.

It did **not** increase overall cooperation. Some players continued to defect regardless of the additional reputation information, though the kinds of cooperative strategies used did change. [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0165176521004444?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

That's exactly the kind of result we want in the book.

It keeps us from saying:

> “Build reputation and everyone cooperates.”

Instead:

> **Reputation changes the information structure, but its effect depends on the game and the players.**

---

# **A recurring interaction can turn trust into an asset**

Repeated trust experiments offer another useful illustration.

When one player has a chance to trust and the other can either reward or exploit that trust, repetition allows reputation to form. Experimental work finds important features of reputation formation even though individual play often deviates from the exact theoretical equilibrium. [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0167268101002165?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Another repeated trust-game study found that ratings and observable reputation could increase cooperation even beyond purely rational reputation-investment motives. [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1053535709000675?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

So in everyday terms:

> **Your history can become a strategic asset because it changes how much uncertainty other people face when dealing with you.**

That connects beautifully to Chapter 7 on credibility.

---

# **But reputation can also be weaponized or distorted**

This is worth remembering later.

A very recent 2026 experiment found that public reputation systems can themselves be strategically misused: public commitment around review thresholds reduced average effort and payments in the experimental environment. [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0165176526004234?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

That reinforces an important theme across the book:

> **A mechanism that can sustain cooperation can also create new strategic incentives.**

Nothing in game theory should be romanticized.

---

# **Noise makes repeated relationships harder**

This may be one of the strongest practical sections in the chapter.

Real people don't observe one another perfectly.

They:

* misunderstand,  
* mishear,  
* make mistakes,  
* forget,  
* receive incomplete information,  
* misattribute intentions.

That matters enormously.

Aoyagi, Bhaskar, and Fréchette experimentally compared repeated Prisoner’s Dilemma under:

* perfect monitoring,  
* noisy public monitoring,  
* noisy private monitoring.

Subjects sustained cooperation in all conditions, but under imperfect monitoring they adopted **more complex and more lenient strategies**, which helped reduce the damage caused by noise. [American Economic Association](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Fmic.20160304&utm_source=chatgpt.com)

That's a powerful human lesson:

> **When mistakes are possible, rigid retaliation can destroy cooperation.**

In a noisy world, some tolerance and repair can be strategically valuable.

This gives strong evidence for something we had already intuited:

> **Don't automatically treat every apparent defection as intentional betrayal.**

---

# **Why forgiveness can be strategically intelligent**

Imagine:

You cooperate.

I accidentally fail you.

You retaliate.

I interpret your retaliation as intentional.

I retaliate.

Now cooperation collapses from a mistake.

If strategies are completely unforgiving, one error can produce an endless punishment cycle.

The imperfect-monitoring evidence suggests successful strategies under noisy conditions can become more lenient. [American Economic Association](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Fmic.20160304&utm_source=chatgpt.com)

That gives us a careful, evidence-backed claim:

> **In relationships where intentions or actions are observed imperfectly, allowing some path back to cooperation can protect the relationship from cascading errors.**

Notice how different that is from:

> “Always forgive.”

This is strategic repair, not moral absolution.

---

# **Repetition doesn't automatically make people virtuous**

This needs to be explicit.

The Nobel committee itself points out that repeated interaction can sustain **harmful cooperation** too—for example, firms maintaining collusive prices. [Nobel Prize](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/2005/ceremony-speech/?print=1&utm_source=chatgpt.com)

So:

> cooperation ≠ good.

Repeated games simply make some forms of conditional behavior sustainable.

Two firms cooperating to fix prices may benefit each other while harming consumers.

A corrupt group can sustain cooperation internally.

Our ethical framework remains necessary because game theory tells us what behavior can persist, not whether it is morally desirable.

---

# **The horizon matters**

The future only affects today's behavior if the future matters enough.

In repeated-game models, players discount future payoffs. If tomorrow is worth almost nothing compared with today, future punishment or reward has little leverage. MIT’s formal notes build repeated games around a discount factor for exactly this reason. [MIT OpenCourseWare](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/14-126-game-theory-spring-2016/3fc58b6d07a73055a44dcbc5aaacc738_MIT14_126S16_Repeated.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Everyday translation:

> **The shadow of the future is longer when people expect to interact again and care about that future relationship.**

Compare:

A one-time tourist transaction.

A customer you expect to serve for 20 years.

A stranger online.

Your spouse.

A business partner.

Same immediate choice.

Very different future stakes.

---

# **The end-game effect**

This creates another important insight.

Suppose cooperation has been sustained partly because:

> “We'll still need each other tomorrow.”

What happens when both players know:

> **There is no tomorrow?**

In some finitely repeated games, backward-induction logic predicts that cooperation can unravel from the final period backward, depending on the stage game and assumptions. MIT explicitly distinguishes finitely and infinitely repeated games because the horizon changes the strategic structure. [MIT OpenCourseWare](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/14-12-economic-applications-of-game-theory-fall-2025/resources/mit14_12f25_lec12_1080p_mp4/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

We shouldn't claim humans always behave according to that exact unraveling logic.

But the mental model is valuable:

> **When the expected end of a relationship becomes visible, incentives can change.**

An employee leaving next week.

A vendor whose contract won't be renewed.

A company closing.

A final transaction.

The future suddenly casts a shorter shadow.

---

# **One-time behavior can mislead you about long-term character**

This is another subtle insight.

Suppose someone cooperates when they know you'll interact repeatedly.

Does that prove they are intrinsically trustworthy?

Not necessarily.

Maybe future incentives discipline their behavior.

Conversely, a one-shot experiment may understate how cooperative they would be in a real relationship where reputation matters.

That means:

> **Behavior is partly a property of the person and partly a property of the game.**

That's an important theme that cuts across the whole book.

---

# **“Winning the round” versus “winning the relationship”**

This should probably be one of the major everyday sections.

Imagine someone has the opportunity to extract an extra \$1,000 from a long-standing business partner by exploiting a technicality.

Immediate payoff:

> \+\$1,000.

Possible future costs:

* lower trust,  
* tighter contracts,  
* fewer opportunities,  
* retaliation,  
* damaged reputation.

The strategic error is measuring only the current round.

The repeated-game lens asks:

> **What is the value of the continuation game?**

You don't need math to understand that.

---

# **A relationship example**

Imagine a couple arguing.

One partner knows something deeply personal the other shared in confidence.

Using it would instantly end the argument.

Maybe they'd “win.”

But what is the likely continuation payoff?

The other person may learn:

> vulnerability is unsafe.

Future disclosure declines.

The immediate argument was won.

The future communication game deteriorated.

This is exactly the kind of example that makes the model human.

---

# **A leadership example**

Manager says:

> “Tell me bad news early.”

Employee does.

Manager punishes them harshly.

The next time:

> future reporting becomes less attractive.

The manager has altered the repeated game.

The mental question becomes:

> **What is today's response teaching people to expect tomorrow?**

That's a powerful leadership lens.

---

# **Precedent is part of repeated interaction**

Suppose you make an exception.

You may think:

> “It's only this once.”

But if the same players will interact again, the action can become information about:

> what you'll tolerate,

> what pressure works,

> what future requests may succeed.

Repeated interactions develop memory.

So another useful question is:

> **What precedent does this move create?**

Again, not every exception is bad.

The reader simply learns to account for its informational and incentive effects.

---

# **Cooperation can survive among strangers too—but institutions matter**

Repeated interaction does not always require dealing with the exact same person.

Markets and communities can create broader reputation systems.

Research on online markets suggests reputation mechanisms can help mitigate some harmful effects of asymmetric information, though results are context-sensitive. [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0167268122002335?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Historically, Douglass North also emphasized that repeated interaction, information about past performance, and institutional structures can support exchange and cooperation beyond tiny face-to-face groups. [Nobel Prize](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/1993/north/lecture/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

That gives us a broader lesson:

> **Institutions can extend the shadow of the future by making today's behavior visible to tomorrow's partners.**

That's very useful, but we should keep it secondary so the chapter doesn't drift into institutional economics.

---

# **The deepest mental-model question**

This chapter is not ultimately about:

> “Be cooperative.”

It is about:

> **How does the existence of future rounds change the current game?**

Sometimes that means cooperating.

Sometimes enforcing a boundary.

Sometimes punishing exploitation.

Sometimes repairing a mistake.

Sometimes protecting a reputation.

Sometimes ending the relationship.

The model doesn't prescribe the move.

It changes what you include in the calculation.

# **What this chapter should NOT claim**

**Not:** “Repeated interaction guarantees cooperation.”  
It doesn't. [AEA Publications](https://pubs.aeaweb.org/doi/10.1257/jel.20160980?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

**Not:** “Tit for Tat is universally optimal.”  
It isn't; Axelrod's tournament results are context-dependent. [UMich Personal Web Server](https://public.websites.umich.edu/~axe/CC/ECHome/ECCitationClassic.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

**Not:** “Reputation always increases cooperation.”  
Experiments show reputation information alone can fail to do so. [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0165176521004444?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

**Not:** “Forgive every defection.”  
Noise makes leniency useful in some repeated settings, but exploitation is still possible. [American Economic Association](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Fmic.20160304&utm_source=chatgpt.com)

**Not:** “Cooperation is morally good by definition.”  
Repeated games can sustain harmful collusion as well as beneficial cooperation. [Nobel Prize](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/2005/ceremony-speech/?print=1&utm_source=chatgpt.com)

The defensible claim is:

> **When future interaction is likely and valuable, today's actions can affect future incentives, beliefs, reputation, punishment, and cooperation—and therefore the strategic value of today's move changes.**

> **What changes because there is a future?**

## **Chapter 10 Research Dossier — Diagnose the Coordination Problem**

The central claim should be:

> **Not every bad outcome comes from conflicting interests. Sometimes people want compatible outcomes but fail because they cannot align their expectations about what everyone else will do.**

That is a foundational game-theory problem. Schelling devoted major attention to **coordination games**—situations in which people benefit from choosing compatible actions but may have several possible ways to coordinate. The Nobel committee specifically highlighted his discovery that people often solve such problems by using salient **focal points** arising from common frames of reference, conventions, and contextual cues. [Nobel Prize](https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2018/06/popular-economicsciences2005.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

For our reader, the first diagnostic question becomes:

> ## **Are we actually in conflict—or have we simply failed to coordinate?**

That distinction can completely change the solution.

---

# **The simplest coordination game**

Imagine you and I get separated in New York City.

We cannot communicate.

If we independently choose the same place to meet, we find each other.

Otherwise, we don't.

Neither of us prefers Times Square to Grand Central strongly enough to matter.

Our main objective is:

> **Choose what the other person chooses.**

This is fundamentally different from a conflict game.

I don't need to persuade you.

I don't need to defeat you.

I need to predict what **you think I will predict you will choose**.

That recursion is the essence of many coordination problems.

---

# **Schelling's breakthrough: focal points**

Pure coordination games can contain many equally valid equilibria.

Game theory alone may tell us:

> “Coordinate.”

But it may not tell us:

> “Coordinate on which one?”

Schelling found that people often use some option that stands out psychologically or culturally—what became known as a **focal point**.

The Nobel committee gives an extremely simple example. If two people must independently select the same positive integer, many select **1**, even though game theory gives infinitely many matching possibilities. One is distinctive because it is the smallest positive integer. Schelling's participants similarly used context, shared references, and salient features to coordinate without direct communication. [Nobel Prize](https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2018/06/advanced-economicsciences2005.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

That gives us a powerful practical lesson:

> **When several workable solutions exist, the best coordination device may be the one everyone can recognize most easily—not necessarily the one that is objectively “best.”**

That is a distinctly game-theoretic insight.

---

# **Experimental evidence: focal points are real**

Mehta, Starmer, and Sugden directly tested Schelling's focal-point hypothesis in pure coordination games. Their 1994 experimental work examined whether subjects could use salient features to select among multiple equilibria and provided evidence that people do indeed coordinate using focality rather than random choice alone. [University of East Anglia](https://research-portal.uea.ac.uk/en/publications/focal-points-in-pure-coordination-games-an-experimental-investiga/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Later experimental work has strengthened and complicated that finding.

Parravano and Poulsen found that in **symmetric** coordination games, people relied more heavily on a salient label as the monetary stakes increased. But in **asymmetric** games where players had conflicting interests over which equilibrium they preferred, larger stakes did not strengthen the focal-point effect in the same way. [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0899825615000688?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

That boundary matters enormously for our book:

> **Focal points work best when the primary problem is coordination. Once genuine conflicts of interest enter the game, salience may no longer be enough.**

Which means we have to diagnose the game correctly.

---

# **This chapter depends on distinguishing two very different problems**

Suppose a married couple is choosing a restaurant.

### **Situation A**

Both genuinely don't care which restaurant.

One drives to the Italian place.

The other expected sushi.

That's principally a **coordination failure**.

### **Situation B**

One desperately wants Italian.

The other desperately wants sushi.

Now there is a **preference conflict**.

Those problems can look identical at the surface:

> “We disagree about where to eat.”

But they require different solutions.

The first needs:

> shared expectations.

The second may require:

> bargaining, tradeoffs, rotation, compromise, or some other rule for resolving competing preferences.

That distinction is one reason this model deserves its own chapter.

---

# **A particularly useful game: Battle of the Sexes**

The classic **Battle of the Sexes** game makes the distinction clearer.

Both players prefer being together rather than apart.

But each prefers a different joint activity.

So they have:

> **a coordination problem and a conflict of interest at the same time.**

That mixed structure is common in life.

Business partners may both want the company to succeed while preferring different strategies.

Parents may both want consistency for their child while disagreeing over which rule should become standard.

Teams may all want the project completed while each department prefers a workflow favorable to itself.

This helps us avoid another oversimplification:

> **Shared goals do not imply identical payoffs.**

Coordination and conflict can coexist.

---

# **Communication can solve coordination problems**

This is one of our strongest experimental evidence bases.

Cooper, DeJong, Forsythe, and Ross experimentally studied coordination games with nonbinding communication before play.

In games with multiple equilibria, communication often substantially improved coordination. One-way communication increased selection of a Pareto-superior equilibrium in one environment, while in another environment two-way communication drove subjects to the Pareto-dominant equilibrium. But importantly, communication did **not** eliminate every coordination failure in every condition. [EconWeb](https://econweb.ucsd.edu/~jandreon/Econ264/papers/Cooper%20et%20al%20QJE%201992.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

That gives us a nuanced statement:

> **Communication can coordinate expectations even when the words themselves are nonbinding, but simply allowing people to talk does not guarantee successful coordination.**

This connects wonderfully to Chapter 7\.

Cheap talk may be weak as **proof**.

Yet extremely useful as a **coordination device**.

Same words.

Different strategic function.

---

# **This explains why “just talk about it” sometimes works—and sometimes doesn't**

If our interests fundamentally conflict, more communication may clarify the disagreement without solving it.

But if our objectives are compatible and we simply don't know what everyone else plans to do, communication can be transformative.

That gives us a diagnostic rule:

> **Before improving the communication, identify what the communication needs to accomplish.**

Does it need to:

> persuade?

> reveal information?

> establish credibility?

> coordinate expectations?

Those are different strategic jobs.

That is the kind of thinking we want the book to teach.

---

# **The Senegal field experiment gives us powerful real-world evidence**

Aflagah, Bernard, and Viceisza studied **79 groundnut-producing cooperatives in Senegal**, first using lab-in-the-field coordination games with **839 farmers** and then a natural field experiment involving roughly **2,800 cooperative members**.

Members benefited from selling collectively but faced strategic uncertainty:

> Would enough other farmers participate?

The researchers asked farmers how much they intended to sell through the cooperative and then revealed the aggregate intentions of the group.

That relatively simple communication intervention improved collective commercialization. In the field experiment, farmers exposed to information about others' intentions contributed more than twice as much toward collective commercialization as the control group on average; the effect was especially important in larger groups, where coordination was harder. [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0304387821001164?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

This may be one of the strongest real-world cases in the entire book.

The farmers already saw benefits from collective action.

The core obstacle was partly:

> **“Will everybody else do it too?”**

Reveal enough information about mutual intentions and behavior changes.

That's coordination.

---

# **Strategic uncertainty deserves emphasis**

This concept may become a key phrase in the chapter.

Ordinary uncertainty asks:

> “What will happen?”

Strategic uncertainty asks:

> **“What will the other players do?”**

Suppose everyone on a team prefers the new system **if everyone else uses it**.

But switching alone is costly.

Each person thinks:

> “I'll switch when everyone else switches.”

Everybody waits.

Nobody switches.

The problem isn't necessarily resistance to the new system.

It can be uncertainty about everyone else's behavior.

That gives us an important diagnostic:

> **Sometimes people aren't unwilling to cooperate. They're unwilling to cooperate alone.**

The Senegal evidence demonstrates this mechanism particularly well: beliefs about others' participation were central, and revealing intended participation improved collective behavior. [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0304387821001164?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

---

# **Common knowledge goes one level deeper**

This is where Chapter 6's Information & Beliefs material becomes crucial.

There is a difference between:

> **Everyone knows X**

and

> **X is common knowledge.**

Roughly, common knowledge means:

> I know X.

> You know X.

> I know that you know X.

> You know that I know X.

> And so on.

That can sound academic until we see why it matters.

Ariel Rubinstein's famous **Electronic Mail Game** demonstrated theoretically that even arbitrarily many levels of “I know that you know that I know…” can have dramatically different strategic implications from true common knowledge. [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/aea/aecrev/v79y1989i3p385-91.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

We should not bury the reader in epistemic game theory.

But the everyday lesson is extremely useful:

> **It can matter not only that everybody received the information, but that everybody knows everybody else received it.**

---

# **The meeting example makes common knowledge intuitive**

Imagine management privately emails each employee:

> “The office will close at 3 PM.”

Everyone receives the message.

But employees don't know whether everyone else received it.

Compare that with the CEO announcing at an all-hands meeting:

> “Everyone here knows we are closing at 3.”

Now employees can see that everyone else heard the announcement.

The information content is nearly identical.

The **coordination structure** is not.

Public announcements, visible rules, schedules, dashboards, and shared rituals often matter partly because they create mutual expectations.

This is not a claim that every public announcement literally creates perfect common knowledge in the formal mathematical sense.

Rather:

> **Publicly shared information can move a group closer to the mutual awareness needed for coordination.**

That distinction keeps us rigorous.

---

# **Conventions are solutions to repeated coordination problems**

Consider driving.

It isn't inherently superior to drive:

> on the right

rather than:

> on the left.

What matters is that everybody uses the same convention.

Once established, the convention becomes self-reinforcing because deviation is dangerous.

The same logic appears in:

* language,  
* traffic rules,  
* meeting procedures,  
* workplace standards,  
* naming conventions,  
* technology standards.

A convention reduces the need to renegotiate coordination every time.

That gives us another useful principle:

> **Good rules often save people from repeatedly solving the same coordination problem.**

This will bridge beautifully into Chapter 11, **Change the Game**.

---

# **Explicit ownership is a coordination device**

Imagine two employees both agree:

> “The client needs a follow-up.”

Each assumes:

> “The other person will do it.”

Nobody does it.

That's not necessarily laziness.

It's a coordination failure.

Now assign:

> “Paul owns the follow-up by 3 PM.”

The underlying desire to serve the client did not change.

The payoff did not necessarily change.

The information about **who is expected to act** changed.

This is why roles, deadlines, protocols, and responsibility assignments can be so powerful.

They collapse ambiguity.

---

# **Sometimes almost any rule is better than no rule**

Imagine two parents repeatedly fight about who handles school pickup when both are available.

They could spend twenty minutes renegotiating every day.

Or establish:

> Parent A handles Monday/Wednesday.

> Parent B handles Tuesday/Thursday.

The precise rule may not be optimal.

But once both understand and expect it, coordination costs collapse.

That's a deeply Schelling-like insight:

> **The value of a convention can come partly from the fact that it is mutually recognizable, not because it is intrinsically superior.**

---

# **Fairness can itself become a focal point**

This is especially interesting.

In tacit bargaining experiments, Isoni and colleagues found that a payoff-equalizing outcome could become a powerful focal point even when it was economically dominated by other coordinated outcomes. Subjects sometimes used an equal split because it provided an obvious way out of strategic uncertainty between several competing equilibria. [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2214804315001433?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

That gives us a sophisticated interpretation of fairness.

Sometimes:

> **50/50 isn't selected merely because people morally prefer equality.**

It may also be selected because:

> **50/50 is easy for both sides to anticipate.**

Fairness can therefore function simultaneously as:

* a social preference,  
* and a coordination device.

That's a wonderful example of how our mental models overlap without needing another foundational model.

---

# **But salience is not magic**

Recent work reinforces the boundary.

Rojo Arjona, Sitzia, and Zheng experimentally manipulated how salient focal points were. Increasing salience improved coordination in some games with a fixed conflict of interest, but not consistently in games involving different degrees of inequality and efficiency; results also differed depending on whether salience was manipulated between or within subjects. [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0899825622001488?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

That's exactly what we want to tell readers:

> **A focal point can help people select among possible solutions, but it cannot erase every conflict embedded in the payoffs.**

Again:

diagnosis first.

---

# **This chapter gives us a major distinction between disagreement and misalignment**

Suppose a project meeting becomes heated.

Everyone keeps arguing.

Before treating this as conflict, ask:

> Are we disagreeing about the desired outcome?

or:

> Are we agreeing on the outcome but operating from different expectations about roles, timing, standards, or next actions?

Those are different problems.

Consider:

> “I thought you were sending it.”

> “I thought you were sending it.”

Nobody opposes sending it.

The team simply lacked a shared expectation.

Trying to improve persuasion there would be absurd.

Nobody needs persuading.

They need coordination.

---

# **Coordination failure often looks like a motivation problem**

This may be one of the chapter's strongest practical insights.

A manager observes:

> “Nobody is adopting the new process.”

They conclude:

> “Employees are resistant to change.”

Maybe.

But suppose every employee actually prefers the new process **if the rest of the company adopts it**, yet worries that using it alone will make their work incompatible with everyone else's.

That's not primarily:

> lack of motivation.

It's:

> **strategic uncertainty.**

The intervention changes completely.

Instead of:

> motivational speech,

you might need:

> a standard,

> a coordinated launch,

> visible participation,

> clear ownership,

> or a shared deadline.

That distinction is extremely valuable.

---

# **The coordination diagnostic**

I think the reader ultimately needs a compact diagnostic rather than a checklist.

When an interaction is failing, ask:

> **Do we actually want incompatible outcomes?**

If no:

> **What must each of us believe the others will do for the preferred outcome to work?**

Then:

> **What can make that expectation mutually clear?**

Those three questions contain most of the chapter.

---

# **Coordination is not automatically ethical**

Just as with cooperation, coordination itself is morally neutral.

A corrupt group can coordinate.

A cartel can coordinate.

Criminal participants can coordinate.

The Nobel background on game theory repeatedly emphasizes that strategically sustained cooperation can benefit insiders while harming outsiders. [Nobel Prize](https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2018/06/popular-economicsciences2005.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

So we retain our ethical constitution:

> **Truth. Autonomy. Dignity. Informed choice.**

Game theory tells us:

> how coordination works.

It does not tell us:

> what people ought to coordinate around.

---

# **What this chapter should NOT claim**

We should be very disciplined here.

**Not:** “Conflict is usually just misunderstanding.”

Sometimes interests genuinely conflict.

**Not:** “Communication always solves coordination.”

Cooper and colleagues found communication was powerful but not universally sufficient. [OUP Academic](https://academic.oup.com/qje/article-abstract/107/2/739/1838346?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

**Not:** “The most salient option is always best.”

It may simply be easiest to coordinate around.

**Not:** “Everyone knowing something means it is common knowledge.”

Rubinstein's work shows why that distinction matters. [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/aea/aecrev/v79y1989i3p385-91.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

**Not:** “Shared goals guarantee coordinated behavior.”

The Senegal field experiment is almost a direct demonstration of why they do not: farmers could see collective benefits yet still fail to participate because of uncertainty about others. [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0304387821001164?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

The defensible claim is:

> **When players benefit from compatible actions but face uncertainty about what others will choose, communication, salient focal points, shared conventions, and mutually visible expectations can sometimes improve coordination.**

That's strong, precise, and extremely well supported.

# **The teaching game**

I think this chapter should open with a simple exercise.

Tell the reader:

> **You and another reader must independently write down the same positive integer. If you match, you each win \$1,000. You cannot communicate.**

Ask them to choose.

Then reveal Schelling's insight.

Why did so many people gravitate toward certain numbers?

Nobody told them what to choose.

They were trying to answer:

> **“What will the other person think is obvious?”**

That's coordination.

Then we move to something like:

> meeting place → workplace → farmer cooperative → common knowledge.

The conceptual journey becomes very intuitive.

## **Chapter 11 Research Dossier — Change the Game**

This is the culmination of the book.

The central claim should be:

> **When the structure of an interaction repeatedly produces the wrong behavior, the solution may not be a better move inside the game. It may be changing the game itself.**

This is directly connected to **mechanism design**, the branch of game theory associated especially with Leonid Hurwicz, Eric Maskin, and Roger Myerson. The 2007 Nobel Prize recognized them for laying its foundations. Maskin describes mechanism design as essentially the **“engineering” side of economic theory**: instead of starting with an institution and asking what behavior it will produce, start with the outcome you want and ask what rules might produce it. [Nobel Prize](https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2018/06/maskin_lecture.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

That's exactly where our book should end.

Chapter 1 asked:

> **What game am I actually playing?**

Chapter 11 asks:

> **Why am I assuming I have to keep playing this version of it?**

# **From playing the game to designing the game**

Most of the book has taken the strategic environment largely as given.

We identified:

players, payoffs, responses, information, beliefs, commitments, repeated interaction, coordination.

Now we go one level higher.

Suppose you're managing a sales team where representatives hoard leads from one another.

You could hold a meeting about teamwork.

You could improve your communication.

You could appeal to company values.

But then you discover the compensation system rewards individual performance in a way that makes sharing leads personally costly.

Now the problem looks different.

The question becomes:

> **Why are we asking people to behave against the incentives we've created?**

Mechanism design studies precisely this kind of problem: how rules, information and incentives can be arranged so that individually rational behavior produces outcomes the designer is trying to achieve. The Nobel committee emphasizes **incentive compatibility**—roughly, designing a system so that the behavior the system needs is also behavior participants have reason to choose. [Nobel Prize](https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2018/06/advanced-economicsciences2007-2.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

That should be one of the chapter's major ideas:

> **Don't rely on exhortation when the structure rewards the opposite behavior.**

# **Incentive compatibility**

Imagine a manager tells employees:

> “I need complete honesty about mistakes.”

But mistakes hurt performance ratings.

Now consider the employee's strategic problem.

Management wants:

> disclosure.

The system rewards:

> concealment.

Then management becomes frustrated:

> “Why aren't people being transparent?”

Game design asks:

> **What behavior have we actually made rational?**

That's much more useful.

It doesn't assume people are selfish automatons. People can act from conscience, loyalty, professionalism, generosity, or duty.

But if your system consistently penalizes the behavior you say you want, you shouldn't be surprised when that behavior becomes scarce.

# **Strong field evidence: change the incentive, change the behavior**

A particularly strong case comes from Pakistan.

Adnan Khan, Asim Khwaja, and Benjamin Olken designed a system in which **525 property-tax inspectors** were ranked according to performance and then given priority in choosing desirable postings.

This wasn't merely:

> “Work harder and we'll give you a bonus.”

The researchers redesigned the way job assignments were allocated.

Over the two-year field experiment, the mechanism increased annual tax-revenue growth by roughly **30–41%**. Inspectors whom the model predicted would face stronger incentives under the new system improved more. [AEA Publications](https://pubs.aeaweb.org/doi/10.1257/aer.20180277?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

That's an excellent Chapter 11 example.

Same general job.

Same inspectors.

Change an institutional rule.

Behavior changes.

It demonstrates the basic proposition:

> **The rules surrounding a decision can change the strategic attractiveness of the available actions.**

# **Sometimes the rule creates the bad behavior**

Boston's former school-choice system gives us an even cleaner example.

Under the old “Boston mechanism,” parents could benefit from strategically manipulating the order in which they listed schools rather than simply reporting their true preferences.

Researchers analyzing actual choice and assignment records found both sophisticated strategic behavior and families who apparently did not understand how to game the system. Some unassigned students could have obtained one of their listed schools had their families used a different strategy.

Boston eventually replaced the system with a deferred-acceptance mechanism that made truthful ranking much safer strategically for families. [National Bureau of Economic Research](https://www.nber.org/papers/w11965?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Think about how different the two interventions are.

One intervention would be:

> **Teach every parent how to game the existing system better.**

The other is:

> **Design a system that doesn't require parents to game it in the first place.**

That's **Change the Game**.

Alvin Roth describes this exact challenge in discussing school-choice design: a mechanism asking people to reveal preferences should ideally make it safe for them to do so truthfully rather than force them into elaborate strategic guessing. [Nobel Prize](https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2018/06/roth-lecture.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

That may become one of the chapter's best lines:

> **Sometimes the highest form of strategy is removing the need for strategy.**

# **The game itself can create sophistication gaps**

The Boston case reveals another important issue.

A badly designed game can reward people who understand the hidden strategy while punishing people who simply follow the stated instructions.

That creates a difference between:

> **what the rules say to do**

and

> **what sophisticated players learn they actually need to do.**

That is extremely common.

A company says:

> “Focus on the customer.”

Employees learn:

> “Actually, maximize the metric that determines my bonus.”

A school says:

> “Choose the schools you truly prefer.”

Parents learn:

> “Not if the assignment algorithm punishes me for doing that.”

An organization says:

> “Speak openly.”

Employees learn:

> “Not if disagreement damages promotion prospects.”

Those aren't necessarily communication failures.

They can be **mechanism failures**.

# **Change the information structure**

Game design isn't limited to money.

Chapter 6 showed us that information affects decisions.

So another way of changing the game is changing:

> who knows what,

> when they know it,

> and what becomes publicly observable.

Auction design provides a remarkable real-world example.

When the United States began auctioning radio spectrum, Paul Milgrom and Robert Wilson helped develop a simultaneous multiple-round auction in which bidding occurred across multiple rounds, with information from one round becoming available before the next. The structure was designed partly to help bidders discover prices and coordinate across related licenses while reducing weaknesses of alternative auction formats. Variants of these designs were subsequently used for spectrum allocation around the world. [Nobel Prize](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/2020/milgrom/biographical/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

The important lesson for our book isn't auction mechanics.

It's:

> **You can change behavior without changing anybody's personality simply by changing what information becomes available at what point in the game.**

That's a major idea.

# **Change the timing**

Consider two organizational systems.

In System A:

> Employees make decisions privately, then management reviews them months later.

In System B:

> Important decisions require peer review before implementation.

Same employees.

Potentially same incentives.

Different **timing and decision sequence**.

That can produce different behavior because the game tree has changed.

This takes us all the way back to Chapter 1:

> timing was always part of the game.

Chapter 11 says:

> **Timing may also be something you can redesign.**

# **Change who has authority**

Chapter 8 showed us how delegation can create commitment.

Here we see delegation as game design.

Suppose every customer complaint requires approval from a senior executive.

Now employees may avoid making concessions even when a quick resolution would benefit everyone.

Give frontline employees authority within defined parameters and you've changed:

> the available actions,

> the timing,

> the information flow,

> and possibly the payoff structure.

You haven't persuaded employees to make decisions differently.

You've changed the decision environment.

# **Rules can solve repeated coordination problems**

Chapter 10 showed why conventions and common expectations matter.

Game design can institutionalize them.

Instead of repeatedly asking:

> “Who's responsible for this?”

create ownership.

Instead of repeatedly negotiating:

> “Who gets priority?”

create a rule.

Instead of everyone guessing:

> “When should we escalate this?”

create an escalation threshold.

A good rule can turn an uncertain strategic problem into a routine decision.

That's one reason institutions exist.

Myerson describes institutions in mechanism-design terms as systems for communicating information and coordinating actions. [Nobel Prize](https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2018/06/myerson_lecture.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

# **But incentives are dangerous**

This is where the chapter needs an important warning.

Once readers understand:

> “People respond to incentives,”

there is a temptation to think:

> “Then simply reward whatever behavior I want.”

That's much too crude.

Holmström and Milgrom's influential multitask principal-agent work showed why.

If someone performs several valuable activities but you strongly reward the one that is easiest to measure, they may redirect effort toward the measured activity and away from valuable activities that are harder to observe. [Stanford University](https://web.stanford.edu/~milgrom/publishedarticles/Multitask%20Principal%20Agent.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

That's a profound strategic lesson:

> **People don't merely respond to incentives. They respond to the particular behavior the incentive actually rewards.**

Those may not be the same thing.

# **A field experiment shows what this can look like**

Glewwe, Ilias, and Kremer studied teacher incentives in Kenya.

Teachers received rewards tied to students' test performance.

Student scores improved while the program operated.

But researchers found little evidence of improvement in teacher attendance, homework assignment, or pedagogy. Teachers instead increased test-preparation activities, and the score gains did not persist after the program ended. [National Bureau of Economic Research](https://www.nber.org/papers/w9671?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

That's almost a perfect illustration of the problem.

The goal might be:

> **Improve learning.**

The measurable target becomes:

> **Improve test scores.**

Once rewards attach to the target, players optimize the target.

Sometimes the target captures the real objective well.

Sometimes it doesn't.

This is why **Change the Game** must include:

> **What behavior will rational players discover that satisfies the rule without accomplishing the real goal?**

That's a great mental question.

# **The same incentive can work differently in different designs**

We also have evidence warning us against simplistic conclusions such as:

> “Teacher incentives don't work.”

A randomized evaluation in rural India found that performance bonuses produced meaningful improvements in math and language test performance, with evidence suggesting gains extended beyond simple test manipulation. [National Bureau of Economic Research](https://www.nber.org/papers/w15323?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

A Tanzanian experiment comparing two different teacher incentive structures found that **both** improved test scores, but a multiple-threshold design produced greater learning gains at lower cost than a rank-based system. [National Bureau of Economic Research](https://www.nber.org/papers/w25903?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

That's actually a stronger lesson for our book:

> **The question isn't merely whether incentives work. The design of the incentive changes the game.**

Same broad objective.

Different rules.

Different behavior.

Different results.

# **Players will often optimize the rules you give them**

This becomes one of the chapter's deepest warnings.

Imagine a company evaluates customer-service agents primarily on:

> average call duration.

What have you incentivized?

Possibly:

> shorter calls.

But the true objective might be:

> solved customer problems.

Those are not identical.

Or salespeople receive bonuses entirely for:

> new accounts.

Don't be surprised if they devote less attention to:

> customer retention.

Every metric creates a game.

The important question is:

> **What behavior becomes strategically attractive once this metric matters?**

A 2024 revision of a natural field experiment involving **294 car dealerships** found evidence that a loss-framed sales-bonus design generated negative effects partly through increased incentives for gaming behavior. [National Bureau of Economic Research](https://www.nber.org/papers/w26619?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Again, the problem isn't simply:

> incentives are bad.

It's:

> **players adapt strategically to whatever rules you construct.**

# **This brings the whole book together**

Think about what game redesign actually requires.

You need to understand **the game**, because you must know what you're changing.

You need to understand **payoffs**, because rules alter incentives.

You need to **think in responses**, because players will adapt to the new mechanism.

You need to anticipate **predictability**, because players may learn how to exploit the rule.

You need to understand **equilibrium**, because a new rule may create a new stable pattern.

You need **information and beliefs**, because mechanisms determine what people observe and report.

You need **credibility**, because rules that aren't enforced don't change expectations.

You need **commitment**, because durable institutions constrain future actions.

You need **the shadow of the future**, because reputation and future consequences affect compliance.

You need **coordination**, because many rules exist to align expectations.

So Chapter 11 isn't merely another model.

It's where the other ten combine.

> **You cannot intelligently redesign a game you don't understand.**

# **Sometimes changing the game means adding an option**

Imagine two departments constantly fighting over a scarce conference room.

Their recurring options are:

> A gets it

or

> B gets it.

They repeatedly bargain.

Perhaps the intervention is simply:

> add another meeting space,

> allow remote meetings,

> create a reservation system.

You've changed the strategy set.

The conflict may disappear without anybody becoming a better communicator.

This should be an important motif:

> **Before trying to resolve a conflict within the current choices, ask whether another choice can be created.**

# **Sometimes it means changing the players**

Two people may be unable to reach agreement.

A mediator changes the game.

Why?

A third player can alter:

> information flow,

> credibility,

> communication,

> options,

> and expectations.

Similarly:

an arbitrator,

a manager,

a neutral expert,

a new partner,

or ultimately exiting a relationship

can change the set of players.

Again, not every interpersonal problem is solved by saying better words to the same person.

# **Sometimes exit is game redesign**

This may be particularly important for the ethical side of the book.

People sometimes assume strategic skill means:

> “I should figure out how to win this interaction.”

Not always.

Sometimes the highest-value strategic move is:

> **stop playing.**

Leave a destructive partnership.

Decline a bad negotiation.

Stop participating in a recurring conflict.

Change suppliers.

Change jobs.

Chapter 1 introduced outside options.

Chapter 11 closes the circle:

> **Sometimes exercising the outside option changes the game more effectively than optimizing your next move inside it.**

That isn't defeat.

It's strategic choice.

# **But mechanism designers don't control people**

This chapter must not become:

> “Pull the right levers and humans will behave exactly as you want.”

Mechanism design itself recognizes constraints imposed by incentives and private information. Even theoretically elegant mechanisms can have multiple equilibria, implementation problems, or tradeoffs. [Nobel Prize](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/2007/9267-presentationstal-2007-4/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

And actual humans create additional complications:

misunderstanding,

limited strategic sophistication,

fairness concerns,

social preferences,

mistakes,

norms,

gaming,

and adaptation.

So the correct mindset is experimental:

> **Design → observe → test → adapt.**

Not:

> **Design → control.**

# **The deepest distinction in the chapter**

I think we need to make this explicit:

> **Behavior problems are not always people problems.**

Sometimes the person really is behaving badly.

Accountability matters.

But sometimes ordinary people are responding quite predictably to a badly constructed system.

That means the strategic thinker learns to ask both:

> **What should this person do differently?**

and:

> **What does this environment make attractive?**

That second question is surprisingly rare.

# **The ethical dimension**

Mechanism design is powerful precisely because you can shape behavior without issuing direct commands.

That creates obvious ethical responsibilities.

Our constitution applies here more strongly than anywhere else:

> **Truth. Autonomy. Dignity. Informed choice.**

A transparent commission structure that rewards customer retention is game design.

A clearly communicated escalation process is game design.

A fair assignment system is game design.

Secretly constructing an environment to exploit someone's vulnerabilities while concealing material facts is something different.

The goal is not:

> **How can I engineer people?**

It is:

> **How can we construct environments in which good choices are strategically viable?**

That's the ethical formulation I would use.

# **What this chapter must not claim**

Mechanism design does **not** tell us there is always one objectively correct rule. Different goals can conflict, and designs frequently trade off properties such as efficiency, fairness, simplicity, stability, revenue, or strategy-proofness. The school-choice literature itself documents such tradeoffs. [American Economic Association](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Faer.99.5.1954&utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Nor should we claim that structural design eliminates human judgment. A theoretically strategy-proof mechanism can still confuse people or produce mistakes in practice. Empirical work has documented mistaken reporting even under deferred-acceptance mechanisms. [American Economic Association](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Faer.p20171028&utm_source=chatgpt.com)

And we absolutely should not claim:

> **Every undesirable behavior can be fixed by changing incentives.**

Sometimes incentives are not the central problem.

Sometimes values matter.

Sometimes competence matters.

Sometimes information matters.

Sometimes a person is simply unwilling to cooperate.

The mental model tells us to **check the structure**, not assume the structure explains everything.

## **The first change: strengthen See the Game**

### **Alternatives and dependence**

One of the most important questions in strategic interaction is:

> **What happens if we don't reach agreement?**

Bargaining theory gives outside options substantial importance, although their effect is more subtle than the simplistic rule that “a better alternative always means more bargaining power.” In Rubinstein-style bargaining models, an outside option may affect the solution only when it is attractive enough relative to what the player would otherwise obtain. [MIT OpenCourseWare](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-254-game-theory-with-engineering-applications-spring-2010/837a6dbd4edfe215c710dd0cf0649021_MIT6_254S10_lec14.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com) Experimental work likewise finds outside-option effects that are context dependent rather than mechanically monotonic. [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S016517652500343X?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

So I would make this a major subsection:

> ### **Dependence changes the game.**

Ask:

> What can I do without them?

> What can they do without me?

> Who needs agreement more?

> What happens to each of us if nothing happens?

That gives us leverage and power without needing a separate “Power” chapter.

### **Coalitions**

This is the one major game-theory family that was barely visible in our eleven models.

Human beings don't always act as isolated players. They form:

> alliances, blocs, teams, factions, partnerships and coalitions.

Cooperative game theory explicitly studies what groups can accomplish together and how the value created by a coalition might be divided; coalition formation remains an active game-theory research area. [MIT OpenCourseWare](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/14-16-strategy-and-information-spring-2016/934c419edafdd09ac7bb8835e17ff3ee_MIT14_16S16_cooperative.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com) Experiments also show that coalition formation creates dynamics that cannot be reduced simply to three people making independent choices: exclusion, loyalty, bargaining power, and coalition stability all emerge. [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0167268116301408?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

But I still **wouldn't create “See the Coalition” as Chapter 12\.**

Instead, Chapter 1 gets another major question:

> **Who can combine with whom?**

That is enough to expose the reader to the lens without taking the book deep into cooperative-game mathematics.

And it reinforces something we already discovered in our Conversation Autopsy work:

> **The person in front of you may not be the whole game.**

# **The second change: put dominance explicitly inside Think in Responses**

This is a genuine omission in the current dossier.

Dominance and rationalizability are among the first tools taught after representing a game. MIT's current undergraduate sequence goes directly from representation to dominance, rationalizability and Nash equilibrium. [MIT OpenCourseWare](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/14-12-economic-applications-of-game-theory-fall-2025/resources/lecture-notes/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

The human translation is wonderfully simple.

Before asking:

> “What do I think they'll do?”

sometimes ask:

> **“Is there an option I shouldn't choose regardless of what they do?”**

Suppose Strategy A leaves you worse off than Strategy B under every plausible response.

You don't need to predict the other person.

Eliminate A.

That becomes a supporting principle inside Chapter 3:

> **Don't solve uncertainty you don't need to solve.**

First eliminate clearly inferior moves.

Then model responses.

I don't think this deserves a chapter because its applicability is narrower than **Then what?**, but it definitely belongs in the book.

# **The third change: make learning and adaptation an explicit thread through the entire framework**

This may be the most important finding from the audit.

Modern game theory does not only ask:

> “What is the equilibrium?”

It also asks:

> **How do players learn what to do?**

MIT's 2024 graduate course explicitly teaches learning in games, including evolutionary and non-equilibrium learning, and modern researchers such as Fudenberg and Levine have argued that learning models are important for improving game theory's descriptive power. [MIT OpenCourseWare](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/14.126-game-theory-spring-2024/resources/lecture-notes/?utm_source=chatgpt.com) Experimental evidence also shows that behavior can evolve substantially through experience rather than starting at equilibrium. [AEAweb](https://swlb2.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2F0002828043052349&utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Our chapters already contain pieces of this:

**Think in Responses:** learn how this person responds.

**Manage Predictability:** they learn your patterns.

**See the Equilibrium:** repeated adaptation can stabilize behavior.

**Information & Beliefs:** update beliefs.

**Shadow of the Future:** history affects future behavior.

But we haven't named the overarching idea.

I don't think it should become Model 12\.

I think it becomes a **meta-rule of the entire book**:

> ## **Your model of the game is provisional. Update it from play.**

Strategic thinking is therefore not:

> Analyze → choose → done.

It is:

> **Model → act → observe → update → adapt.**

That's enormously important for the eventual AI training system too. A user who can diagnose a game once but cannot update after unexpected behavior has not developed strategic judgment.

So this belongs in the introduction, appears repeatedly throughout the chapters, and becomes central to the conclusion.

# **The fourth change: sharpen the distinction between coordination problems and social dilemmas**

This is subtle but important.

Chapter 10 currently says:

> “Sometimes people want compatible outcomes but fail to coordinate.”

Correct.

But there is a dangerous neighboring case.

Imagine four people would all prefer:

> a clean shared workspace.

Yet each individually prefers:

> somebody else does the cleaning.

At the group level everyone likes the same outcome.

But this isn't merely a coordination problem.

It's a **social dilemma / free-rider problem**: the collectively desirable outcome conflicts with individual incentives.

Public-goods games formalize exactly this structure: contributing fully can be socially beneficial while withholding one's own contribution can be privately attractive. [American Economic Association](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Fjep.11.4.209&utm_source=chatgpt.com)

That gives Chapter 10 a critical diagnostic distinction:

> **Shared desire for the final outcome does not automatically mean you have a coordination problem.**

Ask:

> If everyone else cooperated, would I personally still have an incentive not to?

If yes, we may have a social dilemma.

If no, and the main uncertainty is simply:

> “Which action is everybody else going to take?”

then we're closer to coordination.

This prevents one of the biggest possible conceptual errors in the book.

Social dilemmas themselves do **not** need Chapter 12\. They fit naturally within **See the Equilibrium**, **Shadow of the Future**, and **Change the Game**.

# **The fifth change: explicitly teach the structure of interests in See the Payoffs**

Chapter 2 needs a short but important distinction.

Interactions can be:

**fully opposed**, like many zero-sum games;

**largely aligned**, as in pure coordination;

or, much more commonly in human relationships, **mixed-motive**.

MIT defines zero-sum games as complete conflicts of interest, where one player's gain is the other's loss. [MIT OpenCourseWare](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/14-12-economic-applications-of-game-theory-fall-2025/resources/mit14_12f25_lec07_1080p_mp4/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Most human interaction isn't that simple.

A business partnership can contain:

> shared interest in increasing company value

while simultaneously containing:

> conflict over how that value is divided.

A marriage can contain:

> shared interest in family wellbeing

while simultaneously containing:

> different preferences about money or time.

That should become part of **See the Payoffs**:

> **Are our interests opposed, aligned, or mixed?**

This is more useful than creating a separate “zero-sum vs. positive-sum” mental model.

I also tested whether we have eleven chapters only because we've split similar concepts too finely.

I don't think we have.

| Potential overlap | Why both survive |
| ----- | ----- |
| **Think in Responses vs. Shadow of the Future** | One concerns the next move in a sequence; the other concerns consequences across future rounds and relationships. |

Those boundaries are clean enough for a book.

# **What I tested and still would not promote**

This was important because I specifically looked for reasons to overturn our earlier decisions.

**Bargaining:** unquestionably a major field of game theory. MIT devotes multiple lectures to complete- and incomplete-information bargaining. **Correlated equilibrium:** major formal concept, especially in modern multi-agent learning. [MIT OpenCourseWare](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-s890-topics-in-multiagent-learning-fall-2024/pages/lecture-notes/?utm_source=chatgpt.com) For our reader, the useful portion—outside signals or mediators helping players coordinate—can live under Chapter 10\.

**Evolutionary game theory:** major academic field, but it answers somewhat different questions about which strategies survive and spread across populations. It can enrich examples, especially cooperation, but isn't necessary to our core “how should I see this interaction?” thesis.

**Auctions, voting, social choice, matching, network games:** all legitimate game-theory domains. MIT courses explicitly teach them. [MIT OpenCourseWare](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-254-game-theory-with-engineering-applications-spring-2010/pages/lecture-notes/?utm_source=chatgpt.com) But they are domains of application rather than universal lenses for everyday interaction.

That confirms something important:

> **We shouldn't confuse completeness with including all of game theory.**

We're constructing the smallest useful latticework of game-theoretic lenses for human interaction.

# **Section 1 — What does “game” actually mean?**

We need to immediately eliminate the common misunderstanding.

Game theory isn't primarily about:

* games,  
* competition,  
* winning,  
* manipulation,  
* tricks.

A **game** is simply a situation in which the outcome depends on the choices of multiple decision-makers.

That means a game can be:

* cooperative,  
* competitive,  
* mixed-motive,  
* friendly,  
* adversarial,  
* intimate,  
* professional.

A marriage is a game in this sense.

A business partnership is a game.

A negotiation is a game.

A parent-child interaction can be a game.

A conversation can be a game.

Even deciding whether to apologize can be a game.

The point isn't that life is a game in some cynical sense.

The point is:

> **Other people are decision-makers, and their choices affect your outcomes.**

---

# **Section 2 — Identify the players**

This should be more sophisticated than simply:

> “Who am I talking to?”

The reader needs to learn:

> **Who can actually affect the outcome?**

Sometimes the person in front of you isn't the only relevant player.

Example:

You're negotiating with a salesperson.

But:

* their manager approves discounts,  
* their company has pricing rules,  
* another buyer may be interested,  
* you have another supplier.

Suddenly the game is bigger than:

> You vs. salesperson.

This is where we introduce the idea:

> **The visible interaction may not contain all the players.**

And this is where we'll lightly introduce **coalitions**:

> Who can cooperate with whom?

> Who has aligned interests?

> Who might form an alliance?

No cooperative-game mathematics. Just the mental model.

# **Section 3 — What does each player want?**

This is where Chapter 2 will eventually go deeper.

But Chapter 1 needs to introduce the idea.

Don't assume:

> “They want what I want.”

And don't assume:

> “They want the opposite of what I want.”

Real interactions are often mixed.

You might both want:

> the deal to happen.

But disagree about:

> price.

You might both want:

> the company to succeed.

But disagree about:

> how profits should be divided.

So the reader starts distinguishing:

**aligned interests**

from

**conflicting interests**

from

**mixed motives.**

# **Section 4 — What choices does everyone actually have?**

This is critical.

We naturally focus on the choices **we** have.

Game theory forces us to look at everyone's strategy set.

Ask:

> What can they do?

> What can I do?

> What can neither of us do?

> What choices are constrained?

This is where we introduce a very important idea:

### **Don't confuse desire with available action.**

Someone might want to:

> give you a discount.

But company policy may prevent them.

Someone might want to:

> fire you.

But their contract may prevent it.

Someone might want to:

> leave the relationship.

But financial dependence may make leaving extremely difficult.

The game is shaped not merely by preferences but by **available actions and constraints**.

# **Section 5 — Outside options and dependence**

This should be a significant addition based on our audit.

Ask:

> **What happens if we don't reach agreement?**

That's often one of the most important questions in an interaction.

If you have:

> five alternative suppliers

and they have:

> no alternative customers,

the interaction looks very different than if the positions are reversed.

We don't need to teach BATNA as a negotiation technique.

We're teaching the broader game-theoretic concept:

> **Your alternatives outside the current interaction affect the structure of the current interaction.**

This applies everywhere.

Dating.

Employment.

Business.

Friendship.

Partnerships.

Negotiation.

Even everyday favors.

# **Section 6 — Timing changes the game**

A move isn't just:

> **what**

you do.

It's also:

> **when.**

We should introduce sequential games here.

If I move first and you respond, that's different from us moving simultaneously.

If I can wait and see your move, that's different from having to decide immediately.

If a deadline is approaching, incentives change.

If the relationship will continue tomorrow, that changes things too.

We'll save the deeper repeated-game treatment for Chapter 9\.

But Chapter 1 plants the fundamental idea:

> **Timing is part of the game.**

---

# **Section 7 — The response is part of your move**

This is probably the most important section of Chapter 1 because it connects directly to your original intuition.

Most communication advice asks:

> “What should I say?”

Game theory asks:

> **“What is likely to happen after I say it?”**

Your statement isn't the end of your move.

Their response is part of the strategic consequence.

So:

> What does this make them want to do?

> What options does my move create for them?

> What options does it remove?

> What will they infer from it?

> What are they likely to do next?

This is the seed of our third model:

**Think in Responses.**

# **Section 8 — The game behind the game**

This should be a particularly strong section.

Sometimes the obvious game isn't the real game.

Someone says:

> “I just want the price lowered.”

But perhaps the real issue is:

> They need to feel they got a better deal than their partner.

Someone says:

> “I don't care who gets credit.”

But perhaps status matters enormously.

Someone says:

> “I'm just asking a question.”

But the real objective is to establish a precedent.

The lesson isn't:

> “Assume everyone has hidden motives.”

That's too cynical.

It's:

> **Don't assume the stated interaction fully describes the strategic situation.**

That distinction is important.

---

# **Section 9 — A game is not necessarily a battle**

This should protect the entire framework from becoming Machiavellian.

A game can have:

### **Cooperation**

Both benefit from working together.

### **Competition**

One player's gain may come at another's expense.

### **Mixed motives**

Both have things they want together and things they want differently.

### **Coordination**

They want compatible outcomes but aren't sure what the other will do.

This will become a recurring taxonomy throughout the book.

And it reinforces something you've said repeatedly:

> **Game theory isn't about manipulation.**

It's about understanding interdependence.

# **Section 10 — The “game map”**

I think we should introduce a simple visual framework here.

Before acting, ask:

### **WHO?**

Who are the relevant players?

### **WHAT?**

What does each player want?

### **OPTIONS?**

What can each player actually do?

### **CONSTRAINTS?**

What limits those choices?

### **INFORMATION?**

Who knows what?

### **TIMING?**

Who moves when?

### **ALTERNATIVES?**

What happens if there is no agreement?

### **DEPENDENCIES?**

Who needs whom?

### **NEXT?**

How is each player likely to respond?

This is not a tactical checklist.

It's a **perception tool**.

The reader is learning how to see.

# **The first major exercise**

At the end of Chapter 1, give the reader a situation:

> Your boss sends you a message:

> **“We need to talk about your performance.”**

Then ask them to immediately write down:

* What do I know?  
* What don't I know?  
* What might my boss want?  
* What options do I have?  
* What options do they have?  
* What happens if the conversation goes badly?  
* What alternatives do I have?  
* What might they do after I respond?

The reader will probably realize:

> **I was about to walk into the conversation thinking only about what I was going to say.**

That's the transformation we want.
